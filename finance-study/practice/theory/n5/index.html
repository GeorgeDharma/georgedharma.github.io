Mathematical finance · Theory note

How sovereign debt can rise despite a primary surplus

A debt ratio depends on both what a government owes and the size of the economy that supports it. Interest and growth act on different parts of the ratio.

The model

b(new) = [(1 + i)/(1 + g)] × b(old) − s + a

b is debt divided by nominal output. i is the effective nominal interest rate and g is nominal output growth. s is the primary surplus and a is the stock-flow adjustment, both as shares of current output.

Derivation

1. Start in money units

New debt equals old debt plus interest, minus the primary surplus, plus other debt-changing adjustments. A positive surplus reduces the need to borrow.

2. Scale by output

Current nominal output equals prior output times 1 + g. Dividing the money equation by current output creates the ratio (1 + i)/(1 + g).

3. Identify the surplus

If there are no other adjustments, a stabilizing surplus must offset the change generated by the interest-growth gap on the existing debt stock.

4. Add currency risk

Revaluation of existing foreign-currency debt can enter as a stock-flow adjustment. Recording it again in the primary deficit would double count it.

Worked check

At an initial ratio of 60%, 5% interest, 2% nominal growth and a surplus of 1% of current output, debt rises to 60.76%. The stabilizing primary surplus in this case is about 1.76% of output.

When the result changes

The equation reconciles the debt stock. A refinancing crisis also depends on the maturity calendar, liquid assets, market access and the currency of payment.
