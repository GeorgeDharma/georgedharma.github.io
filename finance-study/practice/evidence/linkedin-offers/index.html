LinkedIn and the competing bids

Compare the $196 Microsoft cash offer with a rival mixed offer whose value moved with the bidder’s shares.

What the record shows

The proxy describes a June 5 proposal from “Party A.” It offered $85 in cash plus a fixed number of the bidder’s shares for each LinkedIn share. Using Party A’s June 3 closing price, LinkedIn valued that package at approximately $200 per share. Microsoft and LinkedIn announced a $196 all-cash agreement on June 13. These are offers at different dates. The June 3 reference price lets us put the two payment formulas through the same sensitivity test.

  • LinkedIn’s definitive proxy statement, “Background of the Merger”
  • Microsoft and LinkedIn, joint announcement filed with the SEC

Illustrative price changes. The $85 cash component, approximate $200 reference value and Microsoft offer come from the historical record.

At the reference price, the shares in Party A’s package are worth $200 − $85 = $115. A 1% change in Party A’s share price moves the per-share package by $1.15. Microsoft’s cash promise has no such share-price exposure between agreement and completion. A claim to future cash can still trade below its face amount when closing takes time or might fail.

Test your reasoning

At what change in Party A’s share price do the packages pay the same on completion? If an analyst claims the $200 headline establishes the best deal for every shareholder, what else belongs in the comparison?

Compare your answer

The shares need to be worth $196 − $85 = $111. Solve 111 / 115 − 1 = −3.48%. Below that threshold the mixed package pays less in this exercise. A decision also needs the two closing probabilities, timing, value on a failed deal and the ability to sell or hedge the shares. A forecast of cash flows from combining the firms answers a separate question about what the acquirer can afford to pay.
