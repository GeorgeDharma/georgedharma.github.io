Silicon Valley Bank and a one-day run

Separate cash that already left from requests still pending, then build a same-day liquidity test.

What the record shows

On March 9, 2023, Silicon Valley Bank experienced withdrawals of more than $40bn. More than $100bn was anticipated or queued for the following day, according to the Federal Reserve. Those are different observations. One records withdrawals and the other describes pending demand. Calling the sum a realized two-day outflow would misstate the evidence.

  • Federal Reserve Office of Inspector General, Material Loss Review of Silicon Valley Bank
  • Federal Reserve, May 2023 speech discussing the March 9 and 10 outflows

The table is a hypothetical funding exercise. Its figures do not describe SVB’s actual collateral or financing.

Liquidity is the ability to make a payment by its deadline. Solvency concerns whether assets cover liabilities at the relevant values. A bank can own securities yet have insufficient cash this afternoon. A haircut of 10% means borrowing is capped at 90% of eligible collateral value. If the same securities have been pledged already, counting a sale of them as a second source of cash double counts them.

Test your reasoning

What is the teaching bank’s shortfall today? If $7bn of the withdrawal requests can verifiably settle tomorrow and no other payments fall due today, does the bank meet today’s cutoff? What conclusion can you reach about solvency?

Compare your answer

Cash by cutoff is 12 + 25 × 0.90 = $34.5bn. Against $40bn due this is a $5.5bn shortfall. If $7bn moves to tomorrow, $33bn falls due today, leaving $1.5bn. The next-day table still needs new expected withdrawals and actual availability of the facility. None of these timing calculations values all assets and liabilities, so they cannot settle the solvency question.
