Lending and Credit Analysis · Case seminar

What loan rate pays for default risk?

One-year bullet loans have a simple model that exposes the difference between expected principal loss and a break-even promised rate. The payoff in default must be specified before the formula has meaning.

How to approach the case

Define the cash flows

Advance €1m today. Default probability is 2%. On default the bank receives €0.40m at maturity and no interest. If the borrower performs, it repays principal plus contractual interest. Funding and operating costs require €1.04m total at maturity.

Expected principal loss

Loss given default is 60% of the €1m exposure. Expected principal loss is 0.02 × 0.60 × €1m = €12,000. It is an average across possible outcomes and cannot describe the largest possible loss.

Price the promise

Using millions of euros, solve 0.98 × (1 + r) + 0.02 × 0.40 = 1.04. This gives r = 1.032/0.98 − 1 = 5.306%. The simple 4% + 1.2% shortcut misses that interest is not collected in default.

A break-even rate changes if recovery occurs later, the borrower can draw more, default probability responds to the rate, the bank requires a return on capital or fees are collected upfront. The calculation holds those features fixed.

Work the case

Sample examination, 30 marks. Derive the break-even rate (10). Reprice at 20% recovery (6). Explain why expected loss and capital address different risks (6). Identify one selection effect and one monitoring response (8).

Show the solution and interpretation

At 20% recovery solve 0.98 × (1 + r) + 0.004 = 1.04, giving 5.714%. Expected loss is a mean. Capital supports resilience to adverse outcomes beyond the mean, subject to a chosen risk standard. Monitoring must target a risk the bank can observe and act upon.
