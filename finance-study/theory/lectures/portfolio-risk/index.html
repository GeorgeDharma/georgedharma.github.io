Why owning a second risky asset can lower risk

A pension fund can hold asset A with expected annual return 8% and volatility 10%, and asset B with expected return 12% and volatility 20%. Volatility is the standard deviation of annual returns. A classical mean–variance investor asks how much expected return is available for each level of portfolio variance. The answer depends on when the two assets disappoint together. A volatile asset can improve the portfolio if its weak periods tend to offset strong periods elsewhere.

Let w be the fraction invested in B and 1 − w the fraction in A. For a single period with fixed initial weights the portfolio return is Rₚ = (1 − w)Rₐ + wRᵦ. Expectations add linearly, so its expected return is (1 − w)μₐ + wμᵦ. To obtain variance, subtract that mean from the return, square the weighted sum and take the expectation. Squaring produces two own-variance terms and one cross term.

Var(Rₚ) = (1 − w)²σₐ² + w²σᵦ² + 2w(1 − w) Cov(Rₐ,Rᵦ)

Covariance describes the joint variation of returns about their means. It has squared-return units. Correlation ρ rescales it by both standard deviations, so covariance = ρσₐσᵦ. Although expected returns are weighted averages, standard deviations usually are not. A percentage displayed as 10% enters these formulas as 0.10.

Calculate a portfolio and a minimum

Take ρ = 0.25 and w = 0.50. Covariance is 0.25 × 0.10 × 0.20 = 0.005. The three terms in the variance expression are 0.0025, 0.0100 and 0.0025. Sum them to get 0.0150. The square root gives 12.25% annual volatility. Expected return is 10.00%. The arithmetic average of the individual volatilities would have given 15%, which would require perfect positive correlation here.

Differentiate the variance with respect to w to find the lowest variance. Expanding the squares gives a quadratic with coefficient σₐ² + σᵦ² − 2Cov on w². Set its derivative to zero and rearrange. The unconstrained weight in B is (σₐ² − Cov)/(σₐ² + σᵦ² − 2Cov). In this example w = (0.010 − 0.005)/(0.010 + 0.040 − 0.010) = 12.5%. Expected return is 8.5% and volatility is about 9.68%. Both assets are individually more volatile than this mix.

If correlation is +1, diversification by combining long positions does not reduce volatility below that of the less volatile asset. If it is −1, a precise mix can eliminate risk in this two-asset, one-period model. In practice correlations and volatilities are estimated. They can move materially in stress, and a portfolio that is safe under one estimate can be unstable under another.

The efficient frontier and the tangency portfolio

For every target return, choose weights summing to one that minimize variance subject to achieving that return. Considering all such portfolios gives a minimum-variance frontier. Its upper portion is efficient because there is no alternative with a higher expected return for the same risk. A portfolio on the lower portion can be dominated. Short-sale limits, trading costs or a borrowing limit change the set of feasible portfolios and can introduce corners where an unconstrained first-order solution is infeasible.

Now allow investment in a risk-free asset at rate rᶠ and borrowing at the same rate. Mixing that asset with a risky portfolio produces a straight line between their points in expected-return/volatility space. The slope is the risky portfolio's expected excess return divided by its volatility, the Sharpe ratio. The highest feasible slope touches the frontier at the tangency portfolio. In the textbook setting different investors can combine this same risky portfolio with different amounts of the risk-free asset.

Derive the market beta relation

CAPM adds an equilibrium argument. Suppose investors have common expectations, use the mean–variance criterion, face the same investment opportunities and risk-free terms, and can trade without material frictions. If they all hold the same risky tangency portfolio, market clearing requires it to be the market portfolio. The risky assets everyone collectively owns must add up to the outstanding assets.

Consider changing an investor's position in one small security while holding the rest of the market. The security's contribution to the variance of the whole portfolio depends on its covariance with the market. Risk peculiar to that security can wash out in a broad portfolio. At a tangency the compensation per unit of marginal covariance must agree across securities, otherwise a small reallocation would improve the portfolio's expected return for its risk.

Write this condition as E[Rᵢ] − rᶠ = λ Cov(Rᵢ,Rₘ). Apply it to the market itself to identify λ = (E[Rₘ] − rᶠ)/Var(Rₘ). Substituting gives the security market line below. Beta is Cov(Rᵢ,Rₘ)/Var(Rₘ). This derivation explains why a firm's standalone volatility is insufficient to calculate its CAPM cost of equity.

E(Ri)−rf=βi(E(RM)−rf)

If rᶠ = 2%, expected market return is 8% and beta is 1.25, the required equity return is 2% + 1.25 × 6% = 9.5%. That is an expected or required return. A realized annual return of −8% can still occur. A genuinely low or negative beta can make an investment valuable as a hedge even if the investment's own payoff is volatile.

What a portfolio manager should challenge

An estimated market beta depends on market proxy, currency, return frequency and sample. Thin trading can distort covariance. Levered equity can have a different beta from the assets of the business because creditors absorb part of the operating claim. The market premium used for a corporate discount rate must also match the risk-free rate's currency and horizon. Give a range when input uncertainty matters.

CAPM is one accounting of priced risk. An arbitrage-pricing or empirical multifactor model allows more than one common factor. Observing a nonzero fitted alpha relative to one benchmark can mean mispricing, omitted risk, costs, bad data or sampling noise. An investor with near-term liabilities may focus on surplus relative to those liabilities. Minimizing asset volatility alone could select an inappropriate portfolio for that mandate.

Independent problem

Try an unfamiliar problem

A portfolio invests 75% in A and 25% in B with the same individual means and volatilities. In a stress case their correlation rises from 0.25 to 0.75. Find the two volatilities and the expected return. Separately, a stock has market covariance 0.018 and market variance 0.020. Give its CAPM required return at a 2% risk-free rate and a 6% market premium.

Compare the complete solution
  1. At the original correlation the variance is 0.75² × 0.01 + 0.25² × 0.04 + 2 × 0.75 × 0.25 × 0.005 = 0.0100, giving 10.00% volatility.
  2. At correlation 0.75 the covariance is 0.015. The third term is 0.005625. Total variance is 0.01375, giving about 11.73%. Expected return is 0.75 × 8% + 0.25 × 12% = 9% under both correlation assumptions.
  3. The separate stock's beta is 0.018/0.020 = 0.9. Required return is 2% + 0.9 × 6% = 7.4%. This uses the covariance with the market rather than the within-portfolio covariance used in the first two parts.
