Modigliani–Miller and the price of leverage

Derive Propositions I and II, reproduce an equity claim, and introduce a corporate tax shield.

The question behind Proposition I

Imagine owning a building that earns an uncertain stream of rent. Selling a senior claim to a lender changes the way the rent is split. Can slicing the same underlying cash into debt and equity make the building itself more valuable? Modigliani and Miller begin with a controlled comparison. Hold the assets, investment policy and the operating payoff in every state fixed. Give investors the ability to trade claims and reproduce the firm's leverage on the same terms. First assume no taxes, distress costs, information advantage or trading frictions.

Under those conditions the value of a levered firm equals the value of an otherwise identical unlevered firm. This is Proposition I. It follows from the law of one price. Two portfolios delivering the same dated cash in every state cannot sustain different prices if investors can buy the cheap payoff and sell the expensive one. A claim that debt is cheaper than equity considers only one side of the financing mix.

Construct homemade leverage

Take an unlevered operation with expected perpetual annual operating cash of €24m, no growth or extra reinvestment, and an asset required return of 10%. The unlevered operating value is €240m. Compare it with the same business carrying permanent €80m debt at 5%, with interest of €4m a year. For this numerical proposition assume debt is safe at the stated debt levels and that investors can borrow at the same rate. In distress the state-by-state replication needs matching debt cash flows and seniority.

To reproduce a 10% stake in levered equity, buy 10% of the unlevered business for €24m and finance €8m by borrowing personally. The net initial outlay is €16m. Each year receive 10% of operating cash and pay €0.4m interest. The expected net receipt is €2m, exactly the expected cash on 10% of the company's equity after its €4m interest bill. More importantly, the operating component moves identically in each state in which the debt terms match. The claim's price must therefore equal €16m, implying total levered equity value of €160m and total firm value of €240m.

If levered equity traded at €180m, a 10% stake would cost €18m. In the ideal market one could sell that claim, spend €16m on the replica and retain the difference while maintaining matching subsequent cash flows. The ability to short, borrow and meet any margin calls matters. If households pay much more to borrow or corporate debt has protections individuals cannot reproduce, the example ceases to be an executable arbitrage.

Derive Proposition II

The expected operating return belongs jointly to debt and equity. Using market values, write rA(D + E) = rD D + rE E. Subtract the debt cash claim, divide by E and collect terms. The remaining equity is a more sensitive claim on the same operating asset as debt rises.

rE=rA+DE(rA−rD)

In this expression rA is the required return on operating assets, rD the return on debt and rE the return on equity. D and E are market values in the same currency and on the same date. With D/E = 80/160 = 0.5, rE = 10% + 0.5 × (10% − 5%) = 12.5%. Equivalently, equity's expected annual cash of €20m divided by its price of €160m gives 12.5%. Recombining the claim returns gives ⅓ × 5% + ⅔ × 12.5% = 10%. This weighted capital cost is unchanged in the no-tax benchmark.

Introduce corporate tax without changing the operating asset

Assume now a 25% corporate profit tax, immediate full use of interest deductions, the same pretax operating cash of €24m and zero depreciation, capex and working-capital changes. The unlevered after-tax cash becomes €18m and its value is €180m at the assumed 10% unlevered rate. Comparing €200m of tax-paying levered value against the earlier €240m tax-free value would mix two different economies.

With €80m permanent debt, interest is €4m and the annual reduction in tax is €1m. If those deductions are as certain as the debt and the debt is permanent, their present value is €1m/5% = €20m. Adjusted present value adds the shield to unlevered operations, giving €200m. Subtract debt of 80 to obtain equity of 120. Equity cash each year is (24 − 4) × 0.75 = 15. Its return is 15/120 = 12.5%. WACC is (120/200) × 12.5% + (80/200) × 5% × (1 − 25%) = 9%. Discounting unlevered after-tax cash of 18 at 9% also gives 200.

For fixed permanent nominal debt in this model Vₗ = Vᵤ + TcD. The tax-adjusted equity return can also be written rE = rU + (1 − Tc)(D/E)(rU − rD). A loan that amortizes, a company unable to use all deductions, or a plan that continuously targets market leverage has a different shield path or risk. In an adjusted-present-value model, forecast the actual interest deductions and discount the resulting cash benefit on terms consistent with that policy. Counting the shield in both cash flows and the WACC increases value twice.

Explain what the benchmark leaves for competing theories

A trade-off model weighs the marginal value of a shield against the marginal expected cost of financial distress and other financing effects. Bankruptcy costs include lost sales, disrupted supplier credit or foregone investments before a court filing. A target borrowing level in this explanation depends on cash-flow volatility, usable tax benefits and the assets' value in distress.

The pecking-order model starts from a different friction. Managers may know more about existing assets than outside investors. If outside investors suspect equity is being sold when it is expensive, issuing shares can depress the price and dilute current holders. Retained funds and relatively information-insensitive debt may then come before external equity. A profitable company with low debt is consistent with accumulated internal funding, even though a simple tax-shield story might predict more borrowing. Distinguish these theories by specifying financing needs and plausible alternatives at the time securities were issued.

Debt overhang provides another channel. A project with positive total value may mostly improve recovery for existing lenders. Shareholders who must provide all the new money can decline it. In that case the operating investment policy itself changes with capital structure, breaking Proposition I’s fixed-asset comparison. A credible interviewer answer starts with the benchmark and then names a friction with a sign and a mechanism.

Independent problem

Try an unfamiliar problem

With no corporate tax the same €240m operation issues €120m safe debt at 5%. Find the remaining equity value and required return. Next suppose tax is 25% and permanent debt is €120m, with full usable deductions and no distress costs. Reconcile APV, equity cash and after-tax WACC.

Compare the complete solution
  1. With no tax, E = 240 − 120 = €120m. Expected equity cash is 24 − 6 = €18m and rE = 15%. WACC is half 15% plus half 5%, or 10%.
  2. With tax, unlevered operation value is €180m. Shield PV is 0.25 × 120 = 30, so the levered value is €210m and equity is €90m. Expected equity cash is (24 − 6) × 0.75 = €13.5m. The equity return is again 15% for these chosen inputs.
  3. After-tax WACC is (90/210) × 15% + (120/210) × 5% × 0.75 = 8.5714%. Dividing €18m by this rate gives €210m. If the borrower cannot use tax deductions right away, forecast the timing and size of actual benefits before applying this conclusion.
