Why a bank turns down a higher rate

Derive Stiglitz–Weiss credit rationing from borrower choices and the lender’s payoff.

The apparent puzzle

A bank has funds for 60 identical-sized business loans and 100 applications. Some rejected applicants offer a higher interest rate. In a market for a fixed product, a higher price often makes the sale more attractive to the supplier. A loan is an exchange of money today for a contingent future payment. Raising its price can change who applies and what the borrower does with the money. The bank's expected receipts need not rise with the rate printed on the contract.

Stiglitz and Weiss show conditions under which rationing can persist in equilibrium with imperfect information. Their precise question concerns borrowers who look alike to the lender and would accept the terms, while some receive credit and others do not. A loan refused because the borrower plainly cannot pay is a different observation. To establish rationing, ask what would happen if the lender tried to clear excess demand through a higher price.

Start with a borrower's choice

Consider one year and €100 of principal. The contract promises D = 100 × (1 + r) at the end. A project produces X euros. The lender receives the smaller of X and D. The borrower keeps the larger of X − D and zero. These payoffs sum to X in each state. Assume limited liability, no other collateral, no enforcement cost, risk-neutral borrowers and no ability for the lender to distinguish the two project types. Each applicant compares expected surplus with a real outside opportunity.

There are 80 safe applicants whose projects pay €122 for certain and whose outside option is €7. There are 20 risky applicants. Each risky project pays €200 with probability 0.60 or €5 with probability 0.40. Its expected output is also €122. The risky applicant has an outside opportunity worth €36. These assumptions are chosen to show the selection mechanism. They are not estimates for a real loan portfolio.

At a 10% rate the safe borrower keeps 122 − 110 = €12, more than the €7 outside option. The risky borrower keeps €90 in the good state and zero in the bad, so its expected surplus is 0.60 × 90 = €54. Both apply. The bank receives €110 on a safe loan and 0.60 × 110 + 0.40 × 5 = €68 on a risky loan. Risk affects the lender even though the projects create the same expected total output.

Derive the participation cutoffs

For the safe borrower the threshold solves 122 − D ≥ 7. It applies for D ≤ 115, or r ≤ 15%. For the risky borrower the threshold solves 0.60 × (200 − D) ≥ 36. It applies for D ≤ 140, or r ≤ 40%, over the relevant range of repayments. Assume an indifferent applicant agrees to borrow. The proportion of safe borrowers therefore drops sharply just above 15%. A population with a range of outside options could produce a smoother decline.

L=pD+(1−p)R

Here L is expected lender receipts on one loan, p is the probability of full repayment, D is the promised amount and R is the payment in the default state. The displayed expression applies to a group with these two repayment outcomes. For the whole pool, average the group-specific receipts using the mix of borrowers who still choose to apply. Fixing yesterday's mix while repricing today would remove the very channel the theory studies.

From 0% through 15%, the pool stays constant and expected receipts rise. They reach €106.20 at 15%. Just above the cutoff, all safe applicants leave. For higher rates up to 40%, receipts on the remaining applicants rise again but never exceed €86. Above 40% neither type applies. The €106.20 peak is therefore a global maximum in this teaching economy. Against a €103 funding obligation per €100 advanced, its expected margin is €3.20 before any other costs the example omits.

Suppose funds and capital permit exactly 60 loans. At the bank's preferred rate 100 applicants are willing to borrow. Funding 60 gives an expected total margin of €192 if selection among observationally identical applications is random. There is excess demand for 40 loans. Pricing at 16% could reduce applications below 60, yet the expected economics deteriorate sharply. A competitive market model also needs a funding supply or a required return to determine aggregate lending. This classroom calculation holds that funding capacity fixed to expose the selection effect.

Distinguish adverse selection from moral hazard

In the numbers above, each applicant's project already exists before the quote. The rate changes the composition of people who choose the contract. That is adverse selection. A moral-hazard model instead gives a borrower a choice after financing. Because the borrower receives the upside above promised debt and can leave part of the downside with creditors, increasing promised debt can favour a riskier project.

For a separate illustration let an already financed firm choose between a certain €120 and a project paying €190 or €20 with equal chances. At debt due of €80, the safe equity payoff is €40 while risky equity has expected payoff €55. The risky project has expected output €105, below €120. Equity can prefer the lower-value project because lenders absorb a larger share of its downside. Lower debt due to €30 and safe equity earns €90 versus €80 for risky equity. Here the same firm changes its choice, even if the applicant pool never changes.

A lender can try collateral, covenants, information gathering or monitoring. Each has a cost and a mechanism. Collateral can reduce loss in default and alter which constrained firms can apply. Covenants can restrict a project switch but require measurable triggers and enforcement. Monitoring can reduce uncertainty but only when relevant information arrives before corrective action is too late.

Independent problem

Try an unfamiliar problem

At the 15% quote a second region contains 60 safe and 40 risky applicants with the same project and outside-option assumptions. Funding still costs €103 per €100 advanced. Calculate expected receipts and margin. Suppose screening identifies safe firms perfectly at €4 per completed loan. Would lending only to the identified safe firms cover funding and screening?

Compare the complete solution
  1. With the new mix expected receipts are 0.60 × €115 + 0.40 × €71 = €97.40. After funding of €103, the expected margin is −€5.60 per €100 advanced.
  2. A screened safe loan pays €115. Subtract €103 funding and €4 screening to leave €8. Performance depends on screening cost being per completed loan and on perfect identification. A fee charged on all rejected applications would need to be included.
  3. Observe that every risky borrower's contract is unchanged. The change in profitability comes from population composition. Explain why historical approval data alone may not describe all applicants after the policy changes.

Give the theory a fair empirical test

A fall in approvals after a rate rise has other possible explanations. Funding may have become expensive, the local economy may have weakened or the underwriting standard may have changed. Ask for applications, offers, acceptances and later defaults, not just the book of approved loans. Compare borrowers or branches exposed to plausibly comparable changes, and examine who declined the more expensive offer. A useful interview answer describes the lender's contingent claim, gives at least one mechanism by name, and explains why it can overturn price clearing.
