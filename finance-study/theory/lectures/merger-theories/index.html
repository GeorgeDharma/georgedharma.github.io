Why companies merge, and who keeps the gains

Takeover free riders, operating synergy, market power, agency, hubris and the highest defensible bid.

Start with two separate decisions

A warehouse operator can buy a smaller rival for €92 million. The seller calls it a great merger because it will receive €12 million more than the €80 million value of its business today. The buyer points to duplicate depots it could close. The board still has two separate decisions to make. Would putting the businesses together create resources or cash that neither could obtain separately? If so, how much of that gain will the seller take through the purchase price?

Set a valuation date and use enterprise values throughout, or equity values throughout. In this lecture both firms have no debt or excess cash. Their operating and equity values coincide. A practical model with debt needs an explicit enterprise-to-equity bridge. Compare each projected cash flow against the most reasonable path without this particular deal. Giving the buyer credit for a depot already scheduled to close overstates the gain.

Control and synergy have different counterfactuals

Suppose the buyer is worth €120m independently. The target is worth €80m under current policies. Replacing poor working-capital management could release €8m in present value under any competent controller. That makes €88m the target's improved standalone value. A buyer who can also combine delivery routes obtains a further gain. This last change requires the two networks to operate together. A rival with an identical network might achieve it too.

S=VAB−VA−VT*

In this display V with subscript AB is the value of the combination after improvements and implementation costs. V with subscript A is the buyer's independent value. V with subscript T* is the target's improved standalone value. S is the incremental value requiring combination. Keeping the star in the calculation prevents an €8m control improvement from reappearing in the synergy column.

The reservation price is the highest price at which the buyer breaks even on the assumptions. For a cash bid with no additional financing effects, transaction costs or contingent payments, it equals the improved target value plus net synergy. The price actually struck depends on alternative bids, the seller's willingness to remain independent and which party can credibly leave the negotiation. Model advisory fees and tax effects separately if they are present.

Translate an operating claim into cash

The operating team predicts €4m in annual cost savings at each of four year ends. Headcount, volumes and service quality are held fixed except for the identified change. Cash taxes on the saving are 25%, the risk-adjusted discount rate is 10%, and closing the depots costs €2m in cash today. That initial cost is already stated after tax. There is no saving after year four. A run-rate saving measured once integration is complete would need a ramp-up schedule if year one captures only a fraction.

At an offer of €92m the buyer keeps €95.510m − €92m = €3.510m. The target's shareholders keep €12m. Their gains add to €15.510m, an independent check on the calculation.

Six theories give different predictions

ExplanationMechanismEvidence that distinguishes it
Operating efficiencyServing the same customers requires fewer inputs or better matching of assets.Measure labour, fuel or warehouse space per comparable unit. Check quality, mix and changes in input prices.
Market powerCustomers have fewer outside options, allowing higher prices or tighter contract terms.Compare overlapping routes with unaffected routes, adjusting for costs and demand. A gain to the firms can come at customers’ expense.
Market for corporate controlA new controller can change inefficient policies at the target.Build a standalone improvement plan. Ask whether an unrelated owner could implement it without combining operations.
Jensen’s free-cash-flow agency explanationA manager with cash beyond attractive investments may expand the firm to gain size, influence or compensation.Examine incentives, payout alternatives, board challenge and willingness to exceed the buyer's own valuation ceiling.
Roll’s hubris hypothesisA manager overestimates the precision or level of their valuation and knowingly pursues what they believe is a good investment.Look for unadjusted optimistic estimates, repeated overbidding and weak calibration against prior forecasts. An agency conflict is unnecessary for this mechanism.
Grossman–Hart free-rider problemA small shareholder can retain the value improvement if other shareholders tender and the takeover succeeds.Check the tender terms, treatment of remaining shareholders, the bidder's existing stake and whether a holder can affect success.

Several explanations can fit one transaction. Rising margins fit efficiency and market power. A stronger test decomposes margins into quantity, selling price, physical inputs per unit and input costs. A bidder share-price fall fits overpayment, adverse information about the bidder or costly financing. It cannot, by itself, diagnose hubris.

Why a socially valuable takeover can fail

Take a separate firm with many small shareholders. It is worth €10 per share as run. Under a new controller each share would be worth €13. The bidder must spend €0.50 per acquired share on the transaction. Assume holders are individually too small to affect whether the bid succeeds, and a holder who stays will share in the improved €13 value. A conditional offer of €12 attracts a comparison between tendering for €12 and holding a €13 improved share in the successful state.

If other holders think the same way, getting enough shares can be difficult. Paying €13 removes the gap for holders, but leaves the bidder the €0.50 transaction cost. Value creation at the firm level therefore need not generate a profitable path to acquiring control. An existing stake can allow the bidder to capture gains on shares it owns already. Transaction structure and legal rules for minority holders matter. The comparison changes if a shareholder is pivotal or the bid has different failure payoffs.

What a deal team puts in front of the board

A usable bid paper gives the dated value bridge, the evidence and owner for every synergy line, the after-tax cost to deliver it, a downside case and the maximum acceptable price. It also shows how the consideration changes who bears uncertainty. In an all-cash deal the target receives the agreed cash at closing. In a fixed exchange-ratio share deal the target receives a specified number of buyer shares. Their closing value moves with the buyer's share price, and the seller retains an interest in the combined firm.

To assess a bid described as earnings-accretive, calculate the change in earnings per share separately from incremental value. Borrowing cheaply can raise earnings per share in the first year even if the present value of the target plus improvements is below the price. Share price responses at announcement measure revisions to investors' expectations. If the market had anticipated the bid, even a sound deal can produce a small announcement response.

Independent problem

Try an unfamiliar problem

Another buyer offers €98m. It forecasts exactly the same control improvement and the same four savings, but integration costs €3m today and savings start at the end of year two. Calculate the maximum cash price and the value gap. What new finding could justify matching the offer?

Compare the complete solution
  1. First move the original €9.510m gross present value of savings back by a further year. Its revised value is 9.510/1.10 ≈ €8.645m. Deduct €3m today to obtain net synergy of €5.645m.
  2. The maximum cash price is 80 + 8 + 5.645 = €93.645m. At €98m the buyer loses about €4.355m on the stated economics. The target's gain is €18m.
  3. Matching requires additional verifiable present value of at least €4.355m, a different opportunity cost, or enforceable terms that lower the price paid in weak outcomes. Repeating the headline €4m savings would count an existing assumption twice.

Defend the answer to an interviewer

Start with the transaction the buyer could decline. Explain which improvements any controller could make, which cash flows require combination, when implementation cash leaves, and who receives the resulting surplus. If the interviewer challenges the rationale, name a rival theory and a piece of evidence that could distinguish it. This explains both the valuation and why a board might still choose the wrong deal.
