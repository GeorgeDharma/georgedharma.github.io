Build a five-year leveraged buyout

Reconcile sources and uses, sweep cash into debt, and split operating gains from the sponsor’s return.

What the sponsor is trying to earn

A leveraged buyout uses debt and a sponsor's equity to acquire a company. The company’s future operating cash can repay some debt. If its operating value holds up, reducing senior debt increases the residual available to equity holders at exit. Leverage amplifies the percentage return on a smaller equity contribution and increases the risk of missing a fixed payment. A buyout model must show who funds every use at entry, how the borrower survives each year, and who receives the cash when it is sold.

Keep operating value and equity value separate. Entry and exit enterprise values are calculated from operating metrics and multiples. To obtain equity proceeds, subtract financial claims and add surplus cash on the same date. In this example there is no existing target debt or surplus cash, so the enterprise purchase price equals the amount paid to sellers. An actual deal often refinances old debt and must reconcile minimum cash, fees and a cash-free/debt-free price agreement.

Reconcile sources and uses at entry

The target earns normalised EBITDA of €20m. An entry EV/EBITDA multiple of 8.0 gives operating value of €160m. There are €8m of fees paid at closing. New acquisition debt provides €100m. The sponsor contributes the residual €68m so sources equal uses of €168m. The fee reduces the sponsor's economic return even when it is absent from the advertised headline purchase multiple.

Income-statement EBITDA is measured before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization. Cash to service debt also depends on taxes, maintenance investment and working-capital growth. The cash numbers below are already after those items and before cash interest. We hold the specified tax cash flow fixed as leverage changes, so this simplified model excludes an additional interest tax shield. Calculate taxes explicitly in a full tax-sensitive model.

Build the debt schedule row by row

Assume one debt tranche, no fees beyond the entry uses, year-end principal repayments, a 7% cash coupon on opening debt and a full cash sweep. The borrower maintains no minimum cash reserve in this exercise. A real minimum operating balance must be funded before the sweep. EBITDA growth implies no cash until a separate operating forecast supports it. The given cash available before interest is 15, 18, 21, 24 and 27 for years one through five.

Year one interest is 100 × 7% = 7, leaving 15 − 7 = 8 to repay. Year two opens at 92, so interest falls to 6.44 and repayment rises to 11.56. The mechanism compounds over time because repayments reduce future interest. In each year, repayment cannot exceed the outstanding debt. If debt is fully extinguished, retain excess cash for exit or model an explicit distribution. Intermediate distributions would change both timing and the IRR calculation.

At a minimum, recalculate opening less repayments plus new drawings equals closing for every debt tranche. Match the cash-flow statement’s financing movements to that schedule and bring forward any unpaid interest or covenant shortfall explicitly. Hiding a negative cash balance by assuming an undrawn revolver always remains accessible can turn an insolvent forecast into an apparently excellent return.

Bridge the exit and measure returns

Suppose year-five EBITDA is €28m and the exit multiple remains 8.0. Exit operating value is €224m. Subtract remaining debt of roughly €21.8195m to obtain equity proceeds near €202.18m. No accumulated surplus cash is present in this base case. The gross sponsor multiple on invested capital is 202.18/68 = 2.973×. Profit to equity is about €134.18m. This reconciliation is a useful cross-check: €64m operating value growth + €78.18m debt reduction − €8m entry fees = €134.18m.

IRR=(exit equityentry equity)1n−1

With no intermediate sponsor distributions, exit equity divided by entry equity is the wealth multiple. Raise it to 1/n, where n is years held, and subtract one. Here n = 5 and gross IRR is approximately 24.35%. A full cash-flow series with dividends, additional capital calls or irregular dates requires solving for the rate that makes the discounted investor cash flows sum to zero. Fund management fees, carried interest and the investor's own timing move limited-partner net returns away from this deal-level gross figure.

What moves a buyout result

If the exit multiple contracts from 8× to 6× without changing operating cash or debt, exit enterprise value falls to €168m. Equity falls to approximately €146.18m, or 2.150× entry equity, and the five-year IRR falls to about 16.54%. The company still delivered EBITDA growth and debt reduction. Roughly €56m of enterprise value disappears because exit investors pay two fewer turns on €28m EBITDA.

Higher entry debt can lower initial sponsor equity and boost the base-case return, yet a borrower facing a weak first-year cash flow may have too little headroom to make interest. Check coverage and minimum cash at the low point, covenant definitions, mandatory amortization, interest reset dates and the order of repayments across tranches. An investment committee typically asks about exit multiple, cash conversion and covenant downside separately, then asks whether adverse changes could arrive simultaneously.

A clean investment memo names the sources of return. Show the gain from operating performance at a constant multiple, the gain or loss from changes in valuation multiple, the debt and cash bridge, and fees. The breakdown is sensitive to how the operating/multiple interaction is assigned. State the order or use a consistently defined symmetric attribution. A lender reading the same model needs repayment capacity and collateral coverage, even when the sponsor's IRR is high.

Independent problem

Try an unfamiliar problem

A downside sale after five years produces EBITDA of €24m at 7× and €35m debt with no surplus cash. Assume the same €68m entry equity, no intermediate distributions and no exit fees. Calculate the exit equity, multiple and IRR. What exit multiple would be needed to double the sponsor’s money?

Compare the complete solution
  1. Enterprise value is 24 × 7 = €168m. After debt of €35m, equity receives €133m. MOIC is 133/68 ≈ 1.956×, and five-year IRR is (133/68)^(1/5) − 1 ≈ 14.36%.
  2. To double entry equity, the sponsor needs €136m at exit. Add back €35m debt to get a required enterprise value of €171m. Dividing by €24m EBITDA gives 7.125×.
  3. Check whether the downside debt balance actually follows from the operating and interest schedule. A model that simply types in closing debt cannot tell a lender whether the company survives the earlier years.
