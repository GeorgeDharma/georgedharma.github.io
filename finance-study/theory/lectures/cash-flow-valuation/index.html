Build a DCF and defend its terminal value

Turn accounting profit into free cash flow, fund the growth forecast, and arrive at a value per share.

Define the claim and the valuation date

An acquirer asks, “What is the business worth per share?” The income statement gives profit, the balance sheet gives book equity, and competitors trade at different multiples. None of these objects is automatically the present value of the shares. Start by defining what you will price. This example values the cash generated by the operating assets for all capital providers. Only after that calculation will we allocate the operating value between lenders, other claims and ordinary shareholders.

Fix the end of this year as date zero. Treat forecast cash flows as occurring at year ends, use nominal euro amounts, and quote required returns per year. A midyear convention, a foreign-currency forecast or an inflation-adjusted business plan changes the timing or rate to apply. A clean model carries its date, currency and unit scale at the top of every schedule.

Turn accounting earnings into distributable operating cash

NOPAT is operating profit after taxes attributable to operations. To turn it into free cash flow to the firm, add back operating noncash charges such as depreciation and deduct capital expenditure and the increase in operating working capital. A sale on credit can increase operating profit before cash arrives. A production line can consume cash today while only one year's depreciation passes through profit.

FCFF = EBIT × (1 − operating tax rate) + depreciation − capital expenditure − increase in operating working capital

For the synthetic company, year-one operating profit before tax is €40m. At 25% tax this gives €30m NOPAT. Depreciation is €5m, capital expenditure is €9m and operating working capital rises €2m. Net reinvestment is 9 − 5 + 2 = €6m, so FCFF is €24m. Interest is omitted because the cash is defined before distribution to capital providers. In a more complex group, compute actual operating cash taxes and normalize items that cannot recur.

Construct a discount rate consistent with those cash flows

Suppose comparable operating risk and the target funding policy justify a 12% cost of equity and 6% pretax cost of debt. Use 60% equity and 40% debt by market value. If the company can use the 25% interest deduction, its after-tax debt cost is 4.5%. WACC is 0.60 × 12% + 0.40 × 4.5% = 9%. The operating cash flows use the same currency, nominal basis and risk. A different project or unstable debt policy may call for a different rate or an explicit financing valuation.

Discount the three FCFFs by 1.09, 1.09² and 1.09³. Their present values are €22.02m, €21.04m and €20.08m. Together they contribute €63.14m before rounding. The reference date of the year-three cash flow is the end of year three. A terminal value measured at that same date must also be discounted three years. Adding a future terminal value to today's discounted cash is a timing error.

Pay for perpetual growth

Let the mature business grow operating profit at 3% per year after year three. Its return on new invested capital is 12%. Growing NOPAT sustainably requires capital. If next year's profit increment equals last year's incremental capital multiplied by its return, then g = reinvestment fraction × return on new capital. The mature company must reinvest 3%/12% = 25% of NOPAT. A return on historical assets can differ from the return on the next investment. The latter drives the growth assumption.

TVN=NOPATN+1(1−gROIC)WACC−g

Here N is the last explicit forecast year. NOPAT in N+1 is the first mature-period operating profit. ROIC is the return on new invested capital, and WACC is the cost of capital for the operating cash. The formula requires a stable operating policy and WACC above g. It arises by summing a stream of growing future free cash flows. Multiplying numerator and denominator of the infinite discounted series by its common growth-and-discount factor reveals the usual first-cash-flow divided by WACC minus growth result.

Year-four NOPAT is €34m × 1.03 = €35.02m. Deduct reinvestment of 25% × 35.02 = €8.755m to obtain €26.265m sustainable FCFF. Terminal value is €26.265m/(0.09 − 0.03) = €437.75m at the end of year three. Its present value is about €338.02m. The operating enterprise value is €401.16m. About 84% comes from terminal value, so the terminal economics deserve more scrutiny than the last decimal of the year-one forecast.

Bridge operating value to one ordinary share

Add €14m of genuinely surplus cash outside operations. Deduct €110m of debt and €6m of other senior or minority claims whose associated cash was included in the operating valuation. The resulting ordinary equity value is €299.16m. With 10 million fully diluted shares under the stated convention, the value per share is €29.92. In a real bridge, match leases, pensions, investments, options and non-controlling interests to the treatment used in operating cash flows. Counting both an option's claim and the full dilution from those same options could double count it.

A useful check works backward. A share price of €35 implies equity of €350m and operating value of 350 + 110 + 6 − 14 = €452m. Holding the explicit forecast unchanged, an analyst can investigate which combination of mature margins, growth, reinvestment and risk would support the extra €50.84m. A single market price cannot identify several unknown assumptions. Fix or bracket the others before calling one input “market implied.”

Stress what the business must achieve

Increasing g while freezing the reinvestment rate would implicitly improve the return on new capital. Holding ROIC constant instead forces FCFF and growth to move together. When the return earned on marginal capital equals its cost, additional mature growth can largely reinvest value without generating excess value. When ROIC is below the cost of capital, expansion can be destructive. Compare growth assumptions at the same starting profit date to isolate this effect.

Independent problem

Try an unfamiliar problem

For the same company keep WACC at 9% and the three explicit FCFFs unchanged. Mature NOPAT in year four is fixed at €35.02m. From then on ROIC is only 9% with 3% perpetual growth. Recalculate the terminal value, ordinary equity value and price per share. Which forecast line explained the change?

Compare the complete solution
  1. The reinvestment share is 3%/9% = one third. First stable FCFF is €35.02m × two thirds = €23.3467m. End-year-three terminal value is 23.3467/0.06 ≈ €389.11m.
  2. Discount by 1.09³ and add the €63.14m of explicit cash. Operating value is about €363.60m. Add 14 and subtract 110 and 6 to reach about €261.60m of equity, or €26.16 per share. Small differences can arise from intermediate rounding.
  3. The company still forecasts the same first mature NOPAT and growth, but now needs more capital for each euro of growth. Increased reinvestment reduces distributable cash and the terminal claim. Simply changing a market multiple would conceal that economic mechanism.
