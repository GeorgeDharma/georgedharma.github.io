What an announcement return can tell you

Estimate a counterfactual return, calculate CAR, and defend the benchmark and inference.

Define the event before looking at the chart

A central bank makes a surprise announcement and a listed bank's shares fall. The analyst must decide what the bank would probably have earned over the same days without the news. It is possible to fall 1% on a day when comparable stocks fall 3%, or to rise 1% while an ordinary market exposure would have earned 2%. An event study estimates abnormal performance relative to an explicit model of that counterfactual.

Write an event register with announcement date, time and time zone, market open and close, first trade that could reflect the news, overlapping announcements and the rule used to assign day zero. For a release after a Friday close, Monday can be the first trading session able to respond. Another market may still be open on Friday. Using identical calendar dates across markets can inadvertently compare information sets from different times.

Choose the window and estimate normal returns

A short event window such as trading days −1 to +1 allows for leakage and modest uncertainty about timing. Wider windows also collect unrelated news. Choose the main window before looking at the result. Use an earlier estimation window to fit the normal-return model, leave a gap if advance leakage is plausible, and keep the event window out of the estimation sample. Distinguish prices from returns, use consistent dividend and split conventions, and decide whether every return is measured in local currency or a common investor currency.

A market model writes the bank return as Rᵢₜ = αᵢ + βᵢ Rₘₜ + εᵢₜ. Alpha is the model's intercept per period. Beta is the bank's sensitivity to the chosen market return. The error represents movement unexplained by this model. Estimate alpha and beta from pre-event pairs, usually by ordinary least squares. The slope is the sample covariance of bank and benchmark divided by the sample variance of the benchmark. The intercept is the bank's sample mean less beta times the market's sample mean.

ARit=Rit−(α̂i+β̂iRmt)

A hat over a parameter means it was estimated. The fitted expected return on day t is the estimated intercept plus estimated beta times that day's market return. Subtract it from the bank's observed return to obtain AR in percentage points. The cumulative abnormal return, CAR, is the sum of daily abnormal returns across the selected dates. A buy-and-hold abnormal return instead compares compounded wealth paths. It can give a different result and requires its own definition and inference.

Work a three-day bank example

In this original example the pre-event fit gives daily α = +0.10% and β = 1.10. Both series use synchronized closing times and the same currency. Treat the fitted coefficients as given for the arithmetic. Day-zero and adjacent returns are invented.

The bank's observed arithmetic sum is +1.40%. Its three-day local CAR is +0.40 − 0.70 − 0.80 = −1.10 percentage points. A positive observed gain can be disappointing after accounting for the broader rally. This result is conditional on an estimated normal relationship. It is not by itself a loss of operating profit, a realized cash flow, or proof that the announcement caused exactly −1.10%.

Change the benchmark and see the question change

Suppose a second model was estimated in advance against a regional index. In that fit α = 0 and β = 1.00. Regional returns over the same three synchronized dates are −0.20%, +0.40% and 0.00%. Daily abnormal returns now equal −0.40, +1.20 and +0.40 percentage points. Regional CAR is +1.20 points. The observed bank data did not move. The benchmark removed a different common component.

A domestic index may capture common policy exposure shared by local companies. It can also contain the same large banks being studied, mechanically absorbing part of their movement. A regional index answers a different investor question and may introduce currency, industry-composition and trading-hours differences. A two-factor model can include both benchmarks, but closely related indices can make their individual exposures imprecise. Specify what common risks should be removed and test whether the finding survives plausible choices.

Distinguish the estimate from its uncertainty

For a deliberately simplified check, suppose the model residual has known daily standard deviation 1 percentage point, the three event residuals are independent, event-day volatility is unchanged and the pre-estimated coefficients can be treated as fixed. Variance of the three-day sum is 3 × 1². Its standard error is √3 ≈ 1.732 points. The illustrative t statistic is −1.10/1.732 ≈ −0.64. The point estimate is negative, but the illustrative uncertainty is large relative to the estimated effect.

In finite samples the fitted alpha and beta also introduce prediction error. Events can increase volatility, and daily residuals can be serially dependent. More generally, the variance of a sum includes every variance and twice each pairwise covariance. This matters strongly in a cross-section of banks reacting to the same national announcement. Treating all observations as perfectly independent can understate uncertainty. Depending on the design, use an event portfolio, appropriate clustered inference, comparable placebo dates or cross-sectional-correlation adjustments.

Write a defensible research result

A two-group comparison asks whether banks with different attributes had different CARs. Show sample counts, weighting, the difference in means and the uncertainty. If large banks are disproportionately in one group, consider a prespecified adjustment for pre-event size. Equal-weighted and value-weighted answers can diverge if one bank dominates market value. An association between ownership and the response could reflect sector, size, liquidity or pre-existing differences. Changing the question from association to a causal ownership effect calls for an identification argument.

A useful results table has columns for event, window, benchmark, currency, sample, estimand, estimate and standard error. Here the estimand is the abnormal return relative to the named benchmark. Explain exclusions and actual overlap with bank earnings or regulatory changes. Report interesting reversals alongside the primary result. A line chart alone cannot tell a reader whether an apparent effect is a change in prices, benchmark exposure, sample composition or genuine company-specific news.

Independent problem

Try an unfamiliar problem

A three-day event produces abnormal returns of +0.5, −0.2 and +0.9 percentage points. The daily residual standard deviation is 0.8 point. Assume each pair of event days has correlation 0.25, the residual covariance is known, and estimation error is negligible. Calculate CAR and its standard error. Compare with an independence assumption and explain the direction.

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare the complete solution
  1. CAR = 0.5 − 0.2 + 0.9 = +1.2 percentage points. Each variance is 0.8² = 0.64 squared points. Each pair's covariance is 0.25 × 0.64 = 0.16.
  2. The variance of the sum is 3 × 0.64 + 2 × 3 × 0.16 = 2.88. The standard error is √2.88 ≈ 1.70 points. The associated illustrative t statistic is about +0.71.
  3. Independence would give √1.92 ≈ 1.39 points and a larger t statistic. Positively correlated residuals add less independent information. Neither specification proves that the announcement caused the return.
