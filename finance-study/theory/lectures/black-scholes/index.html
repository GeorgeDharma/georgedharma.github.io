From a replicating hedge to Black–Scholes

Build an option from stock and borrowing, then derive the continuous-time pricing equation.

What are we pricing?

A European call gives its holder the right to buy one share for a fixed strike K on one specified future date T. At maturity a share trading at 120 with a strike of 100 makes the right worth 20. At 80 it is worth zero. The holder can decline to exercise. Before maturity the claim depends on the possible future share price as well as the money and time needed to finance it.

The question is what a dealer must charge today to manufacture that payoff by trading. An investor might have an optimistic forecast of the share and another investor a pessimistic one. If both can buy the same stock-and-cash hedge, the cost of reproducing the contract disciplines its price. That is the starting point for risk-neutral valuation.

Replicate a call in two states

First take one year with a share price of 100, possible end prices of 120 and 80, a strike of 100 and a 5% annual effective risk-free return. Let Δ be shares held and B be cash invested today. Negative B means borrowing. Write one equation per possible state.

StateShare priceCall payoffHedge payoff that must match
Up12020120Δ + 1.05B = 20
Down80080Δ + 1.05B = 0

Subtract the down equation from the up equation. The cash terms cancel, leaving 40Δ = 20, so buy half a share. In the down state its value is 40. Borrow enough today to owe 40 in one year, giving B = −40/1.05 = −38.0952. The hedge costs 0.5 × 100 − 38.0952 = 11.9048 today. It pays 60 − 40 = 20 in the up state and 40 − 40 = 0 in the down state. Both state-by-state checks matter.

The same result can be written with a risk-neutral probability. To grow like a risk-free asset in expectation, the share in this pricing calculation needs an up weight p satisfying 100 × 1.05 = 120p + 80(1 − p). This gives p = 0.625. The call value is (0.625 × 20 + 0.375 × 0)/1.05 = 11.9048. An empirical belief that the up state has a 75% probability does not alter the replicating cost. Both weights must be between zero and one, which here requires the gross risk-free return to lie between the stock's down and up factors.

Let trading times get closer

In the continuous model assume dS = μS dt + σS dW. Over a short time step dt, W changes unpredictably with mean zero and variance dt. The stock has expected growth rate μ and annual volatility σ. Write the derivative value as C(t,S), a function of time and the current share price. Its slope in the share price is delta and its curvature is gamma.

An ordinary first-order chain rule misses something because the random change in S is of order √dt. Its square is of order dt. A second-order expansion keeps that contribution as trading intervals become very small. Itô’s formula gives the expression below. The subscripts t, S and SS mean time slope, stock-price slope and second stock-price derivative.

dC = (Cₜ + μS Cₛ + ½σ²S² Cₛₛ) dt + σS Cₛ dW

Build a self-financing hedge worth C from Δ shares and cash C − ΔS. Self-financing means all later changes in stock and cash holdings are funded by exchanging one for the other. With no dividends the portfolio changes by Δ dS + r(C − ΔS)dt. Set Δ = Cₛ to match the random term. Equate the remaining time terms on both sides. The μS Cₛ terms cancel. Rearranging gives the pricing equation below.

∂C∂t+12σ2S2∂2C∂S2+rS∂C∂S−rC=0

The first term describes the passage of time, the second prices local curvature, the third comes from the stock position and the last charges funding on the derivative value. The disappearance of μ is a consequence of the hedge. Risk preferences can still influence the observed stock price and other traded inputs. A terminal condition completes the problem: C(T,S) = max(S − K, 0). For a call on a non-dividend-paying share, C approaches zero as S approaches zero, and far above the strike its value approaches S less the present value of K.

Calculate the price from the closed form

Let τ = T − t, the remaining time in years. Assume constant continuously compounded r, constant σ, no dividends, continuous trading at one borrowing and lending rate, no jumps and no material transaction costs. N(z) denotes the cumulative probability up to z under a standard normal distribution. Its range is zero to one. With these assumptions the equation has the European call solution below.

d₁ = [ln(S/K) + (r + σ²/2)τ] / (σ√τ) d₂ = d₁ − σ√τ

C = S N(d₁) − K e^(−rτ) N(d₂)

Use S = K = $100, σ = 20%, r = 5% continuously compounded and τ = 1. The log price ratio is zero. The numerator of d₁ is 0.05 + 0.20²/2 = 0.07 and its denominator is 0.20, so d₁ = 0.35 and d₂ = 0.15. A normal table gives N(d₁) ≈ 0.63683 and N(d₂) ≈ 0.55962. The stock leg is $63.683. The discounted strike leg is 100 × e^(−0.05) × 0.55962 ≈ $53.232. The call price is $10.451. The earlier two-state example fixes different possible stock paths and uses an effective annual rate. Its two-state price is not a numerical target for this continuous model.

Under these assumptions call delta is N(d₁), about 0.6368 shares per option. N(d₂) is the risk-neutral exercise probability. The stock leg depends on how large the share can be when exercise occurs, so it uses a different quantity from the simple probability of exercise. Put–call parity gives the corresponding European put at C − S + K e^(−rτ) ≈ $5.57. This verifies a second contract without solving another differential equation.

Why a delta hedge can lose money

Imagine selling 100 calls and buying roughly 63.68 shares. This cancels the first-order stock sensitivity at today's price. The option's delta changes after a stock move. Here gamma is about 0.01876 per dollar per call. A $2 stock move changes delta by roughly 0.0375 per call and creates a curvature contribution of approximately ½ × 0.01876 × 2² = $0.0375 per call before time decay and other changes. A dealer short the calls bears the opposite sign on that curvature.

Rebalancing has a cost, large jumps skip intermediate hedge prices, and the volatility surface in actual markets changes with strike and maturity. To quote implied volatility one instead starts with a market price and solves for the σ that reproduces it under a chosen convention. Different implied volatilities across strikes mean one constant σ cannot match all prices simultaneously. Dividend payments alter both the hedge cash flows and forward price. Early exercise on an American contract changes the boundary problem.

Independent problem

Try an unfamiliar problem

A different stock starts at 50 and can finish in one period at 65 or 45. A European call has strike 55. The gross risk-free rate is 1.02. Find the replicating shares and cash, the price and the risk-neutral up probability. What happens to the hedge if the share can jump to 30 as an additional state?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare the complete solution
  1. The call pays 10 or zero. Subtracting the two payoff equations gives Δ = (10 − 0)/(65 − 45) = 0.5. In the down state the stock holding is 22.5, so B = −22.5/1.02 ≈ −22.0588.
  2. The initial call cost is 0.5 × 50 − 22.0588 ≈ 2.9412. The risk-neutral up probability solves 51 = 65p + 45(1 − p), giving p = 0.30. Discounted risk-neutral value is 3/1.02 ≈ 2.9412.
  3. At a third end price of 30 the portfolio pays 15 − 22.5 = −7.5 while the call pays zero. The two-state hedge fails there. With three states and only two independent traded payoffs, a single no-arbitrage price for every claim generally requires additional traded instruments or model assumptions.

Explain it in a desk interview

A clear answer starts with the contract, reproduces its payoff, and explains which risk disappears when delta is matched. Then describe one assumption the desk must monitor. If asked why the stock's expected return does not enter, show it cancels on the stock and hedge sides of Itô’s expansion. If asked whether a dealer is perfectly hedged tomorrow, identify gamma, discrete rebalancing, volatility changes and jumps, and name the position that would gain or lose under the proposed move.
