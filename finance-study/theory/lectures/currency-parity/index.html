Covered interest parity and the cross-currency basis

Construct two financing paths with identical dated cash, then add trading frictions.

Read the quotation as a unit conversion

A euro manufacturer must pay a US supplier $1m in one year. A dealer quotes spot at 1.10 USD per EUR. Write the units underneath: one euro buys $1.10 today. Today’s euro cost of one dollar is therefore 1/1.10 = €0.90909. If someone says the euro strengthened, check that the quoted dollars per euro increased. A reciprocal quote reverses the direction of the number.

A forward fixes the exchange rate for a given future value date. Its forward price is the agreed exchange rate. A newly struck at-market forward usually has close to zero value at inception under the specified collateral/funding convention. A previously agreed forward can acquire a positive or negative value as rates move. The initial exchange rate and the contract's market value are distinct units.

Build the two covered paths

Use one year, no transaction costs, the same borrowing and lending rate in each currency, no default, and a forward that settles on the deposit maturity date. The one-year effective euro interest rate is 2% and the dollar rate is 5%. Compare using €1 under two strategies. Path one invests €1 in euros, receiving €1.02 in one year. Sell those future euros today at the forward rate F, locking $1.02F at maturity.

Path two converts €1 at spot, obtaining $1.10, and invests it in dollars. The final locked dollars are $1.10 × 1.05 = $1.155. Both strategies use the same initial euro and have risk-free terminal dollars in this model. Equal prices for identical payoffs imply 1.02F = 1.155. Solving gives F = 1.13235294 USD per EUR.

F=S1+rUSD1+rEUR

S and F are quoted in dollars per euro. The numerator uses the interest rate on dollars, the price currency. The denominator uses the rate on euros, the unit being priced. The reciprocal EUR-per-USD quote naturally reverses this ratio. Under continuously compounded zero rates and maturity T, the relation becomes F = S exp[(rUSD − rEUR)T]. Use matching dates, calendars, day counts and compounding before comparing quotes.

Take a quoted forward apart

Suppose the dealer will buy euros in one year at $1.15 per euro. With the assumptions above, forward euros sell for more than their replicating cost. Borrow $1.10 today, convert to €1 and place the euro on deposit. At inception the cash flows cancel. Sell the resulting €1.02 forward at 1.15. At maturity deliver those euros for $1.173. Repay $1.155 on the borrowing and retain $0.018 for each initial €1 in this illustrative trade.

If the forward quote were below the parity value, reverse the positions under symmetric funding and executable quotes. An indicative midpoint does not establish that either path can be traded. If the bank's dollar borrowing rate exceeds its dollar deposit rate, use the actual rate for each direction separately. Pair the spot ask with an actual spot purchase and the forward bid with a forward sale.

Hedge the payable with a forward or money market

The manufacturer needs dollars and has euros. Under a forward quoted 1.15 USD per EUR, it agrees to pay 1,000,000/1.15 = €869,565.22 at maturity to receive $1m. If the final spot is 1.00 the unhedged bill would have been €1m. If spot is 1.20 it would have cost €833,333.33. The hedge has exchanged that uncertain euro bill for a specified one. It is successful in locking the budget even in the second state, where the unhedged bill would have been lower.

A money-market hedge buys the dollars now. Discount the future dollar bill at 5% to $952,380.95. Convert it at spot to an initial €865,800.87 financing need. Borrow those euros at 2%, or charge the same opportunity cost to available euros. At maturity the dollars have grown to $1m and the euro obligation is about €883,116.88. This agrees with 1,000,000 divided by the parity forward of 1.13235294. It can differ from the actual dealer quote because the hypothetical arbitrage assumptions do not always apply.

Covered parity, uncovered parity and the basis

Covered parity compares two contracted, currency-hedged payoffs. Uncovered parity replaces the locked exchange rate with an expectation about the future spot under an additional restriction on currency risk premia. If an investor accepts exchange risk, the payoff is uncertain. A rate differential by itself does not make an executable forecast of next year's spot. Quote convention, the currency in which returns are measured and approximating percentages versus exact gross returns all affect how an uncovered relation is stated.

In real markets a cross-currency basis records a difference between funding through a swap and a reference cash funding rate under a specified quote convention. Dealers incur balance-sheet costs, face collateral and counterparty constraints, and may see one-sided demand for hedges. A persistent difference from textbook covered parity can compensate for limited intermediation. Report which currency leg receives the spread before interpreting a signed basis. A funding desk can face a material marginal cost even when a benchmark rate is freely observable.

Independent problem

Try an unfamiliar problem

A second company must pay €2m in one year and budgets in dollars. Spot is $1.25 per euro, USD earns 4% effective for one year and EUR earns 1%. Compute the covered forward, future dollar budget and initial dollars needed for a money-market hedge. If the forward dealer asks $1.31, explain the trade direction before considering frictions.
