Build and reconcile the three statements

Follow one shop from its opening ledger through profit, cash and closing balances.

Start with a shop and its owners

A shop can sell goods today, earn a profit today and collect the money next month. Three statements let a reader see each part of that story. The income statement measures performance over an interval. The balance sheet records resources and claims on one date. The cash-flow statement explains the cash movement between two dates. The same event can appear in all three with different timing.

At the start of the example the shop holds €40k cash, €20k inventory and €40k net equipment. Suppliers are owed €20k, and the owners' existing claim is €80k. There is no debt, no receivable from customers and no other claim. Both sides of the opening balance are €100k. All further amounts are in thousands of euros. The business has no tax, interest, dividends, foreign currency or unusual items in this exercise. Those simplifying assumptions allow the underlying links to be checked directly.

Assets=Liabilities+Equity

A debit is the left side of a journal entry and a credit is the right. An increase in an asset or an expense normally appears as a debit. An increase in a liability, equity or revenue normally appears as a credit. Cash coming in increases an asset, so it is a debit to the shop's cash account. A bank statement may describe the same amount as a credit from the bank's perspective because the bank owes that deposit to the shop.

The delivery generates two entries with different measurement bases. One records the €70k selling price. The other transfers €30k of actual recorded inventory cost to expense. Recording only the customer invoice would leave the business reporting goods it no longer controls and an overstated profit. The €50k collection later exchanges one asset for another. Recording a second sale at collection would overstate revenue.

Equity rolls from 80 to 103 through net income of 23. There are no owner distributions or contributions in this period. The new loan funded the business but did not enrich it at the moment of borrowing because a matching repayment claim arose. Likewise the €15k equipment purchase uses cash now but charges income over the periods earning revenue from it.

If a customer subsequently experiences financial difficulty, separate recognition of the original sale from measurement of the remaining receivable. A supported loss allowance reduces expected collection value and current earnings. Depending on the facts, it can be a credit-loss assessment rather than a reversal of the entire sale. A real close would distinguish price disputes, returns, unperformed obligations and inability to pay.

Build the balance sheet from movements

For every account write opening balance, additions, reductions and closing balance. Receivables start at zero, rise by 70 and fall by 50, so 20 remains. Inventory starts at 20, rises by 25 and falls by 30, so 15 remains. Suppliers start at 20 and are owed another 25, then receive 22, leaving payables of 23. Net equipment closes at 40 + 15 − 5 = 50. The new borrowing is 30.

Compute cash from actual bank transactions. Start at 40, add borrowing of 30 and customer receipts of 50, then deduct supplier payments of 22, staff payments of 12 and capex of 15. Closing cash is 71. Assets total 71 + 20 + 15 + 50 = 156. The claims side totals 23 + 30 + 103 = 156. Setting cash equal to the amount needed to balance would destroy this independent check. A balanced sheet alone can still hide a missing transaction or a wrong classification.

Reconcile cash by two routes

The direct route calculates operating cash as customer receipts of 50 less cash paid to suppliers of 22 and to staff of 12, giving 16. The indirect route begins with net income of 23. Add back the five of depreciation because it reduced earnings without paying cash this period. Deduct the receivables increase of 20 because some earned revenue remains unpaid. Add five for the inventory decline because goods consumed exceeded purchases, and add three for the payables increase because purchases exceeded supplier payments. The total is 23 + 5 − 20 + 5 + 3 = 16.

Investing cash is −15 from buying equipment. Financing cash is +30 from borrowing. Operating 16 − investing use 15 + financing source 30 = cash increase 31, exactly the movement from 40 to 71. The balance-sheet cash, bank movement and sum of cash-flow sections should agree. If direct and indirect operating cash disagree, inspect receivables, inventory and payables separately before treating any residual as “other.”

Independent problem

Try an unfamiliar problem

A rival with the same opening balances and identical sales, costs, purchases and borrowing collects only €35k this period. It also delays €6k of the planned supplier payment until the next period. Find closing receivables, payables, cash, net income and both measures of operating cash. Which change deserves attention from a lender?

Compare the complete solution
  1. Receivables close at 70 − 35 = 35. Supplier payments are 22 − 6 = 16, so payables close at 20 + 25 − 16 = 29. Revenue and the cost of goods delivered are unchanged and net income remains 23.
  2. Direct operating cash is 35 − 16 − 12 = 7. Indirect cash is 23 + 5 − 35 + 5 + 9 = 7. The nine arises because payables grow from 20 to 29.
  3. Closing cash is 40 + 7 − 15 + 30 = 62. Balance-sheet assets are 62 + 35 + 15 + 50 = 162 against payables 29, debt 30 and equity 103. A lender should trace whether slower collections will reverse and when the deferred supplier payment is due. Higher reported profit would not by itself fund that date.

Present it as a workpaper

A reviewer needs the reporting period, the unit scale, opening balances tied to the previous close, transaction support, separate movement schedules and a list of unresolved reconciling items. In an interview, start with one event, describe each account affected, and then walk it through the statements. Explaining why a collection changes cash but leaves profit untouched gives the reader a test they can apply to any credit sale.
