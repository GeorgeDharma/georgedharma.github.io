Bootstrap a yield curve and calculate its risk

Build discount factors from three traded bonds, recover forwards and reprice a curve shock.

Start with promises, dates and a common risk basis

A bond is a sequence of contractual cash flows. Valuing it requires a price today for receiving money on each payment date. Let D₁ be today's price of one unit of currency certain in one year, D₂ for two years, and D₃ for three years. A discount factor has price-per-future-unit units. If a two-year promise of 100 has discount factor 0.90, its value today is 90. The factors used together must correspond to the same currency and compatible credit, collateral and funding assumptions.

This teaching curve has a single issuer or common default-free basis, annual coupons, annual effective rates, face value 100 and valuation on a coupon date. There is no accrued coupon, optionality or transaction cost. Market inputs in a real implementation require settlement date, clean versus dirty price, business-day rules, day-count fractions, stub periods and inclusion of the correct upcoming coupon. A current clean quote alone does not specify the model's full cash flows.

P=∑t=1TCFtDt

Cash at date t times its discount factor gives the present value of that payment. Add the present values because independent claims on dated cash can be bought and combined. A yield to maturity compresses all of one bond's payments into a single internal rate. A spot curve instead assigns a separately identified rate to each maturity. Two bonds with the same maturity but different coupons can have different yields when the spot curve slopes.

Bootstrap the unknowns in date order

An annual one-year zero paying 100 costs 96. A two-year bond with 5% annual coupon costs 99. A three-year bond with 4% annual coupon costs 97. The shortest instrument contains one unknown. Substitute that result into the next price, and continue. This triangular system is called bootstrapping.

The present values of its payments are 3.8400, 3.5886 and 89.5714. They total 97.0000. Discount factors can exceed one in a negative-rate environment. The familiar declining positive-rate pattern is not a universal condition across currencies and dates.

Recover the rate on a future interval

A one-year interest rate beginning in one year is a forward rate for the interval between years one and two. The contract value of a zero-cost forward investment requires one unit placed at year one to buy D₁/D₂ units at year two. Thus the one-year forward rate is D₁/D₂ − 1, about 7.01% on these inputs. A two-year spot rate is an average through compounding, whereas this forward isolates one interval.

Arbitrage gives a transaction connecting the two dates. It does not assert that the short rate actually observed next year must equal today's implied forward. Forward prices can include term premia and new information can arrive. A trading strategy that borrows short and invests long without locking future borrowing costs bears refinancing risk.

Reprice before approximating

Suppose each annual zero rate rises by 100 basis points. Add 0.01 to each original zero rate and calculate the new present value of each payment. That is an exact revaluation for this specified shock and the assumed fixed cash flows. Moving a single bond's yield-to-maturity by 100 basis points is a different shock on a nonflat curve. State what was bumped, especially when comparing the risk of multiple instruments.

DV01 expresses the price change for roughly a one-basis-point rate change. This case uses a positive magnitude for price lost when rates rise. Compute [P(curve − 1bp) − P(curve + 1bp)]/2. For the 3-year bond it is approximately 0.02661 per 100 face. A position of 1,000,000 face has 10,000 units of face 100, giving about 266.10 currency units per basis point. Keep signed exposure and quoted DV01 conventions explicit in a hedge ticket.

For a simple flat-yield bond the first derivative gives modified duration and the second derivative gives convexity. Under a small change dy in that yield, ΔP/P ≈ −Dmod × dy + ½ × convexity × dy². Duration is locally linear. For the usual option-free fixed payments at positive discount factors, downward-yield price gains generally exceed equal-size upward-yield losses because the price-yield relation is curved. Callable debt can have different convexity because the expected repayment dates themselves change.

Make the risk usable at a desk

A portfolio can have zero parallel DV01 and still be exposed to a steepening curve. Calculate key-rate sensitivities by changing rates near selected maturities individually, with a declared interpolation method, then reprice. Choose available hedge instruments with different maturity exposures. Solve for positions matching the desired sensitivities and independently stress a twist. Cash-flow matching or immunization for a liability also needs present-value and horizon checks.

Observed market instruments may not provide one equation per date. Interpolating discount factors, log discount factors or forwards produces different intermediate cash-flow prices. A model should record its inputs, interpolation and extrapolation conventions and reprice the instruments it used to fit. In multi-curve derivatives valuation the curve used to discount a collateralized payment and the curve used to project a floating reset can differ. The single-curve example is the starting mechanism, not a prescription for all markets.

Independent problem

Try an unfamiliar problem

A fourth instrument pays 5 in year one, 5 in year two and 105 in year three. On this curve find its fair price and the price effect if only the year-three discount factor falls by 0.010 with all other factors fixed. Does this shock equal a 100-basis-point rate rise?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare the complete solution
  1. Price is 5 × 0.96 + 5 × 0.897142857 + 105 × 0.861263736 ≈ 99.72 per 100 face. Each cash flow is discounted at its own maturity.
  2. Only the final cash flow is affected. The price falls by 105 × 0.010 = 1.05 per 100 face, to about 98.67. Earlier cash flows keep their values.
  3. A change in a discount factor is dimensionless price per future unit. A change of 100 basis points is a one-percentage-point change in an interest rate. Their relation depends on maturity, starting rate and compounding. Convert the new factor into a three-year spot rate before making a rate-shock comparison.
