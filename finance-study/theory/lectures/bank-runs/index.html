Diamond–Dybvig and the logic of a bank run

Build a deposit contract and find when a patient depositor would join the queue.

Why offer a withdrawable deposit?

A household may need to consume its savings unexpectedly next month. A factory may need several years to turn equipment into output. If every household holds only cash, it can meet emergencies but gives up the productive long investment. If each household invests all its savings in the factory, an emergency forces a costly early sale. A bank can pool unpredictable individual needs and finance a long asset while offering short claims to depositors.

Diamond and Dybvig model this arrangement as liquidity insurance. Depositors do not yet know whether they will need to consume early or can wait. In the model, pooling helps when individual needs are uncertain but the usual aggregate proportion needing cash is predictable. A contract that shares resources across early and late consumers can improve their expected utility. The same contract can give a patient person reason to rush when many others are expected to withdraw.

Build the contract at three dates

At date zero depositors contribute €1 each. At date one 40% ordinarily need to consume immediately and the rest can wait until date two. Short storage returns €1 per €1. A long asset pays €1.60 at date two per €1 invested, but liquidating it at date one realizes only €0.80. The bank keeps €0.46 in storage and invests €0.54 long, per euro deposited. It promises €1.15 to each early claimant, in queue order while resources remain. Late claimants split what is left at date two. This is an illustrative feasible contract, with no claim that its parameters maximize utility.

If only the genuinely early 40% withdraw, their claims consume 0.40 × 1.15 = €0.46. The remaining long investment matures at 0.54 × 1.60 = €0.864. Dividing by 0.60 patient depositors gives €1.44 each. An individual who could need the money early receives more than the €1 initially deposited. Patient depositors earn part of the long investment's return. The bank can pay both because, in this allocation, it avoids liquidating the productive asset early.

For a concrete welfare comparison assume logarithmic utility of consumption and private storage plus long investment on the same terms. A single person choosing short investment a and long investment 1 − a can consume a + 0.8(1 − a) if early or a + 1.6(1 − a) if late. Pooling separates aggregate reserves from individual consumption timing. To make a welfare claim, compare expected utilities under an explicit utility and feasible allocation. The fact that one contractual payment exceeds one euro alone is insufficient.

Run the queue under unexpected withdrawals

Let w be the fraction arriving early, including any patient people who run. The early claims total 1.15w. If they exceed €0.46, the bank sells long assets to cover the difference. Raising €0.08 of cash requires selling €0.10 of the long asset at its acquisition cost, sacrificing €0.16 of later maturity value. Liquidation transfers a liquidity decision made today into a lower payoff available tomorrow.

For withdrawals between 40% and the exhaustion of all assets, the long quantity sold is (1.15w − 0.46)/0.80. Remaining resources at date two are 1.60 × [0.54 − (1.15w − 0.46)/0.80]. Divide by 1 − w to obtain the late payoff per person. At w = 60%, sell 0.23/0.80 = 0.2875 long units. The remaining 0.2525 units pay 0.404 at maturity. Split among 40% of the depositors, that gives only €1.01 each.

Find the incentive to join a run

Set the late payoff equal to the early promise of 1.15. Multiplying by 1 − w and simplifying gives 1.784 − 2.30w = 1.15 − 1.15w. The crossing is w ≈ 55.13%. Above that level, and before early claims exhaust the bank, a patient depositor has a financial reason to arrive early if they can preserve the withdrawal for consumption later. Depositors reacting to one another create a coordination problem.

If everybody arrives, maximum date-one cash is 0.46 + 0.54 × 0.80 = 0.892. The bank can pay the full 1.15 promise to only about 0.892/1.15 = 77.57% of original depositors under the simple sequential rule. People near the back of the queue are exposed to failure. The bank could meet its ordinary obligations at date two under the original withdrawal behavior, yet expectations can move it toward a run. A model with fundamental asset losses can also produce withdrawals. Observing a queue does not by itself distinguish those explanations.

What intervention changes

Credible deposit insurance can remove the incentive to rush merely to beat other insured claimants. Its scope, payout speed and credibility affect behavior. Full coverage can also weaken monitoring by depositors or encourage bank risk taking, which creates a role for capital requirements and supervision. A lender of last resort can lend cash against acceptable collateral, allowing some long assets to remain invested. The lender must decide collateral values, haircuts, funding dates and how to treat insolvency.

Holding more liquid assets reduces the need for fire sales and gives up some expected maturity return. Suspension of immediate convertibility after a cutoff could protect remaining assets but interferes with genuine early consumption. A real bank's uninsured deposit mix, speed of electronic transfers, collateral eligibility and concentration of depositors belong in the liquidity stress. These are distinct from whether a bond portfolio has fallen in fair value and from whether the bank has enough capital after recognizing losses.

Independent problem

Try an unfamiliar problem

Suppose half of depositors withdraw and the secondary market now pays only €0.50 for an early sale of one long unit. Keep initial reserves, the long return and the contractual early payment fixed. Calculate the late payoff. Find the largest share of original depositors that could be paid in full at date one if everyone ran.

Compare the complete solution
  1. At 50% withdrawals, claims require 0.575. After applying 0.46 reserves the cash shortage is 0.115. To raise that money at 0.50 per unit the bank sells 0.23 long units.
  2. It retains 0.31 long units worth 0.496 at maturity. Dividing by the 0.50 depositors who waited yields 0.992 each, below the 1.15 early payment. The lower sale price changes incentives even at the same frequency of withdrawals.
  3. All assets sold immediately realize 0.46 + 0.54 × 0.50 = 0.73. The full early promise can be paid to 0.73/1.15 ≈ 63.48% of original depositors. The number is a queue capacity under these terms, not an estimate of any observed bank’s recovery.
