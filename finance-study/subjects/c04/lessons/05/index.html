Terminal value

Estimate the cash after the explicit forecast. A perpetual-growth formula requires a discount rate above the growth rate and economically sustainable reinvestment.

The starting point

A discounted cash-flow model usually forecasts several years separately. Terminal value estimates the remaining cash flows beyond that explicit forecast. A growing perpetuity assumes free cash flow grows at a sustainable constant rate indefinitely.

At the final forecast date, terminal value is the next year’s cash flow divided by the discount rate minus the growth rate. The discount rate must exceed growth. Use cash after the reinvestment needed for that growth and then discount the terminal value back to the valuation date.

Theory and method

Gordon growth model with endogenous reinvestment

Growing perpetuity · Value-neutral growth · Continuing value

Can raising perpetual growth from 2% to 3% lower a company's terminal value when its discount rate is 8%?

A low-return business can grow only by retaining cash that investors could have deployed at a higher return elsewhere. Raising long-run growth increases future earnings but also reduces what can be distributed next year. The common shortcut of increasing growth while leaving cash unchanged misses this cost. If new capital earns less than the return investors require, the additional reinvestment can reduce the estimated value of the mature business.

How the result is built

TV = N₁(1−g/R)/(k−g) ; sign of ∂TV/∂g = sign of (1−k/R)

TV is terminal operating value at the end of the explicit forecast; N₁ first continuing-year after-tax operating profit, g stable growth, R return on incremental capital and k operating discount rate. ∂TV/∂g describes local value sensitivity holding N₁, R and k fixed.

  1. Sustainable reinvestment equals N₁g/R, so first continuing free cash is N₁(1−g/R). Discount its growing series to obtain the terminal value.
  2. Increasing g shrinks the denominator but also shrinks free cash. Algebra gives ∂TV/∂g = N₁(1−k/R)/(k−g)².
  3. For N₁ = 10 and k = 8%, setting R = 6% makes additional growth destroy value, R = 8% makes it value-neutral and R = 12% makes it add value.

When the reasoning holds

  • Keep growth below the discount rate for convergence and retain a feasible investment fraction. Constant marginal returns and a stable competition environment are long-run assumptions.
  • The sensitivity holds first continuing operating profit fixed. If a proposed change alters next-year margins, risk or incremental capital return, reforecast the full expression.

Terms in this lesson
Discount rate
Rate used to express a future payment in today’s money, reflecting time and the relevant risk.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Perpetuity
A stream of payments assumed to continue indefinitely under the model.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
How to read the notation
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.

All notation examples

First example

Year 5 ends with expected year-6 free cash flow of 12, growth of 2% and a discount rate of 8%. Terminal value at year 5 is 12/(0.08 − 0.02) = 200. Discounting five years gives 200/(1.08⁵) ≈ 136.12 today.

Place terminal value on the forecast timeline

A company can keep earning long after the analyst's detailed five-year forecast ends. Terminal value is the value at the end of the explicit forecast of all later cash flows covered by the continuing-business assumptions. It is a value measured on a future date, not a cash receipt available today.

For stable cash flows growing at g and discounted at r, each discounted payment is a constant fraction of its predecessor. Summing the geometric series gives next period's free cash divided by r minus g. Convergence requires r greater than g in the standard positive-cash perpetuity.

Build a sustainable first continuing-period cash flow

The numerator must be the first cash after the final explicit period. Apply growth only once. If the final forecast cash includes a temporary tax benefit, unusual margin or low capital spending, normalizing sustainable operating earnings and reinvestment can matter more than mechanically growing the last cash number.

A positive perpetual growth forecast needs capital to support it. With stable incremental return on capital, the required reinvestment fraction is growth divided by that return. Apply the resulting free-cash conversion to sustainable after-tax operating profit and check that maturity assumptions fit the industry.

Handle sensitivity and finite businesses

As the discount-rate minus growth spread shrinks, value becomes highly sensitive. A table changing r and g can show this, but the cash numerator should also respond if greater growth requires more reinvestment. A firm cannot outgrow the market that sustains it indefinitely without a convincing change in assumptions.

A concession that expires without renewal and equipment exhausted at contract end need a finite cash-flow forecast and any net recovery or cleanup cost. A perpetuity for an asset with no continuing rights overrides the economics of the contract.

Method

Reconcile the continuing value

  1. Identify the date

    Mark the final explicit cash flow and the first continuing cash flow separately. State the date on which the terminal value is measured.

  2. Normalize

    Forecast sustainable operating earnings, reinvestment and free cash for the first continuing year. Check the long-run growth and required return.

  3. Discount and test

    Divide first continuing cash by r − g and discount this value back the correct number of years. Stress both the spread and the investment supporting growth.

Worked case

A mature business settles into steady growth

At the end of year 3 an analyst values cash flows from year 4 onward. Year-4 after-tax operating profit is 30, long-run growth 3%, return on incremental capital 12% and operating discount rate 9%. Ignore other reinvestment effects.

Working

  1. The sustainable investment fraction is 3%/12% = 25%. Year-4 free cash flow is 30 × (1 − 0.25) = 22.5.
  2. Terminal value at the end of year 3 is 22.5/(0.09 − 0.03) = 375. The year-3 cash flow, if any, is still counted separately in the explicit forecast.
  3. Present value of the continuing business is 375/1.09³ = 289.57. Treating all 30 of profit as cash would have valued the continuing claim at 500 in year 3 and ignored its growth investment.

Interpretation

A large continuing component makes the model sensitive to mature margins and capital productivity. Show its share of total estimated value to indicate where forecast scrutiny belongs.

A common mistake

Using year 4's growth-inclusive cash as the numerator and multiplying it by growth again.

How to correct it

Use the cash immediately following the terminal valuation date exactly once. Label every input by year.

Quick check
Keeping year-6 cash flow and the discount rate fixed, what is terminal value at year 5 if growth is 1%?

The spread increases to 7 percentage points. The calculation 12/0.07 gives 171.43 at year 5. Discounting this amount to today is a separate step.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

A firm forecasts year-5 after-tax operating profit of 48. Year 4 is the last explicit period. Sustainable growth is 2%, return on new capital 10%, and discount rate 8%.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Derive year-5 free cash and end-year-4 terminal value.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Calculate the terminal component in today's value.

  3. Part C · 2 marks

    If the business instead loses its operating license after year 7 without renewal, explain the correction.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Reinvestment fraction is 0.02/0.10 = 20%. Free cash is 48 × 0.80 = 38.4. Terminal value at year 4 is 38.4/0.06 = 640.

Part B 2 marks

Discounting four periods gives 640/1.08⁴ = about 470.42. Explicit cash flows through year 4 are valued separately.

Part C 2 marks

Forecast finite cash for years 5–7 and any net asset recovery or shutdown costs. The assumed infinite stream is unavailable once the operating right expires.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Can raising perpetual growth from 2% to 3% lower a company's terminal value when its discount rate is 8%?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Yes. I would hold first continuing operating profit fixed and include the reinvestment needed for the higher growth. At a 6% return on new capital, maintaining 3% growth requires reinvesting half of profit, whereas 2% requires one third. At an 8% required return the additional investment destroys value. The precise result reverses if the company can sustain a return on new capital above 8%.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If incremental return exactly equals the discount rate, why can terminal value remain constant as stable growth changes?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Replace R with k. The numerator becomes N₁(k−g)/k, cancelling the growth spread in the denominator and giving TV = N₁/k. Investors are indifferent in this model because every additional investment earns exactly its required return.
