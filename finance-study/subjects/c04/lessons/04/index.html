Cost of capital

Use a rate consistent with currency, inflation, financial leverage and operating risk. Distinguish diversifiable company risk from priced market risk.

The starting point

A discount rate describes the return required on a particular stream of cash. Match currency, purchasing power, risk and recipient. Nominal cash includes anticipated price changes and needs a nominal rate. Cash in today’s purchasing power is expressed in real terms and needs a real rate.

In the standard diversified-investor model, the risk premium compensates for exposure to broad risks that remain in an investor’s portfolio. Diversifiable events can still reduce expected cash flows. A company-wide hurdle can misvalue a project whose industry, debt or currency differs from the parent’s.

Theory and method

Hamada equation and bottom-up beta

Unlevering and relevering beta · Pure-play cost of capital · CAPM

A listed peer has equity beta 1.4 and much more debt than the project you are valuing. How would you use it without importing its financial risk?

A peer's shareholders bear its operating risk amplified by the lender's prior claim. An analyst looking for a comparable business risk first removes the peer's leverage effect, checks whether the operations genuinely match, and then applies the financing policy appropriate to the target. Otherwise a change in capital structure alone can appear to transform the projected cash flows' underlying industry risk, distorting an acquisition ceiling or project hurdle.

How the result is built

βᵁ = βᴸ/[1+(1−T)D/E] ; βᴸ,target = βᵁ[1+(1−T)Dₜ/Eₜ]

βᴸ and βᵁ denote levered equity beta and unlevered operating beta. D/E is market debt divided by market equity, T the assumed corporate tax rate and Dₜ/Eₜ the target leverage ratio. This Hamada specification treats debt beta as negligible under its tax-shield assumptions.

  1. Suppose the peer's debt/equity is 1 and its tax rate is 25%. Unlevering its 1.4 beta gives 1.4/[1 + 0.75] = 0.80.
  2. Apply target debt/equity of 0.4 at the same tax rate. Target equity beta is 0.80 × [1 + 0.75 × 0.4] = 1.04.
  3. At a matching 3% risk-free rate and 5% market premium, target cost of equity is 3% + 1.04 × 5% = 8.2%. Moving from this to WACC also requires the target debt cost.

When the reasoning holds

  • Economic comparability matters before financial adjustment. A technology platform and a utility can have different asset risk despite identical leverage ratios.
  • The compact relation assumes negligible debt beta and a particular treatment of tax shields. Risky debt, excess cash or materially different rebalancing policies warrant an adjusted model.

Terms in this lesson
Discount rate
Rate used to express a future payment in today’s money, reflecting time and the relevant risk.
Risk premium
Extra expected return for bearing risk, or the amount a risk-averse person would give up to replace a gamble with certainty, depending on context.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
How to read the notation
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.

All notation examples

First example

Suppose a certain real receipt of €100 next year becomes €103 in nominal euros as prices rise 3%. If the real required return is 4%, the consistent nominal rate is 1.04 × 1.03 − 1 = 7.12%. Either 100/1.04 or 103/1.0712 gives about €96.15 today.

Choose the discount rate by the cash promise

A known euro payment and a risky share of a company's cash cannot be assigned the same discount rate solely because they occur on the same date. Begin by identifying payment currency, inflation basis, business risk, financing exposure and who receives the cash.

The risk-free starting rate can differ across currencies because nominal inflation and monetary conditions differ. A project located in one country can generate cash in another currency. Location alone does not determine which nominal rate matches the cash forecast.

Translate real to nominal consistently

A real cash flow is expressed in the purchasing power of a specified date. A nominal cash flow includes future changes in the price level. With constant inflation π, the consistent nominal gross return is the real gross return multiplied by 1 + π. The extra product term makes exact results differ from simply adding rates.

Choose real-real or nominal-nominal for the whole calculation. Specific selling prices or import costs may rise differently from broad inflation, so the operating forecast can still require relative-price adjustments even if it is stated in constant purchasing-power units.

Distinguish market risk from bad expected outcomes

A forecast of expected cash should already reflect such possibilities as product failure or customers paying less than promised. A required risk premium compensates for exposure to risk investors price, such as outcomes correlated with market conditions. A diversified investor can bear some firm-specific variation without requiring the same premium as broad market exposure.

State whether a scenario adjustment changes the average cash forecast or its systematic exposure. Reducing expected cash for an identified loss and mechanically adding a second full penalty for that same expected loss risks overstating the discount. Other remaining systematic uncertainty may still warrant a premium.

Method

Audit cash flow and discount-rate consistency

  1. Cash-flow labels

    Write currency, real or nominal, payment date, and recipient beside the cash-flow series. Confirm whether expectations already incorporate an adverse event.

  2. Rate construction

    Select a currency-matched baseline and a risk premium suitable for the operations or equity claim. Convert real and nominal rates using growth factors.

  3. Independent check

    If assumptions permit, value once in real terms and once in nominal terms or compare an appropriate peer rate. Reconcile the two present values.

Worked case

A nominal forecast built from real factory income

A factory will produce expected operating cash in today's purchasing power of 50 in each of two years. Annual inflation is 4% and the appropriate real operating discount rate is 6%. Assume the specific operating cash rises with general prices.

Working

  1. Nominal cash is 52 in year 1 and 54.08 in year 2. The consistent nominal required rate is 1.06 × 1.04 − 1 = 10.24%.
  2. Real valuation is 50/1.06 + 50/1.06² = 91.67. Nominal valuation is 52/1.1024 + 54.08/1.1024², also 91.67.
  3. Discounting the nominal cash at 6% would count inflation in cash without requiring the corresponding nominal return. That produces an artificially higher value.

Interpretation

The equality relies on applying inflation consistently to both dated cash flows and the discount factors. Changed real operating margins would require a different real forecast as well.

A common mistake

Adding a country premium solely because headquarters are registered in a higher-risk jurisdiction.

How to correct it

Identify the locations of operations, cash currency, sources of revenue and exposure to market-wide risk. Explain how any chosen premium relates to the cash flows.

Quick check
Which rate should be applied directly to that €103 cash flow if it already includes inflation?

The €103 is stated in next year’s euros. Discounting that nominal amount at its consistent nominal rate gives the same value as working wholly in real terms.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

A euro project pays expected year-end operating cash of 108 in nominal euros. It is 100 in today's purchasing power before 8% inflation. The appropriate real return is 5%.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Derive the consistent nominal return and value the payment by both methods.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    What error results from discounting 108 at 5%?

  3. Part C · 2 marks

    Would a rate for levered dividends necessarily apply to this operating cash?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Nominal return is 1.05 × 1.08 − 1 = 13.4%. Value is 108/1.134 = 95.24, identical to 100/1.05.

Part B 2 marks

The estimate is 102.86, an overstatement of 7.62 against the matched valuation. The discount rate ignores inflation already present in the cash.

Part C 2 marks

No. Dividends belong to residual shareholders after debt financing. Operating cash potentially serves all funders and requires a rate consistent with business risk and financing assumptions.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A listed peer has equity beta 1.4 and much more debt than the project you are valuing. How would you use it without importing its financial risk?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would screen peers on operating exposure, then unlever their market equity betas using market-value debt and the assumptions of the chosen tax-shield model. For the stated peer that produces asset beta 0.8. At target debt/equity 0.4, equity beta becomes 1.04 and the CAPM equity cost 8.2%. I would check debt risk, omitted cash and the reliability of the peer beta before relying on a single estimate.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if the peer consists half of low-risk cash and half of operating assets by value?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Its observed unlevered beta is diluted by cash. If cash beta is zero and the split is truly half, operating beta is roughly twice the inclusive asset beta. I would remove cash consistently before relevering, using the actual asset values and cash risk assumptions.
