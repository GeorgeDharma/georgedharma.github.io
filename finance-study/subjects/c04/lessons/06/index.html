Bridging to equity

Adjust operating value for non-operating cash, debt and other claims. Match debt, leases and minority interests to what the starting value includes.

The starting point

Start an equity bridge by checking what the operating value includes. A forecast covering 100% of a consolidated subsidiary includes cash flows partly belonging to minority shareholders. A business with excess cash or a separate investment may own assets omitted from the operating forecast.

To estimate common equity, add non-operating assets excluded from operations and subtract claims that rank ahead of, or belong to someone other than, common shareholders. Check leases and pensions against how the operating cash flows were constructed. An obligation already allowed for in cash flows can be double-counted.

Theory and method

Sum-of-the-parts valuation and enterprise-to-equity bridge

Noncontrolling interest · Equity-accounted associates · Holding-company costs

How can you value a group with a controlled subsidiary and a minority associate without subtracting the same outside ownership twice?

A group can consolidate all the income from a majority-owned factory while receiving only its ownership share of a separate associate's profit. A group DCF or peer valuation that covers the whole factory includes cash belonging to other factory shareholders. A valuation that excludes the associate's operations may still omit a valuable stake owned by the parent. The right bridge follows economic inclusion. Begin each calculation by marking exactly which operations, debt and ownership interests it has already counted.

How the result is built

Eparent = EVconsolidated + Aexcluded + Csurplus − Dgroup − NCI − other senior claims

Eparent is parent common equity. EVconsolidated covers all consolidated operations before financing claims. Aexcluded is the parent's interest in assets outside those operations, Csurplus excess cash, Dgroup consolidated debt and NCI outside equity in consolidated subsidiaries. All market values share the same date and currency.

  1. Value the controlled operations in full if the business forecast includes their entire operating cash. Subtract group debt once, including its consolidated subsidiaries' debt.
  2. Subtract the outside shareholders' interest in subsidiary equity. Applying their percentage to operating value and separately deducting the same subsidiary debt can distort the bridge.
  3. Add the fair value of the parent's interest in an excluded associate, plus eligible surplus cash. Reconcile corporate overhead and taxes if divisional valuations omitted them.

When the reasoning holds

  • The NCI valuation must match an equity claim if all subsidiary debt has already been deducted. Different consolidation practices require different bridge rows.
  • A quoted associate stake may attract realization costs or restrictions. Model these if material rather than always treating its gross market quote as distributable cash.

Terms in this lesson
Discounted cash flow (DCF)
A valuation method that brings a series of future cash flows back to a common date.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
First example

Suppose consolidated operations are worth 300. Excess cash of 20 was excluded. Debt is worth 80 and outside owners’ interest in a consolidated subsidiary is worth 15. Parent common equity is 300 + 20 − 80 − 15 = 225.

Define exactly which assets generated operating value

A consolidated group may report all the sales and expenses of a subsidiary it controls even when it owns only part of the shares. If the operating forecast includes 100% of those cash flows, some value belongs to outside owners. That minority claim must be considered before reporting parent common equity.

The reverse issue arises with investments whose dividends or earnings were excluded from operating cash. A wholly owned non-operating asset can add value without appearing in the operating forecast. Trace inclusion from the original cash flows rather than applying a standard checklist blindly.

Evaluate cash, debt and other senior claims

Surplus cash can be added to operating value if the discounted cash flows did not already include it. Cash required for normal operations may already be effectively valued with the business. Cash restricted or trapped can require an appropriate adjustment for timing and accessibility.

Deduct market or estimated fair debt obligations where supplied, including any claims needed to maintain consistency with treatment of interest and leases. Preferred stock, unfunded pensions or employee options may require analysis on their own contractual terms. Avoid counting a liability both in operating cash and again in the bridge.

Think like a buyer of the whole group

A buyer taking over all the operating assets does not automatically keep every euro generated. Debt holders, other equity owners and contractual holders may have their own entitlements. Moving from operating value to one share means explicitly following those entitlements down to the residual owner.

A minority interest balance on a consolidated balance sheet may be an accounting carrying amount, not its economic value today. If consolidated subsidiary cash is valued at market assumptions, a matching fair value for the outside ownership is preferable when available.

Method

Build a sources-and-claims bridge

  1. Perimeter

    Inventory subsidiaries, acquisitions and investments included in forecast operating cash. Mark full versus proportionate consolidation in the case.

  2. Assets and claims

    Add only excluded non-operating assets. Subtract only claims senior to or outside the particular common equity being valued.

  3. Reconciliation

    Rebuild operating value from estimated common equity by reversing the bridge. If the result differs, locate a missed or double-counted item.

Worked case

A parent owns only part of a subsidiary

The operating DCF for a consolidated group is 420 and includes all of a subsidiary worth 100 after its own debt on the supplied basis. Outside shareholders own 30% of the subsidiary. The group operating figure excludes 25 of genuine surplus cash. Consolidated debt is 110 and preferred stock is 10.

Working

  1. The outside owners' equity interest is 30% × 100 = 30. Use their equity claim because all consolidated debt is already deducted separately.
  2. Common equity for the parent is 420 + 25 − 110 − 10 − 30 = 295. Dividing by 25 parent shares gives 11.80 per share.
  3. A proportionate model that excluded the subsidiary minority cash flows from the beginning would require a correspondingly revised bridge. Subtracting the same 30 again would understate parent equity.

Interpretation

The calculation specifies what is valued before and after debt. Applying the minority percentage to 420 would incorrectly treat the outside holders as owners of the entire group.

A common mistake

Subtracting all cash from debt even though some cash is operating working capital or restricted.

How to correct it

Identify the cash balance truly surplus to operating requirements and its accessibility. Match the model's starting value to the amount being added.

Quick check
What if an additional separate investment worth 10 was also excluded from operations and is wholly owned by the parent?

Add the 10 once because it is an owned asset absent from the starting valuation. The other claims remain unchanged.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A group's consolidated operating value is 360. It has non-operating cash of 14, an excluded independent investment worth 8, debt worth 90 and noncontrolling equity worth 18. There are 20 parent shares and no preferred stock.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate parent common equity and share value.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    If an enforceable obligation of 6 was fully included in the forecast operating outflows, how would you treat it in this bridge?

  3. Part C · 3 marks

    How would the implied operating value change if the shares actually traded at 12 and the other bridge items held?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

The bridge is 360 + 14 + 8 − 90 − 18 = 274. Per parent share this is 274/20 = 13.70.

Part B 2 marks

Leave the bridge unchanged for this obligation because operating value already reflects its cash costs. Quantify separately only any residual claim omitted from the forecast.

Part C 3 marks

Market common equity is 240. Implied operating value is 240 − 14 − 8 + 90 + 18 = 326. Compare 326 with the estimated 360 on the same operating basis.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How can you value a group with a controlled subsidiary and a minority associate without subtracting the same outside ownership twice?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would map the legal group to the valuation perimeter. Starting from 100% of controlled operations, I deduct all consolidated debt and then the fair value of outside subsidiary equity. I add the parent's stake in an associate whose operating cash was excluded, with any access adjustment. In the example the operating €350m becomes €266m of parent equity. I would also check duplicated overhead, excess cash and any parent-level tax.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if €24m of associate value is added while its dividends were capitalized in the original group DCF?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Trace the dividend series and remove its present value from the initial operating model, or omit the separate stake add-back if an equivalent complete value is already counted. Partial dividends and complete stake value require reconciliation rather than automatic substitution.
