Value a closely held company when the founder leaves

Normalize owner-managed earnings for an economically equivalent replacement team.

In this lecture

  1. Value an operating business another owner can run
  2. Pick risk evidence and buyer perspective
  3. Price the stake's actual rights
  4. Rebuild the calculation
  5. Separate transferable operations from the buyer's exit rights
  6. A founder-run technical distributor
  7. Independent exam problem
  8. Explain it in an interview
Theory and terminology

Private-company valuation and key-person risk

normalized owner compensation · discount for lack of marketability

What you should be able to explain

  • Normalize owner-managed earnings for an economically equivalent replacement team.
  • Value dependent customer relationships and a specific restriction without charging the same loss twice.

Value an operating business another owner can run

A founder can pay herself a low salary, use the business to meet personal expenses or supply an undocumented relationship with a distributor. A buyer needs a realistic cost for replacing the work and a separately supported view of customer retention. Reversing the founder's salary without hiring anyone implicitly assumes free management.

Treat personal items and one-off costs as adjustments only when records show they are genuine and will cease. Anchor the argument in the company's books, replacement pay benchmarks, contracts and customer interviews. Any operational dependence that survives the normalizations belongs in the earnings scenarios.

Pick risk evidence and buyer perspective

A private firm's shares usually have no long public return history. Build a market-risk estimate from comparable operating businesses, unlevering and relevering when using a cash-flow valuation. A diversified strategic buyer and a founder holding nearly all personal wealth in the business face different risks. State the buyer and reason for the valuation.

A multiple can also be used if earnings, growth, reinvestment, risk and accounting are comparable. If the risk of losing a distributor is modelled with explicit cash-flow probabilities, avoid also reducing the multiple for precisely that same loss without new evidence. The example specifies a common multiple to isolate the earnings impact.

Price the stake's actual rights

A minority holding may lack control, face transfer restrictions or have an enforceable exit right. Start with what the investor can receive and when. A buyer of every share who can sell the whole business has different rights from a restricted individual investor.

A published average private-company haircut is weak evidence for a specific block. A dated exit fee can be valued directly. Limits on the ability to choose when to sell may also matter but require evidence and a valuation method of their own. Identify which effects already enter the chosen multiple or cost of capital.

Method

Build the result

E[EBITDA] = EBITDAₙ − pL ; Vstake = f[m × E[EBITDA] + C − D] − PV(F)

EBITDAₙ is normalized earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization after replacing the owner. p is the stated probability of losing a customer, L the resulting annual contribution loss, m a comparable enterprise multiple, C excess cash, D debt, f the economic ownership fraction and F a distinct holder-paid exit fee. All monetary amounts are in millions.

  1. Bridge the reported EBITDA to the economics of transferable operations before a customer-loss scenario.
  2. Compute distinct ownership states and their weights. A constant multiple in both states makes applying it to expected EBITDA equivalent to probability-weighting their enterprise values.
  3. Bridge each enterprise claim to equity, apply the stake fraction and deduct only the present value of costs specific to that holding.
Worked case

A founder-run technical distributor

Reported EBITDA is €2.40m after €0.18m founder pay, €0.09m verified personal travel and €0.15m exceptional cleanup. A replacement manager costs €0.40m. With 40% probability a departing-founder customer takes €0.50m annual contribution permanently. Assume 6× in either state, €0.8m excess cash, €3.2m debt and a pro-rata 25% holding. Its sole incremental exit restriction in this exercise is a €0.20m holder fee in two years, discounted at a stipulated 10%.

Working

  1. Normalized EBITDA before customer loss is 2.40 + 0.18 − 0.40 + 0.09 + 0.15 = €2.42m.
  2. Surviving and lost-customer states have EBITDA of €2.42m and €1.92m. Their weighted mean is €2.22m. This corresponds to a €0.20m expected annual loss.
  3. Enterprise value is 6 × 2.22 = €13.32m. Add cash and subtract debt for common equity of €10.92m. The unrestricted quarter share is €2.73m.
  4. The fee is worth 0.20/1.10² = €0.16529m today. The modeled restricted stake is €2.56471m. A second percentage haircut for the same fee would reduce value twice.

Decision and interpretation

Use approximately €2.565m under the given contract and scenarios. An actual investor would test the customer probability, replacement manager's effectiveness and support for the multiple before treating this as an offer.

Quick check
Which adjustment belongs at the individual stake level under the worked case?

The first two change operating earnings for all owners. The fee is an incremental obligation of the specific block.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

A services firm reports EBITDA €1.80m after founder salary €0.25m and verified personal expenses €0.10m. Replacement costs €0.40m. A 25% customer-loss chance would remove €0.40m annual contribution. Use 7× in each state, €0.50m cash and €2m debt. Value a 30% block with a €0.12m fee at one year, discounted at 8%.

Reveal the worked solution
  1. Normalize EBITDA to 1.80 + 0.25 − 0.40 + 0.10 = €1.75m. Deduct the expected 0.25 × 0.40 = €0.10m customer loss for €1.65m.
  2. Enterprise value is €11.55m, common equity €10.05m and the pro-rata stake €3.015m.
  3. Present fee is €0.11111m. The restricted block is worth approximately €2.90389m under the supplied assumptions.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

What would you change in a valuation when one founder controls the customer relationships and the shares cannot be sold freely?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would put the founder's actual work back into the budget at a market replacement cost, then model which customers might leave even after that hire. Those are business-wide effects. I would next value the particular stake and its contractual restrictions. An exit fee belongs to that holder. A blanket private-company discount can count effects already captured in the earnings scenarios or peer price twice.

Interviewer’s follow-up

A peer multiple already incorporates an identical mandatory exit fee for an otherwise comparable stake. Should the fee be deducted again?

Compare with the follow-up answer

No. First identify whether the numerator and claimant really include the same fee and rights. Normalize the comparison or use the already-adjusted price. Applying the same fee on both routes double counts the contractual cost.
