Reverse valuation

Work backwards from market price to a growth rate or margin. Challenge the implied assumptions against competitive economics and plausible industry size.

The starting point

Ordinary valuation starts with a forecast and estimates value. Reverse valuation begins with an observed price and asks what growth, margin or return would make that price consistent with a specified model. This can turn a price into a concrete business question.

Fix the other inputs before solving. Many combinations can explain the same price, so an implied growth rate belongs to a set of assumptions. Test it against market size, competitors and the reinvestment needed to achieve it.

Theory and method

Reverse discounted cash flow and implied expectations

Expectations investing · Implied ROE · Valuation sensitivity

An acquisition target's valuation implies either 3% growth or a 15% margin, depending on the analyst. Can both calculations be correct?

A price supplies one equation. A model of a company can contain growth, margin, capital efficiency, discount rate and the duration of competitive advantage. Pinning down one unknown requires fixing the others. Reverse valuation becomes informative when the implied combination is converted into an actionable operating test, such as the required share of a fixed market, the margin after a supplier repricing, or the sustainable return on a regulated bank's equity.

How the result is built

m = V(k−g)/[Sales₁(1−T)(1−g/R)] in a stable operating model

m is the implied operating margin, V the operating value implied by the transaction price, k operating cost of capital, g long-run growth, Sales₁ next-year revenue, T corporate tax and R return on new operating capital. All values use compatible currencies and dates.

  1. Translate the price from shares into an operating value including debt and excluding surplus cash as appropriate. Solve the business equation on that same claim basis.
  2. For V = 180, k = 8%, g = 2%, first-year sales 100, tax 25% and incremental return 10%, required free cash is 180 × 0.06 = 10.8.
  3. A fifth of after-tax profit is reinvested. Therefore operating profit must be 10.8/[0.75 × 0.80] = 18, implying 18% margin. Reinsert that result to check the price.

When the reasoning holds

  • Margin, growth and capital return cannot all be uniquely inferred from one price. Report which are fixed, and run alternate combinations consistent with the same observed value.
  • A stable perpetual model needs sustainable long-run economics. A multistage model may be required if today's margins or market share are far from maturity.

Terms in this lesson
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Discount rate
Rate used to express a future payment in today’s money, reflecting time and the relevant risk.
Perpetuity
A stream of payments assumed to continue indefinitely under the model.
Margin
In business accounts, profit expressed relative to sales. In derivatives trading, margin is collateral required to support a position.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
First example

A business trades at an operating value of 100. Suppose next year’s free cash flow is 5, the discount rate is 9%, and cash thereafter grows at a constant rate. Solving 100 = 5/(0.09 − g) gives g = 4%. That 4% rests on the cash-flow and discount-rate assumptions.

Use an observed price as an equation

A market price represents one total value. A cash-flow model contains many unknowns including sales growth, margins, investment returns and the cost of capital. Reverse valuation holds enough of those inputs fixed to recover the remaining assumption that would reconcile the model with the observed price.

The recovered value is conditional. A high implied margin may instead reflect a lower required return or expected growth in later years. Use the result to identify what the buyer would have to believe under your model, then compare those beliefs with the business.

Choose an economically meaningful unknown

A simple growing-perpetuity inversion with next-year cash C, price V and discount rate r gives implied growth g = r − C/V. It treats C as supplied. If the growth assumption itself changes reinvestment and therefore C, solve a model that links both variables.

Alternatively solve for the operating margin required to generate an implied cash flow. Work from operating value rather than common market capitalization when your cash measure is available to all funders. Convert price per share to total equity and bridge backwards for debt and surplus assets first.

Test the business implication

Translate an implied percentage into concrete operating quantities. What share of a realistically sized market must the company win? How many stores or deliverable units does it need? Will investors need to provide fresh capital to support that scale? Compare results with actual capacity and competitor responses.

Stress one uncertain input at a time and explain why a range arises. A solution with perpetual growth above the discount rate is invalid for the stated perpetuity. A margin beyond feasible unit economics may indicate a wrong perimeter, misplaced terminal assumption or an optimistic market price.

Method

Reverse an operating DCF

  1. Align the price

    Convert observed equity price to an operating value using all included and excluded assets and claims on the same date.

  2. Solve the equation

    Define the unknown, hold the other inputs explicit and rearrange. Reinsert the result into the original expression to verify the price.

  3. Translate and challenge

    Convert the implied input into sales, operating profit, capital needs or market share. Identify alternatives that could produce the same valuation.

Worked case

A retailer's required margin

The observed common market capitalization is 210, debt is 70 and surplus cash is 20. Forecast year-1 sales are 200. The operating discount rate is 9%, stable growth 3%, tax 25% and return on incremental capital 12%. Assume constant margins and no other cash adjustments.

Working

  1. Observed operating value is 210 + 70 − 20 = 260. For g = 3%, required year-1 free cash is 260 × (0.09 − 0.03) = 15.6.
  2. Supporting growth requires reinvestment of 3%/12% = 25% of after-tax operating profit. Free cash is therefore 75% of that profit. Required after-tax operating profit is 15.6/0.75 = 20.8.
  3. Pretax operating profit is 20.8/0.75 = 27.7333. On sales of 200 the implied margin is 13.87%. Substituting this margin into the cash-flow bridge recovers the price.

Interpretation

A 13.87% margin is a business question about achievable pricing, distribution costs and competition. It is conditional on both the growth and the capital required to deliver it.

A common mistake

Inferring the market's single true growth forecast from one quoted share price.

How to correct it

Show the assumptions held fixed and supply an alternative input combination. The observed price alone cannot identify every parameter of a valuation.

Quick check
At the same value of 100 and rate of 9%, what long-run growth is implied if next year’s cash flow is only 4?

Rearrange the model to g = required return − cash flow/value. A smaller initial cash flow requires more future growth to support the same value.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

An equity claim trades for 150. Next year's cash available to shareholders is estimated at 7.5 and grows at a constant rate. The required equity return is 9%. All values are on the same share or total-equity basis.

  1. Part A · 2 marks

    Solve for implied growth and confirm the result.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    If sustainable growth can be only 2%, what next-year cash would support the same price at the same required return?

  3. Part C · 2 marks

    How could operating evidence distinguish the two stories?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 2 marks

g = 0.09 − 7.5/150 = 4%. Substitution gives 7.5/(0.09 − 0.04) = 150.

Part B 3 marks

Cash must be 150 × (0.09 − 0.02) = 10.5. This is 3 higher than the first forecast, or 40% more.

Part C 2 marks

Estimate distributable year-1 cash from margins, reinvestment and financing needs. Test whether growth of 4% or immediate cash of 10.5 is consistent with capacity, competitors and sustainable investment.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

An acquisition target's valuation implies either 3% growth or a 15% margin, depending on the analyst. Can both calculations be correct?

Compare with a spoken answer

Both can be mathematically correct if each holds different assumptions fixed. I would turn the price into an implied operating value and solve for one variable at a time. Here one consistent set requires 18% operating margin. I would then test whether planned stores, unit costs and competitor pricing support that margin and whether new capital can actually earn the assumed return.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If the target's sustainable operating margin is capped at 15% with the base 2% growth, what operating value follows?

Compare with the follow-up answer

After-tax operating profit is 100 × 15% × 0.75 = 11.25. Reinvestment uses 20%, leaving 9 cash. Capitalizing at 8% − 2% gives operating value 150, which is 30 below the proposed 180 value.
