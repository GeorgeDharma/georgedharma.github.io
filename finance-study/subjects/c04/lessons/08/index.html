Banks and financial firms

Debt is part of a bank’s operations, making industrial cash-flow methods awkward. Relate equity earnings and book value to sustainable return on equity.

The starting point

Banks obtain deposits and other funding as part of their core business. Separating interest from operations, as one might for an industrial company, can strip out part of a bank’s economic activity. Analysts therefore often value the common equity directly.

One approach compares the price-to-book ratio with sustainable return on equity (ROE), growth and the return investors require. With stable ROE and growth, consistent payout and accounting, justified price-to-book equals (ROE − growth)/(cost of equity − growth). Growth must remain below the cost of equity.

Theory and method

Justified price-to-book for financial firms

Dividend discount model · Sustainable return on equity · Bank capital retention

A bank trades below common book even though its earnings are positive. Which combination of profitability and capital requirements could justify that?

A bank earning €8 on every €100 of common equity may still need to retain part of the €8 to support its lending book. Shareholders can only receive the amount left after required capital growth. If comparable-risk equity requires 10%, reinvesting at 8% can keep the franchise worth less than its accounting base. A reserve-release gain or an understated loan loss can make one year's reported ROE a poor guide to sustainable profitability.

How the result is built

P/B = (ROE−g)/(kᴱ−g) ; payout capacity = 1 − g/ROE

P is common equity value, B opening common equity, ROE sustainable net income divided by opening equity, g stable growth in that book equity and kᴱ required common return. The payout capacity is a fraction of earnings, given no new equity and clean surplus.

  1. If book equity is 100 and sustainable ROE is 8%, next year's earnings are 8. Book growth of 3% requires retaining 3 and leaves a dividend of 5.
  2. At a 10% equity cost and 3% growth, value is 5/0.07 = 71.43 or 0.7143 times book. The sustainable payout is 5/8 = 62.5%.
  3. If accounting understates expected losses, adjust equity and normalized earnings together. If the capital ratio forces extra retention, model the transition before applying the stable ratio.

When the reasoning holds

  • Payout must be compatible with required capital and the risk-weighted asset path. A statutory minimum can differ from the firm's operating buffer.
  • Current book and ROE must be comparable across banks after credit losses, preferred claims and accounting adjustments. Stable growth must remain below the equity required return.
Terms in this lesson
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
Perpetuity
A stream of payments assumed to continue indefinitely under the model.
How to read the notation
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.

All notation examples

First example

A bank can sustain 12% ROE, grow equity at 4% and faces a 10% cost of equity. Its model price-to-book is (0.12 − 0.04)/(0.10 − 0.04) ≈ 1.33. A premium is possible because the bank is assumed to earn more than shareholders require.

Value the bank from the shareholder's perspective

A retailer borrows to finance stock and stores. A bank collects deposits to fund the loans and securities on which it earns interest, so interest paid and received are central to its operations. Removing deposit funding and interest as though they were incidental industrial financing can produce an uninformative operating-cash measure.

Bank analysts often start from common equity, earnings available to shareholders and capital needed to support the balance sheet. The bank may report a profit but need to retain part of it to replace losses or support asset growth. Dividend capacity can therefore differ from the dividend actually paid.

Derive a stable price-to-book relationship

Suppose beginning book equity is B, sustainable return on equity is ROE, equity grows at g and required equity return is k. Next year's earnings are ROE × B. Retaining g × B supports the specified book growth, leaving a dividend of (ROE − g) × B under the simple clean-surplus assumptions.

A growing dividend perpetuity has equity value equal to next dividend divided by k − g, provided k exceeds g. Dividing by B gives justified price-to-book of (ROE − g)/(k − g). An ROE above k can support a premium to book when the assumed returns persist.

Interpret book equity with care

Loan-loss provisions, asset measurement, goodwill and unrecognized credit deterioration can distort reported book value or near-term ROE. A low price-to-book might represent expected losses rather than a cheap franchise. Tangible common equity or normalized earnings sometimes provide a useful additional view if used consistently.

If credit conditions are temporarily excellent, current ROE can exceed its sustainable level. A valuation should allow for normal provisioning, costs, capital needs and the effect of competition. Using an optimistic growth rate alongside high dividend payout can imply retention insufficient to support the balance sheet.

Method

Model the retained capital

  1. Opening capital

    Identify reported common book equity, any clearly justified adjustments and sustainable earnings relative to that adjusted base.

  2. Retention

    Calculate the equity increase needed for growth and the implied portion of profits retained. Treat regulatory or risk limits as constraints if specified.

  3. Distribution and value

    Value sustainable distributions at the matched cost of common equity. Compare implied price-to-book with ROE relative to that cost.

Worked case

A lender retains equity to grow

A bank begins with common equity of 200. Sustainable ROE is 13%, book equity can grow 4% each year, and the required equity return is 10%. Assume clean surplus, stable ROE, feasible regulatory capital and no issuance.

Working

  1. Next year's earnings are 200 × 13% = 26. Increasing equity by 4% requires retaining 8, leaving a dividend capacity of 18.
  2. The equity value is 18/(0.10 − 0.04) = 300. The implied price-to-book is 300/200 = 1.5, matching (0.13 − 0.04)/(0.10 − 0.04).
  3. If sustainable ROE falls to 10% and all else holds, earnings are 20, dividend is 12 and value becomes 200. The original premium relied on sustained excess return.

Interpretation

The model does not claim that every booked asset can be sold for book value today. It values measured equity through future earnings and distributions.

A common mistake

Applying an industrial EV/EBITDA multiple to a bank after adding its customer deposits to ordinary financial debt.

How to correct it

Analyse how deposit funding, credit losses and retained capital support banking earnings. A common-equity comparison can preserve those economic relationships.

Quick check
Keeping required return at 10% and growth at 4%, what ratio follows if sustainable ROE falls to 10%?

Numerator and denominator both become 6%. The model values the bank at book when sustainable ROE equals the required return under these assumptions.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A bank has opening common book equity of 150. Sustainable ROE is 9%, expected book growth 3%, and common equity costs 11%. Assume clean surplus and no capital injections.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate expected earnings, retained equity and dividend capacity.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Determine equity value and price-to-book.

  3. Part C · 2 marks

    Explain why simply accelerating book growth could destroy value here.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Earnings are 13.5. Required retained equity is 4.5, leaving 9 for distribution under the stated growth plan.

Part B 3 marks

Equity is 9/(0.11 − 0.03) = 112.5. Price-to-book is 112.5/150 = 0.75. Sustainable ROE is below the cost of equity.

Part C 2 marks

Additional capital earns 9% while investors require 11%. Retaining more for the same below-cost opportunities can reduce the value of distributions. Reassess incremental returns before assuming growth deserves a premium.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A bank trades below common book even though its earnings are positive. Which combination of profitability and capital requirements could justify that?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Positive earnings only show that the bank covers accounting expenses. Here it earns 8 on 100 of equity while investors require 10, and book growth needs 3 retained. Distributable cash is 5 and value is 71.43, below book. I would examine normalized credit loss, deposit economics and the capital needed for growth. A distressed realization or a capital shortfall could require a separate scenario.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If the bank raises target book growth to 4% while its sustainable ROE remains 8%, what happens?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The bank retains 4 on opening equity of 100 and distributes 4. At a 10% required return the value becomes 4/(0.10−0.04) = 66.67. More growth at below-hurdle returns transfers cash into low-return investments and lowers value in this model.
