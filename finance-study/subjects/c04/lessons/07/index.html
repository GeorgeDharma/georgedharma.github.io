Trading comparables

Put companies on a common accounting and time basis. Similar multiples can conceal different growth prospects or risk.

The starting point

A valuation multiple compares a price with a measure such as earnings, sales or operating profit. The numerator and denominator must belong to matching claimants. For example, price-to-earnings uses common equity value and earnings available to common shareholders.

Choose peers with similar economics and put them on the same period and accounting basis. Different growth, reinvestment or risk can justify different multiples. A low multiple can signal weak prospects or an accounting difference, so a peer figure is a starting hypothesis.

Theory and method

Fundamental multiples and comparable-company valuation

Justified forward EV/sales · Peer normalization · Relative valuation

One peer trades at twice another's EV/sales. Which measurable differences could justify both prices?

Two retailers can each sell €100 of goods and generate radically different cash for their investors. One keeps a larger operating margin, pays less tax, needs less new capital for growth or has lower systematic risk. A sales multiple silently embeds all these assumptions. Deriving a benchmark multiple from discounted cash flow gives an analyst a way to challenge peer selection and to decide whether a simple median makes economic sense.

How the result is built

EV/Sales₁ = m(1−T)(1−g/R)/(k−g)

EV is operating enterprise value, Sales₁ next year's revenue, m its operating profit margin, T tax rate, g sustainable annual growth, R incremental after-tax operating return and k operating discount rate. This is a stable-growth forward sales multiple.

  1. Multiply next year's sales by the operating margin and by one minus tax to obtain after-tax operating profit.
  2. Only a fraction 1−g/R remains free after funding growth. Capitalize that free cash at k−g for a stable perpetuity.
  3. Divide the resulting EV by next-year sales. With T = 25%, g = 3%, R = 12% and k = 9%, a 16% margin gives 1.50× while an 8% margin gives 0.75×.

When the reasoning holds

  • The derivation assumes stable margins, growth, risk and reinvestment. A high-growth peer with changing economics needs a multistage interpretation.
  • Use forward sales with the forward formula and consistent accounting perimeters. Gross agency revenue and net commission revenue are often economically incomparable.
Terms in this lesson
Discounted cash flow (DCF)
A valuation method that brings a series of future cash flows back to a common date.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
First example

Suppose two comparable manufacturers trade at operating values of 180 and 240, with forecast operating profits of 18 and 30. Their multiples are 10× and 8×. Applying 9× to a target’s comparable forecast operating profit of 12 implies operating value of 108 before adjusting debt or cash.

Match the multiple to the financial claim

A price-to-earnings multiple compares common equity value with earnings available to ordinary shareholders. An operating-value-to-operating-profit multiple compares all funders' claim on operations with earnings before interest. Combining equity price with operating profit can make companies with different debt loads look deceptively cheap or expensive.

Write units beside each input. A multiple of eight means that eight units of value are paid for each unit of the specified earnings, sales or cash metric. A sales multiple can be calculated for a loss-making company, but its meaning depends heavily on attainable margins and capital needs.

Normalize the comparison before ranking peers

Ensure that all companies use the same earnings period. Trailing earnings refer to a completed period while forward earnings depend on forecasts. Remove one-off effects only with a clear reason and make equivalent accounting adjustments for all firms. Persistent restructuring charges may be part of normal economics.

A peer with a lower multiple might have heavier reinvestment, lower growth, higher exposure to a customer or less predictable margins. Select peers for economic comparability, explain genuine differences, and consider both the median and the spread rather than selecting the most convenient company.

Challenge a multiple with underlying economics

A durable high return on new capital can support a high multiple because future growth needs relatively little cash investment. A heavily leveraged equity can have a low P/E due to risk even when its operating business is not cheap. Relative prices can also be broadly inflated or depressed across an entire peer set.

Check whether earnings are close to zero or negative. Dividing by a tiny number can create an enormous ratio with little interpretive value, and a negative multiple ranks badly as a value signal. A DCF or a normalized operating measure can help assess what the observed ratio implies.

Method

Apply a peer range

  1. Choose the metric

    Write down the numerator, denominator, period, currency treatment and exceptional items. Explain why the two sides belong to matching claimants.

  2. Derive peers

    Make the same adjustments to each peer. Compute individual multiples and summarize a range without hiding important differences.

  3. Value the target

    Apply the chosen multiple to comparable target earnings, then bridge to equity if using operating value. Stress a weaker but credible peer multiple.

Worked case

Two peer observations and a nonrecurring loss

A target expects operating profit of 15 after a documented one-time closure cost of 3. Matched peer multiples of normalized next-year operating profit are 8× and 10×. Assume the target is genuinely comparable, with surplus cash 6, debt 40 and no other claims.

Working

  1. Comparable operating profit is 15 + 3 = 18. The midpoint 9× implies operating value of 18 × 9 = 162.
  2. Estimated common equity is 162 + 6 − 40 = 128. The peer range gives operating value from 144 to 180 and common equity from 110 to 146.
  3. If closures recur and the 3 add-back is unwarranted, the same midpoint gives operating value 135 and equity 101. The normalization assumption affects equity by 27.

Interpretation

A thoughtful comparison explains why the closure is exceptional and why the peer range fits the target's economics. A median cannot correct incomparable accounting inputs.

A common mistake

Adding back a one-off charge only for the target while using peer earnings that include comparable charges.

How to correct it

Restate both target and peer denominators on the same basis and run the case without the discretionary adjustment.

Quick check
Using the same peer assumption, what operating value follows if the target’s normalized forecast operating profit is 10?

Multiply the comparable normalized profit of 10 by 9. Changing the profit basis without changing the input would break comparability.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

Two peer companies have enterprise values of 180 and 300 and normalized forecast EBIT of 20 and 25. A target has comparable EBIT of 16, debt of 50 and surplus cash of 8.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate the peer multiples and the midpoint operating estimate.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Calculate midpoint equity and the equity range across these peers.

  3. Part C · 2 marks

    Name one reason the target could deserve a multiple below the range.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

The peers trade at 180/20 = 9× and 300/25 = 12×. Midpoint is 10.5×. Applied to target EBIT of 16 this gives operating value of 168.

Part B 3 marks

Midpoint equity is 168 − 50 + 8 = 126. At 9× it is 144 − 50 + 8 = 102 and at 12× it is 192 − 50 + 8 = 150.

Part C 2 marks

For example, a concentrated customer contract facing renewal could reduce expected earnings durability or raise systematic risk. Explain how the fact changes cash flows or discounting rather than assuming a low ratio alone implies a bargain.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

One peer trades at twice another's EV/sales. Which measurable differences could justify both prices?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would compare the conversion of an extra euro of sales into sustainable distributable operating cash. At an 8% margin the case economics justify 0.75 times sales. At 16% margin, holding tax, growth, reinvestment and risk fixed, they justify 1.50 times. I would normalize revenue recognition and check whether margins persist before applying the peer midpoint to the target.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The target reports double the revenue because it records pass-through supplier billings gross. Could its EV/sales look cheap with identical economics?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Yes. If gross revenue doubles while operating profit and operating cash are unchanged, its reported sales multiple halves. Restate revenue to a comparable basis or compare normalized operating earnings and cash before interpreting the multiple.
