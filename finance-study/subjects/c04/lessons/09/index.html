Company and Equity Valuation
Core finance / Lesson 9 of 13

Residual income

Start with book equity and add the value of future profits above the equity charge, under consistent accounting and payout assumptions.

The starting point

A profitable business may still earn less than shareholders require. Residual income subtracts an equity charge from accounting net income. The charge is the opening book value of common equity multiplied by its required return for the period.

Under clean-surplus accounting, ending book equity equals opening book equity plus comprehensive earnings captured by the model, less distributions, allowing for owner contributions. With a consistent forecast and terminal treatment, value is opening book equity plus the present value of future residual income.

Theory and method

Residual income valuation and clean surplus

Abnormal earnings valuation · Edwards–Bell–Ohlson model · Equity charge

Derive why equity value can equal book value plus the present value of excess earnings, and identify the boundary condition.

A shareholder can wait for a dividend or leave earnings in the company. Retention increases next period's opening equity and therefore the capital on which shareholders expect a return. Substituting this book-value roll-forward into a dividend valuation separates the starting book from earnings above the required charge on that book. It is possible for all residual income beyond the forecast to be zero while the firm remains profitable and worth its continuing book capital.

How the result is built

V₀ = B₀ + Σₜ RIₜ/(1+k)ᵗ + (Vₙ−Bₙ)/(1+k)ⁿ ; RIₜ = NIₜ − kBₜ₋₁

B is common book equity, V common equity value, NI earnings attributable to common, RI residual income, k required equity return and n the last explicit date. The finite sum covers dates 1 through n. The final premium is continuing value in excess of book.

  1. Start with clean surplus Bₜ = Bₜ₋₁ + NIₜ − Divₜ for a firm with no new share issuance or repurchases. Rearrange to express dividend from earnings and change in book.
  2. Insert dividends into the finite discounted-dividend formula. Terms in beginning and ending book cancel across years, leaving opening book and dated earnings minus k times opening book.
  3. At the final date keep the remaining premium Vₙ−Bₙ. If future ROE equals k under consistent assumptions that premium vanishes. If excess returns persist it must be modeled.

When the reasoning holds

  • Earnings and book must capture all relevant value changes consistently. Items bypassing earnings, issuance or repurchases require proper adjustments to the clean-surplus roll-forward.
  • An infinite-horizon form needs an appropriate terminal or transversality condition. Dropping a material final excess-value term understates equity.
Terms in this lesson
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
Benchmark
A reference chosen in advance for comparison, such as a market index or an equal-weight portfolio.
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
How to read the notation
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.

All notation examples

First example

Opening equity is 100, next year’s net income is 14, and shareholders require 10%. The equity charge is 10 and residual income is 4. If all later residual income is zero, equity value today is 100 + 4/1.10 = 103.64.

Charge equity capital for its opportunity cost

Accounting net income deducts interest on debt but does not normally charge a cost for the shareholders' opening capital. Residual income calculates earnings remaining after that charge. For a period, take net income attributable to common shareholders and subtract the opening common book equity multiplied by its required return.

A business can report positive profit but negative residual income. Investors may be better off elsewhere if the return on the capital committed is below the risk-appropriate benchmark. The charge is an economic comparison rather than a payment the company actually sends shareholders.

Connect residual income with dividends

Clean surplus links closing book equity with opening equity, earnings and distributions, adjusting for owner contributions where relevant. In a simplified company with no new shares, closing book equals opening book plus earnings minus dividends. Rearranging expresses dividends in terms of earnings and changes in book.

Substitute this expression into a dividend valuation and discount across periods. The book-value changes telescope, leaving current book equity plus the present value of future residual income under consistent accounting and terminal assumptions. It is a different way to describe the same economic claim.

Forecast the charge on a moving base

Use opening book equity for each year's charge. Retained earnings increase the book capital on which shareholders require a return in subsequent years. Starting every year from the original base can inflate future residual income. If earnings bypass the assumed income measure, reconcile the clean-surplus relationship before applying the model.

The terminal assumption matters. If competition erodes return on equity to the required return, subsequent residual income becomes zero even though profit continues. Persistent excess returns instead require a continuing residual-income value. Explain why a forecast supports either outcome.

Method

Construct a residual-income timeline

  1. Roll forward equity

    Start from common book equity, add forecast earnings and deduct distributions. Identify any issuance, buyback or accounting item that requires an adjustment.

  2. Calculate charges

    Multiply each year's opening equity by the corresponding required equity return. Subtract this amount from common earnings.

  3. Value excess earnings

    Discount the resulting signed residual incomes and add opening book once. Evaluate residual income after the forecast rather than quietly assuming it vanishes.

Worked case

A two-year earnings franchise

Opening common equity is 100 and the equity cost is 10%. Year 1 net income is 16 and dividends are 6. Year 2 income is 15 and dividends 7. After year 2 the firm earns exactly the required return. There are no owner contributions or bypass entries.

Working

  1. Year 1 equity charge is 10 so residual income is 6. Closing book is 100 + 16 − 6 = 110. Year 2 charge is consequently 11 and residual income is 4.
  2. After year 2 residual income is zero. Value today is 100 + 6/1.10 + 4/1.10² = 108.76.
  3. A dividend cross-check gives end-year-2 book equity of 110 + 15 − 7 = 118. With zero future excess return, year-2 equity value equals 118. Discounting dividends of 6 and 7 plus that 118 yields the same 108.76.

Interpretation

The terminal 118 is needed in the dividend cross-check because dividends after year 2 have value. Adding another 118 to the residual-income valuation would count book equity twice.

A common mistake

Interpreting zero residual income as no future profits or no terminal business value.

How to correct it

If return on equity matches its cost, profit exactly covers the equity charge. Future claims may still be worth book value even though future excess earnings add zero.

Quick check
If next year’s net income is 8 instead, with all later residual income still zero, what is equity value today?

Residual income becomes 8 − 10 = −2. Subtracting the discounted shortfall from book equity gives 100 − 2/1.10 ≈ 98.18.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

Opening book equity is 80, equity costs 12%, year 1 earnings are 12 with dividends of 4, and year 2 earnings are 8.8. All residual income after year 2 is zero.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find opening book values and residual incomes for both years.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Calculate present equity value.

  3. Part C · 2 marks

    If an exceptional loss bypasses forecast earnings but reduces closing equity, what should the analyst do?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Year 1 begins at 80, its charge is 9.6 and residual income is 2.4. Year 2 starts at 80 + 12 − 4 = 88, giving a charge of 10.56 and residual income of −1.76.

Part B 3 marks

Value equals 80 + 2.4/1.12 − 1.76/1.12² = approximately 80.74. The positive first-year excess more than offsets the discounted second-year shortfall.

Part C 2 marks

Reconcile the changed book base with an income measure covering the loss or otherwise adjust the model consistently. Applying the unmodified clean-surplus equation would leave an unexplained movement.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Derive why equity value can equal book value plus the present value of excess earnings, and identify the boundary condition.

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would begin with opening common book and charge shareholders' required return against that capital each year. Clean surplus lets me replace dividends with earnings minus the increase in book. The resulting terms cancel through time and leave opening book, discounted residual incomes and a discounted continuing premium above terminal book. In the example that €11 terminal premium matters. Calling future earnings positive is insufficient to decide it.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if €4 of losses bypass ordinary net income but reduce book equity this year?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Reconcile the exact equity roll-forward and use a consistent comprehensive earnings measure or an explicit adjustment for the bypassed loss. Ignoring it produces incorrect residual income and a false equivalence with dividends.
