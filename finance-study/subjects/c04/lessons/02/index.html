Operating cash flows

Convert after-tax operating profit into cash available to all capital providers through reinvestment and working-capital adjustments.

The starting point

Free cash flow to the firm is cash generated by operations after operational reinvestment and before payments to lenders or shareholders. Start with operating profit after the taxes a debt-free business would pay. Then add back noncash expenses and subtract actual investment.

Depreciation lowers accounting profit without using current cash. New equipment and a rise in operating working capital do use cash. For example, selling on credit can increase profit while leaving money tied up in unpaid customer invoices.

Theory and method

Free cash flow to the firm and to equity

FCFF–FCFE bridge · Net borrowing · Operating cash conversion

A forecast shows €35m of free cash to operations and €47m to shareholders in the same year. Is that necessarily a model error?

Shareholders can receive operating cash after the lenders' share, plus money raised from new debt that exceeds principal repayments. If a company borrows to expand leverage, cash available to shareholders for one year can exceed cash generated by operations. This can change financing risk and future debt service. Reconcile operations and financing date by date before extrapolating a large distribution into terminal value.

How the result is built

FCFE = FCFF − interest × (1−T) + net borrowing

FCFE is free cash flow to common equity, FCFF cash after operating tax and reinvestment before financing, T the usable marginal tax rate, and net borrowing new debt proceeds less principal repaid during the period. Both cash flows use the same currency and dates.

  1. Start with operating taxes as if no interest were deducted. When moving to actual equity cash, deduction-eligible interest reduces those taxes by T times interest.
  2. Subtract the actual interest outflow, add back its usable tax saving, then add debt issued less principal repaid.
  3. With FCFF 35, interest 4, tax 25% and net new debt 15, FCFE = 35 − 3 + 15 = 47. A repeat would require another 15 of new net borrowing.

When the reasoning holds

  • The formula assumes no preferred claim, separate nonoperating cash distributions or other financial flows. Add these explicitly when present.
  • Only actual usable interest deductions belong in the after-tax interest adjustment. If tax losses defer relief, model timing rather than taking the statutory rate automatically.

Terms in this lesson
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
Working capital
Money tied up in day-to-day operations. For a deal, agree which receivables, stock and payables count.
Revenue
Amount earned by selling goods or services. The customer may pay later.
Receivable
Money a customer owes for a sale already made.
First example

Operating profit is 60, the tax rate 25%, depreciation 8, equipment spending 16 and the rise in operating working capital 5. Free cash flow is 60 × 0.75 + 8 − 16 − 5 = 32. Interest is excluded because lenders are among the claimants on this cash.

Convert operating profit into distributable operating cash

A sale made on credit can create reported revenue before the customer pays. Equipment bought today can use cash long before its depreciation is recorded. Free cash flow to the firm reconciles these timing differences to measure cash available to all capital providers after operating reinvestment.

Begin with operating profit before financing charges. Calculate operating taxes on a consistent unlevered basis using the case assumptions. Add back depreciation and other relevant noncash charges already deducted. Subtract actual capital expenditure and the increase in noncash operating working capital.

Rebuild operating working capital

Operating working capital can include trade receivables and inventory less trade payables and other comparable operating liabilities. Ordinarily exclude debt, interest-bearing financing, and surplus cash from this operating balance. A supplier granting the business a longer credit period can temporarily free cash even if sales and profit are unchanged.

Use the change between opening and closing balances. A positive operating working-capital change absorbs funds, since more resources are tied up. A decrease releases funds. Working-capital improvements may reverse if customers leave or suppliers demand quicker payment, so avoid projecting one-off releases forever.

Identify reinvestment without double counting

If you start with after-tax operating profit after depreciation, add depreciation back then subtract gross asset spending. Equivalently subtract net capital expenditure, defined as gross capital expenditure minus depreciation, without also adding depreciation. Keep acquisitions and disposals on the same operating perimeter as the earnings forecast.

A company can show positive operating cash while consuming value if it postpones necessary maintenance. Sustainable free cash flow depends on realistic capital replacement and customer credit policies. Compare assets, sales growth and operations with the reinvestment assumptions behind the model.

Method

Build the FCFF bridge

  1. Unlevered earnings

    Identify EBIT, meaning earnings before interest and tax. Multiply by one minus the operating tax rate where the problem uses that simplification.

  2. Fixed investment

    Add back eligible noncash depreciation then deduct the capital purchases actually paid. Treat gross and net expenditure conventions consistently.

  3. Working investment

    Compute opening and closing noncash operating working capital, deduct its increase and explain any release. Confirm interest has not entered twice.

Worked case

A furniture wholesaler grows on credit

Operating profit after depreciation but before tax is 90. Tax on operating profit is 25%. Depreciation is 12 and capital spending 25. Receivables rise 6, inventory rises 8 and supplier payables rise 5. Ignore other changes.

Working

  1. After-tax operating profit is 90 × 0.75 = 67.5. Add depreciation and deduct capital spending, leaving 67.5 + 12 − 25 = 54.5.
  2. The increase in operating working capital equals 6 + 8 − 5 = 9. This uses cash because customers and inventory absorb more than suppliers finance.
  3. Free cash flow to the firm is 54.5 − 9 = 45.5. The equivalent shortcut is 67.5 − (25 − 12) − 9 = 45.5.

Interpretation

A financing decision about paying down existing debt comes after this operating calculation. It affects what shareholders can receive rather than the amount generated before capital-provider payments.

A common mistake

Subtracting the full closing working-capital balance each year.

How to correct it

Include the initial commitment when a project starts. In subsequent years use only the change relative to the prior balance.

Test the assumptions in the interactive model

Quick check
If working capital instead falls by 2, with the other inputs unchanged, what is free cash flow?

The fall releases 2 of cash. The calculation becomes 45 + 8 − 16 + 2 = 39.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

A distributor has EBIT 72, depreciation 9 and capital spending 20. The operating tax rate is 25%. Opening receivables, inventory and payables are 18, 22 and 16. Closing amounts are 24, 20 and 19.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate working capital and its change.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Calculate annual FCFF.

  3. Part C · 2 marks

    A manager reduces maintenance spending by 4. What changes now and what must a valuation check?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Opening working capital is 18 + 22 − 16 = 24. Closing working capital is 24 + 20 − 19 = 25. The increase of 1 absorbs cash.

Part B 2 marks

After-tax operating profit is 72 × 0.75 = 54. FCFF is 54 + 9 − 20 − 1 = 42.

Part C 2 marks

Current FCFF rises from 42 to 46 if everything else is unchanged. Check whether deferred maintenance will require later spending or reduce capacity and earnings. One year's cash improvement need not raise intrinsic value.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A forecast shows €35m of free cash to operations and €47m to shareholders in the same year. Is that necessarily a model error?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would reconcile interest, the tax saving and net borrowing. Here €35m minus €3m after-tax interest plus €15m in new net debt gives €47m to common equity. I would next check whether the financing plan supports this leverage and what happens to interest and principal later. Projecting €47m forever without modeling continued borrowing could badly overstate sustainable distributions.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If the company must repay €10m net next year while FCFF and interest remain at the example levels, what is FCFE?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Net borrowing becomes minus €10m. FCFE is €35m − €3m − €10m = €22m. The €25m swing from the prior €47m is entirely due to the new net financing flow under the stated unchanged operating assumptions.
