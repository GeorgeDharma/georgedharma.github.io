Growth and reinvestment

Higher sustainable growth generally requires more reinvestment or better returns on new capital. Check that forecast margins and capital needs agree.

The starting point

A business can grow by putting more capital into productive activities or by earning a higher return on the capital it adds. Under stable conditions, expected growth in operating profit is approximately the reinvestment rate times the return earned on new investment.

The reinvestment rate is the share of after-tax operating profit committed to additional business assets. Reinvestment reduces near-term free cash flow. It creates value when the new assets earn more than investors require for their risk. Sales growth alone cannot establish that.

Theory and method

Fundamental growth and incremental return on invested capital

Reinvestment rate · Marginal ROIC · Value of growth opportunities

Two companies want 5% operating-profit growth. Why does an analyst care whether the next euro invested earns 10% or 20%?

A grocer can raise after-tax operating earnings by €5m with €25m of extra capital if new investment earns 20%. If a rival earns 10%, it needs €50m to generate the same additional profit. Both reports may show 5% growth while leaving very different cash for investors. The expected return on the next store or warehouse governs the reinvestment required; a historic return on an older, more attractive estate may be misleading.

How the result is built

g = b × R ; FCFF = NOPAT × (1−g/R) in the steady operating model

NOPAT is after-tax operating profit, b the share reinvested, R the prospective after-tax return on new operating capital, g sustainable growth and FCFF free cash available to operating capital providers. Percentages apply over the same period.

  1. Additional investment equals b times operating profit. Multiplying by R gives the next period's additional operating income.
  2. Divide by current operating profit to get the sustainable profit growth rate bR. Solving for investment gives b = g/R.
  3. At NOPAT 100 and g 5%, an R of 10% requires 50 reinvestment and leaves 50 free. At R of 20%, investment falls to 25 and free cash rises to 75.

When the reasoning holds

  • The timing assumes today's investment earns its full expected return in the next period and the firm has reached stable margins. Long construction ramps need an explicit forecast.
  • The return used is incremental and risk-matched. A high return on a fully depreciated legacy asset can have little relevance to new capital.

Terms in this lesson
Weighted average cost of capital (WACC)
A blend of the required returns on debt and equity, weighted by how much each supplies. It can be used to discount cash flows available to both.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Working capital
Money tied up in day-to-day operations. For a deal, agree which receivables, stock and payables count.
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
First example

A business earns 100 after operating taxes, reinvests 30 and expects a 12% return on that new capital. The added profit is 30 × 0.12 = 3.6, or 3.6% growth on the original 100. Cash left for capital providers is 70.

Connect additional profit with additional capital

Investing 30 of this year's after-tax operating profit into equipment and working capital creates extra earnings only if the added capital is productive. The return on incremental invested capital compares next period's increase in operating profit with this period's additional investment under the simple steady model.

If after-tax operating profit is E and a fraction b is reinvested at an incremental return R, next period's extra profit is E × b × R. Dividing by E gives the growth rate g = b × R. This relation links a forecast of earnings growth to an explicit financing need.

Separate prospective returns from historical averages

A company might have earned 25% on early patents but achieve only 8% on new expansion. The old average return does not establish the return available at the margin. Competition, required distribution spending and asset intensity can all reduce the return on later projects.

Growth can also come temporarily from higher margins or better use of assets already in place. A mature forecast cannot rely forever on rising utilization or margin. Separate the transitional improvement from the new capital needed once the business reaches a stable operating level.

Check whether growth creates value

If the incremental return exceeds the required return for the incremental investment's risk, reinvestment can generate economic surplus. When the returns are equal, a faster-growing business can redistribute cash across time without increasing the present value created by each extra investment.

Use the same asset and tax basis on both sides of the comparison. Compare after-tax unlevered operating return on new assets with a suitable operating cost of capital. Comparing return on levered common equity with a business WACC mixes claimant groups.

Method

Build the financing side of a growth forecast

  1. Start from operating earnings

    Use after-tax operating profit as the base when modelling operating assets. Identify how much is distributed and how much is retained for reinvestment.

  2. Derive capital needs

    Divide desired sustainable growth by return on new capital. Multiply the resulting reinvestment rate by the earnings base.

  3. Test feasibility

    Check whether the required fraction, capital spending and operating working capital fit the forecast. Explain how returns might fall as new competitors enter.

Worked case

An expanding distribution network

A distributor earns 80 after operating tax, targets 6% sustainable growth, and expects 15% on new investment. Management suggests annual reinvestment of only 20. The required operating return for these projects is 10%.

Working

  1. Desired extra profit next year is 80 × 0.06 = 4.8. At a 15% return the required new capital is 4.8/0.15 = 32.
  2. The implied reinvestment fraction is 32/80 = 40% and the remaining operating cash is 48. Reinvesting only 20 would produce 3 extra profit and growth of 3/80 = 3.75%.
  3. On the 32 invested, annual incremental operating profit of 4.8 exceeds a one-period required capital charge of 3.2 by 1.6. A full project valuation also needs the persistence and timing of those returns.

Interpretation

The spreadsheet cannot simultaneously maintain the 6% growth, 15% marginal return and spending of only 20 without identifying another source of improvement.

A common mistake

Assuming a higher growth rate always raises value while holding available cash fixed.

How to correct it

Tie growth to reinvestment, then revalue the cash remaining. A higher growth assumption can lower value if the new investments earn too little.

Quick check
If new capital earns 10%, what share of profit must be reinvested to sustain 4% growth under this model?

Solve 4% = reinvestment rate × 10%. The result is 40%, leaving 60% of after-tax operating profit available before other adjustments.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

An operator earns 120 after operating taxes. New investment can earn 8%, and the risk-appropriate operating capital cost is 10%. It wants sustainable profit growth of 4%.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate the required reinvestment rate, amount and resulting free cash.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Calculate incremental annual operating profit and its first-year economic spread against required return.

  3. Part C · 2 marks

    Identify a business assumption that could change the conclusion.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

The reinvestment rate is 4%/8% = 50%. Invest 60 and retain free cash of 60 under the simple model.

Part B 2 marks

New profit is 60 × 8% = 4.8. The required annual charge is 60 × 10% = 6, leaving a negative 1.2 spread.

Part C 2 marks

If distinct projects have a higher prospective after-tax return, lower relevant risk or valuable additional cash flows, reevaluate them. A high historical return on legacy assets alone does not establish a higher return on the next 60.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Two companies want 5% operating-profit growth. Why does an analyst care whether the next euro invested earns 10% or 20%?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would forecast the cash cost of growth. At a 10% incremental return, €5m of extra operating earnings requires €50m of new investment. At 20%, only €25m is tied up. If the market return required on these investments is 12%, one company is growing through below-hurdle investments and the other through profitable opportunities. I would check store-level capital and mature margins before extrapolating either.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Could a company temporarily grow without this much new capital?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Yes. Filling idle capacity, improving utilization or restoring margins can raise earnings on existing assets for a period. I would model that transition directly and establish when the business must again fund ordinary capacity expansion.
