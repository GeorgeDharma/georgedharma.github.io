Value at risk

Use a quantile to find a loss threshold at a stated confidence level. Record the estimation window, method and treatment of ties.

The starting point

Imagine sorting daily trading losses from the smallest to the largest. Value at risk, or VaR, marks a chosen point in that ordered list. A one-day 95% VaR of €40,000 says that the model puts 95% of daily losses at or below that threshold.

An estimate from past observations depends on the window, portfolio and rule for choosing a percentile. Under a nearest-rank rule, multiply the number of observations by the confidence level, round upward and select that ranked loss. Extreme losses beyond the threshold can be much larger.

Theory and method

Variance–covariance value at risk

Delta-normal VaR · Gaussian quantile

What information is lost when a desk reports the sum of standalone normal VaRs as its total risk?

Two business lines can lose money on different days. Their total loss varies with their individual volatilities and how the losses move together. A normal approximation uses the variance of the combined loss and then takes the chosen normal percentile. Adding two standalone estimates effectively assumes perfect positive co-movement when the means and confidence levels align. The approximation is a starting point for linear market exposures and can materially miss option curvature or heavy tails.

How the result is built

VaR꜀ = μ + z꜀√(σ₁² + σ₂² + 2ρσ₁σ₂)

c is confidence, μ expected total loss in euros over the chosen horizon, z꜀ the matching unit-normal quantile, σ₁ and σ₂ the lines' euro loss standard deviations, and ρ their correlation.

  1. For zero-mean one-day losses with €2m and €1m standard deviations, standalone variances contribute 4 + 1 in squared millions.
  2. At correlation 0.25 the covariance term adds 2 × 0.25 × 2 × 1 = 1. Combined standard deviation is √6 = €2.449m.
  3. Multiply by the 95% one-sided normal quantile 1.645 for VaR about €4.03m. Standalone VaRs sum to €4.935m.

When the reasoning holds

  • The combined distribution must be approximately normal at the chosen horizon. Skewed option or default losses can produce very different tail quantiles.
  • Estimated covariance must remain informative for today's holdings. Correlations calculated on calm markets may understate exposure in a joint sell-off.
Terms in this lesson
Value at risk (VaR)
For a chosen horizon and probability, a threshold in the loss distribution. Larger losses remain possible.
Risk factor
A common source of variation in returns, such as market movements or a change in interest rates.
Monte Carlo simulation
Estimating an outcome by generating many possible paths and summarizing the results.
Correlation
A measure of how closely two series move together in a linear pattern.
Diversification
Combining exposures that do not always move together to reduce dependence on a single outcome.
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
How to read the notation
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.

All notation examples

First example

With 100 equally weighted daily losses, the 95th ordered loss is €40,000. If the largest is €300,000, the historical 95% VaR is still €40,000 under this rule. The largest outcome matters for other measures of exposure.

The meaning of a quantile

A daily loss distribution includes ordinary gains, small losses and rare large losses. At confidence c, define VaR as the smallest threshold whose cumulative probability is at least c. This definition remains meaningful when many outcomes repeat, as in a credit portfolio with a substantial chance of zero defaults.

VaR does not report the largest possible loss or average loss beyond the threshold. Its value can move abruptly when a small change in scenario weights moves an outcome across the selected probability. Reporting the underlying distribution makes that jump understandable.

Three ways to estimate the threshold

Historical simulation applies past movements of risk factors to today's holdings, revalues each scenario, then ranks the losses. Parametric methods assume a probability distribution and estimate its parameters. Monte Carlo simulation draws hypothetical risk-factor changes from a model and reprices the holdings.

Historical windows can omit a crisis. Normal-distribution approximations can underrepresent asymmetry in option portfolios. More simulated scenarios can reduce numerical sampling noise but cannot repair a wrong dependence model or omitted risk factor. The method is part of the result.

Why diversification needs care

For approximately normal linear losses, correlations allow partial offsets. But VaR need not reward diversification for every discontinuous loss distribution. A position that rarely defaults can have zero VaR at one threshold, while combining two independent positions can make at least one default sufficiently likely to cross that threshold.

Use a confidence level and holding period consistent with the actual decision. Scaling a one-day VaR by the square root of days relies on restrictive assumptions about independent increments, stable volatility and position behaviour.

Method

Constructing a weighted VaR

  1. Define the loss sign

    Put all trade outcomes into the same currency and horizon, with larger numbers representing worse results.

  2. Accumulate probabilities

    Sort losses from low to high and add each scenario's probability, retaining its exact weight.

  3. Find the first crossing

    Select the first loss at which cumulative probability reaches the selected confidence. State the quantile rule and inspect the more severe scenarios.

Worked case

Why two small loans can cross a threshold

Two €100 loans each independently default with 4% probability over a year, with total loss on default. Ignore interest. The loss on one loan is zero with probability 96%, so its 95% VaR is zero.

Working

  1. For both loans together, the probability of no default is 0.96² = 92.16%. At least one default occurs with probability 7.84%, pushing the combined 95% threshold above zero.
  2. Exactly one default has probability 2 × 0.04 × 0.96 = 7.68%. Cumulative probability through a €100 loss reaches 99.84%, so portfolio 95% VaR is €100.
  3. The €200 outcome remains possible with probability 0.16%. The example describes a discontinuous quantile. It does not imply that independence increases the default probability of either loan.

Interpretation

Adding two individually reported VaRs would yield zero and miss the combined threshold. Reprice and aggregate whole portfolio scenarios before extracting a quantile.

A common mistake

Selecting a ranked observation without respecting scenario probability can put the threshold in the wrong place.

How to correct it

Rank losses and cumulate their actual weights. Equal scenario counts are valid probability weights only when the scenarios are equally likely.

Quick check
A desk orders 200 daily losses. Under the nearest-rank rule, the 190th is €70,000, the 191st is €73,000 and the maximum is €410,000. What is its 95% VaR?

The rank is 200 × 0.95 = 190. The maximum records one observed extreme and the 191st observation sits above the selected threshold.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A portfolio has losses of €0m with 94% probability, €2m with 5% and €9m with 1%. Use the smallest loss whose cumulative probability reaches the chosen level.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find 95% and 99% VaR and explain the tie at 99%.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Move two percentage points of probability from the zero-loss state to the €9m state. Recalculate 99% VaR.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

The cumulative probabilities are 94%, 99% and 100%. The first crossing is €2m at both confidence levels, including exactly 99%.

Part B 3 marks

Probabilities become 92%, 5% and 3%. Cumulative probability at €2m is now 97%, below 99%, so the new 99% VaR is €9m.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

What information is lost when a desk reports the sum of standalone normal VaRs as its total risk?

Compare with a spoken answer

The standalone sum corresponds to both lines moving together with correlation one under the normal assumption. Our estimated correlation of 0.25 gives a combined €4.03m VaR instead of €4.94m. I would report both the diversification implied by the data and a high-correlation stress. I would also inspect full revaluation for material options before relying on the normal approximation.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Suppose the expected total daily loss is €0.2m while covariance is unchanged. What happens to 95% VaR?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The entire normal loss distribution shifts upward €0.2m. The 95% threshold becomes approximately €4.23m at correlation 0.25. This adjustment adds a loss mean in the same units and horizon as the volatility.
