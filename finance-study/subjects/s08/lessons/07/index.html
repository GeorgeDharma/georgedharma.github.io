Stress testing

Choose internally coherent shocks and revalue both assets and liabilities. Reverse stress tests ask what would have to happen to breach a specified limit.

The starting point

A stress test asks what a defined bad event would do to the whole institution. Specify the economic story, reprice affected assets and liabilities, and include immediate cash demands. A fall in collateral prices may create a margin call even when an offsetting contract gains value.

Reverse stress testing starts at the other end. Select a threshold such as minimum cash, then solve for the smallest shock in a stated scenario that reaches it. Seek combinations that could produce the breach, including feasible management responses and their timing.

Theory and method

Reverse stress testing with a liquidation feedback

Fire-sale externality · Break-even shock

At what initial shock does selling assets to meet margin itself exhaust the cash buffer?

A fund must sell assets when margin rises. Selling a large quantity into a thin market depresses prices, which generates further margin outflow. An ordinary one-round stress misses this second demand for cash. A reverse test starts at the money the fund can afford to lose and solves backward for the initial margin call that produces that total after repeated rounds. The feedback coefficient is a stated scenario assumption that should be challenged using actual market depth and collateral terms.

How the result is built

T = A + kT = A/(1 − k); A* = H(1 − k)

T is total cash demand after all rounds, A the initial call, k the incremental call generated per euro of liquidation-related demand, H available euro headroom, A* initial call reaching the threshold, with 0 ≤ k < 1.

  1. An initial €3m call triggers another €1.2m at k = 0.4, then €0.48m, then smaller calls.
  2. The geometric sequence sums to 3/(1 − 0.4) = €5m. Equivalently all secondary calls together equal k times total demand.
  3. With €6m headroom the initial threshold is 6 × 0.6 = €3.6m. The first call consumes only 60% of the cash headroom when total feedback eventually uses all of it.

When the reasoning holds

  • The feedback coefficient is constant and below one for the exercise. At or above one this stylized infinite-round sum does not converge.
  • The same positions and margin rules must actually connect sales to additional calls. If sales immediately reduce exposure enough to offset pressure, k can be smaller.
Terms in this lesson
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
Margin
In business accounts, profit expressed relative to sales. In derivatives trading, margin is collateral required to support a position.
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
Percentage point
The difference between two percentages. A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises one percentage point.
First example

A portfolio has €5m cash and must retain €2m overnight. In a scenario that calls €0.6m of additional collateral for each 1% price decline, a 5% decline uses its €3m headroom. A bigger decline would breach the minimum.

Historical, hypothetical and reverse tests

A historical stress replays a past pattern of risk-factor changes on today's balance sheet. A hypothetical stress constructs a relevant new pattern, such as a drought, inflation shock and currency depreciation. Exposure composition and market depth can make a familiar episode behave differently today.

A reverse stress defines failure first and works backward to combinations that produce it. This reveals assumptions that have to fail together, including the ability to refinance or sell assets. It identifies vulnerability without attaching an unsupported probability to the scenario.

First and second rounds

A first-round effect is the immediate change in value or cash under the shocks. A second-round effect follows from responses, such as other firms selling similar assets or customers withdrawing after losses become visible. Specify the sequence and do not count the same fire-sale loss twice.

Test both sides of the balance sheet. A decline in long-term yields can raise the value of an insurer's bond assets but raise its longer-duration liabilities further. A favourable asset movement alone can therefore conceal a deterioration in capital.

Can management actually respond?

A static balance-sheet assumption holds positions fixed to reveal the initial exposure. A dynamic test includes decisions such as cancelling dividends or raising funds. An action should have a date, legal authority, realistic capacity and a named condition under which it works.

Use sensitivity ranges for key assumptions rather than a single heroic forecast. A proposed asset sale and a secured borrowing against the same asset are competing responses. Also separate actions controlled by management from support that requires an outside party's consent.

Method

Constructing a reverse loss boundary

  1. Define failure

    Name the metric and the precise boundary, including whether reaching or falling below the limit counts as a breach.

  2. Translate economic shocks

    Revalue entries under the specified responses and add cash demands with the correct sign and timing.

  3. Solve and challenge

    Solve for combinations at the boundary. Inspect the most plausible combinations and what evidence would give early warning.

Worked case

Two shocks reach a capital floor

An institution has €14m capital and must retain €8m. A credit shock costs €0.8m per percentage point of defaults, while a separate collateral-price loss channel costs €0.5m per percentage point of decline. Assume linear losses solely for this exercise.

Working

  1. Headroom is €6m. With x points of additional defaults and y points of collateral decline, the boundary is 0.8x + 0.5y = 6, in millions.
  2. At x = 5 the credit loss is €4m, leaving €2m capacity. A further four-point collateral decline reaches the floor. A larger decline breaches it.
  3. The two shocks may reflect the same recession. Before applying the result to a live book, check whether the two estimated loss channels include overlapping collateral losses on defaulted loans.

Interpretation

Mapping combinations is more informative than presenting one isolated percentage. It shows which movements would change the decision to reduce exposure or secure funding.

A common mistake

Adding unrelated worst values from different historical dates can create an incoherent scenario or double-count the same shock.

How to correct it

Explain relative directions and timing and retain a distinct sensitivity table for individual factors.

Quick check
Cash is €7m, the operating minimum is €3m, and the scenario creates €0.5m outflow per percentage point of decline. At what decline is the minimum reached?

The available headroom is €4m. Dividing by €0.5m per percentage point gives eight percentage points. The result depends on the assumed outflow schedule.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A firm has €9m liquidity with a €3m floor. Each one-point currency decline triggers €0.75m margin outflow, and losing a particular customer creates an immediate €1.5m outflow.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    With the customer loss, what currency decline reaches the floor?

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    A manager proposes a €2m asset sale taking three days. Can it protect against a next-morning breach? Specify evidence needed for an alternative.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

There is €6m headroom. After the €1.5m loss, €4.5m remains. Dividing by €0.75m gives six percentage points.

Part B 3 marks

The sale does not settle in time. Verify a truly available same-day line, its unused amount, collateral and contractual cutoff or arrange an agreed payment delay before the due time.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

At what initial shock does selling assets to meet margin itself exhaust the cash buffer?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would distinguish the initial shock from its own amplification. With forty cents of extra calls per euro across rounds, total demand is the initial call divided by 0.6. That means an initial €3.6m call reaches our €6m buffer. I would show the sensitivity to k and test the validity of our liquidation-depth estimate before adopting a hard limit.

Interviewer’s follow-up

A precommitted line adds €2m before the first call but the stressed feedback rises to 0.6. Does the tolerable initial shock improve?

Compare with the follow-up answer

New headroom is €8m and the threshold is 8 × 0.4 = €3.2m. That is below the original €3.6m. Extra funding helps, but a stronger assumed feedback more than offsets it in this scenario.
