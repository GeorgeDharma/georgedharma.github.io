Rating migration, credit revaluation and portfolio loss

Convert a credit rating transition row into a probability distribution of horizon values.

In this lecture

  1. Value every surviving rating

  3. Move from a single borrower to a portfolio
  4. Rebuild the calculation
  5. Surviving downgrades explain nearly half the expected credit loss
  6. A one-year risk review of a small corporate bond
  7. Independent exam problem

Theory and terminology

Credit migration and mark-to-market loss

rating transition matrix · credit revaluation

What you should be able to explain

  • Convert a credit rating transition row into a probability distribution of horizon values.
  • Separate migration loss from default loss and calculate a two-year default probability.

Value every surviving rating

A debt issuer can continue paying and still become less creditworthy. If investors then demand more return, promised future payments are discounted more heavily and a traded bond loses value. A migration model considers rating improvement, downgrade, an unchanged rating and default.

A transition matrix assigns probabilities to next year’s ratings conditional on the rating today. Each row totals one. For a defined one-year horizon, determine each surviving bond’s price using the spread appropriate to its end rating. Default gets its own recovery value. Align coupon income and benchmark carry across all outcomes.

Set a meaningful comparison value

A risk officer may compare each horizon value with its unchanged-rating value. The result isolates credit repricing under the assumptions. This is an internal mark-to-market exercise. The accounting charge for a particular banking-book asset can follow different recognition rules.

Historical transition frequencies describe one possible physical distribution of changes. Market discount rates set prices conditional on each state. These are different tasks. Inserting pricing-implied default probabilities into a forecast without justification changes the interpretation.

Move from a single borrower to a portfolio

Multiplying a transition matrix by itself gives two-period path probabilities if transitions depend only on the current state and the same matrix applies in both years. A borrower that defaulted in year one stays in default in the model.

Two issuers may have identical individual transition rows and still have very different joint losses. Common exposure to energy prices or a recession can make downgrades cluster. Combining individual risk measures requires additional dependence assumptions or explicitly specified joint scenarios.

Method

Build the result

Expected horizon credit loss = Σⱼ pⱼ(B − Vⱼ). Two-year transition probability from i to k = Σⱼ PᵢⱼPⱼₖ.

B is the unchanged-rating horizon benchmark, Vⱼ the horizon value in end state j, both currency per bond. pⱼ is the current transition probability into j. Pᵢⱼ is a one-year probability from rating i to j. The sums run over all mutually exclusive states.

  1. Set a common date and include the same coupon convention in every horizon value.
  2. Subtract each state value from the benchmark, order the losses and weight each by its own transition probability.
  3. For two years, multiply the probabilities along each possible intermediate path and add the path probabilities. Consistency requires including the already-defaulted path.
Worked case

A one-year risk review of a small corporate bond

The unchanged-rating benchmark is €100 per bond. Starting strong, next year it stays strong with 80% probability, becomes weak with 17%, or defaults with 3%. Horizon values including all carry are €100, €90 and €40. From weak, next year’s probabilities are 10% strong, 75% weak and 15% default. Default is absorbing.

Working

  1. The three horizon losses are €0, €10 and €60. A bond that survives weak still loses €10 relative to the reference.
  2. Expected value is 0.80 × 100 + 0.17 × 90 + 0.03 × 40 = €96.50. Expected loss is €3.50, comprising €1.70 downgrade and €1.80 default effects.
  3. The 95% VaR is €10 because cumulative probability reaches 97% there. The worst 5% combines all 3% default states and 2% of downgrade states, giving (0.03 × 60 + 0.02 × 10)/0.05 = €40 expected shortfall.
  4. Two-year default probability is 0.80 × 0.03 + 0.17 × 0.15 + 0.03 × 1 = 7.95%. A simple doubling of the initial 3% ignores the changed prospects of the downgraded group.

Decision and interpretation

Include spread and migration risk in the limit review. The €40 tail mean is conditional on the stated probabilities and recovery, so a recession test should also change both.

Quick check
A bond is worth €100 if its rating is unchanged, €92 if downgraded, and €40 in default. These states have probabilities 85%, 12% and 3%. What is expected loss relative to €100?

Surviving downgrades contribute 0.12 × €8 = €0.96 and defaults contribute 0.03 × €60 = €1.80. Together they contribute €2.76.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

A different bond has an unchanged-rating benchmark of €100. At the one-year horizon it has 90% probability of €100, 8% probability of €85 and 2% probability of default recovery of €30, all including coupon handling. Calculate expected loss, 95% VaR and 95% expected shortfall.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked solution
  1. State losses are €0, €15 and €70. The mean is 0.08 × 15 + 0.02 × 70 = €2.60.
  2. The cumulative probability is 90% at €0 and 98% at €15, so 95% VaR is €15.
  3. The worst 5% consists of 2% at €70 and 3% at €15. Its average is (1.40 + 0.45)/0.05 = €37.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How can a bond book generate a material credit loss even when every issuer still pays on schedule?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

A surviving bond falls in value if a weaker rating makes the market demand a higher yield. In the worked transition row the surviving downgrade loses €10 and occurs 17% of the time, adding €1.70 to expected loss for each bond. Default adds €1.80. I would use both in a traded-book risk distribution and model how downgrades cluster across issuers.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Could a two-bond portfolio simply double the €37 tail mean?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Exactly doubling is appropriate if the two identical positions lose the same amounts in the same states, as for two units of one bond. Distinct issuers need a joint distribution. Dependence determines how frequently both names are in their worst states together.
