Operational loss frequency, severity and insurance

Calculate expected annual loss and dispersion from event count and per-event severity.

In this lecture

  1. Count events separately from their monetary size
  2. Aggregate a random number of losses
  3. Read the insurance layer
  4. Rebuild the calculation
  5. Apply the insurance contract before averaging
  6. Payment exceptions insured per occurrence
  7. Independent exam problem
  8. Explain it in an interview
Theory and terminology

Frequency–severity models of operational risk

insurance deductible · loss aggregation

What you should be able to explain

  • Calculate expected annual loss and dispersion from event count and per-event severity.
  • Compare a retained-loss distribution with the premium, cash timing and coverage exclusions of insurance.

Count events separately from their monetary size

A failed payment process can cause many small errors or one large loss. A frequency model specifies the annual number of incidents. Severity specifies the loss whenever an incident occurs. Distinguishing the two identifies which operating control could change the answer.

A stronger approval step may prevent errors and lower frequency. A smaller transfer-authority cap may reduce severity. Recording only losses over a threshold hides small incidents, while combining several incidents caused by one outage can double-count the frequency of the same event.

Aggregate a random number of losses

A Poisson count is a simple model with mean and variance both equal to an annual event rate. With independent severities and an independent count, annual expected loss is rate times mean severity. Annual dispersion includes uncertainty about event size and about how many events occur.

Shared outages or a newly introduced payment product can break independence or change the event rate. A loss distribution helps a bank plan internal controls, reserves and insurance. Deriving a regulatory capital requirement requires the rules applicable to the institution, jurisdiction and reporting date.

Read the insurance layer

A deductible is the amount retained before protection starts. A policy limit caps the insurer’s payment. Apply both to each incident if the policy is written per occurrence, then aggregate retained losses. A policy may instead have an annual deductible, an aggregate limit or exclusions.

Premium is a known expense. Recoveries may arrive after the original outflow. A bank can prefer a policy that reduces severe losses even at a cost above expected payouts, but the risk appetite, liquidity timing and incremental premium should be visible to the committee.

Method

Build the result

E[L] = λE[X]. Var(L) = λE[X²] for a compound Poisson model. Insurer payout per event = min(limit, max(0, X − deductible)).

L is total annual loss. X is one event’s loss, λ the annual mean number of events, E the probability-weighted average and Var the variance. X, the deductible and limit use the same currency. E[X²] averages squared event losses.

  1. Compute event mean and second moment from all severity states before multiplying by frequency.
  2. Use the rate times the mean for annual expected loss and the rate times the second moment for annual variance. The square root restores currency units.
  3. Apply the insurance contract to each loss amount and repeat the mean calculation with retained severity. Compare expected savings with the annual premium.
Worked case

Payment exceptions insured per occurrence

A bank expects 3 independent incidents per year under a Poisson model. Each costs €10,000 with 90% probability or €110,000 with 10%. A policy pays losses above €20,000 up to €50,000 per incident, with unlimited eligible occurrences. Annual premium is €18,000. Ignore discounting and exclusions in the calculation.

Working

  1. In €thousands, mean severity is 0.90 × 10 + 0.10 × 110 = 20. Annual expected gross loss is 3 × 20 = €60,000.
  2. The event second moment is 0.90 × 10² + 0.10 × 110² = 1,300 squared thousands. Annual variance is 3,900 squared thousands, giving a standard deviation of about €62,450.
  3. The policy pays zero on a €10,000 event and €50,000 on a €110,000 event. Retained severity is consequently €10,000 or €60,000. Its mean is €15,000 and expected annual retained loss is €45,000.
  4. Expected total annual cost with the policy is €45,000 + €18,000 = €63,000. It costs €3,000 in excess of expected recoveries but removes €50,000 from every eligible large event.

Decision and interpretation

Present the €3,000 expected extra cost alongside the tail relief and a schedule for prefunding claims. A loss of €200,000 would still leave €150,000 on the bank after the capped payout.

Quick check
An incident costs €80,000. Insurance pays the portion above a €15,000 deductible, capped at €40,000 per event. How much loss does the bank retain?

The loss above deductible is €65,000, but the insurer pays only €40,000. The bank retains €80,000 − €40,000 = €40,000.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

A different operation expects two independent events a year. Severity is €5,000 with 80% probability and €55,000 with 20%. Insurance covers loss above €10,000 up to €30,000 per event and costs €9,000 annually. Compute the expected cost change and the gross annual loss standard deviation under a Poisson count.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked solution
  1. In €thousands, mean severity is 0.8 × 5 + 0.2 × 55 = 15, so annual gross loss averages €30,000.
  2. Retained severities are €5,000 and €25,000. Their mean is €9,000. Annual retained cost plus premium is €18,000 + €9,000 = €27,000, a €3,000 expected saving.
  3. The squared-severity mean is 0.8 × 25 + 0.2 × 3,025 = 625 squared thousands. Annual variance is 1,250 squared thousands and standard deviation is about €35,355.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How do event prevention, lower severity and capped insurance affect a bank’s annual payment-error loss in different ways?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would distinguish how often incidents occur, what each costs and which losses a contract transfers. The payment bank’s annual expected gross loss is €60,000. Its policy brings retained loss down to €45,000 but costs €18,000. The additional €3,000 buys a reduction in the size of severe events. A preventive control that reduces their frequency would change a different input.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The insurer limits total annual payouts to €30,000. Can you keep the previous €12,000 expected annual gross recovery?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The simple product of rate and per-event payout overstates recovery if two large events occur. Under independent Poisson marking, large-event count has mean 2 × 0.20 = 0.40. Only the first generates a payout. Expected recovery is €30,000 × (1 − exp(−0.40)), about €9,890 before timing and exclusions.
