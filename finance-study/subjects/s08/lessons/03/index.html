Expected shortfall

Average outcomes in the worst specified fraction. Two portfolios can have equal value at risk and very different losses beyond that point.

The starting point

Two portfolios can have the same loss threshold and very different disasters. Expected shortfall asks how large losses are on average in the worst chosen fraction of outcomes. It adds information about the depth of the tail that a single percentile cannot supply.

With equally likely scenarios, take the losses comprising the worst fraction and average them. When that fraction cuts through a tied outcome, include only enough of its probability to fill the tail. Counting every tied observation can change the intended tail size.

Theory and method

Coherent measures of risk and expected shortfall

Artzner–Delbaen–Eber–Heath coherence axioms · Tail mean

Why can expected shortfall reward diversification even when the separate desks have identical tail losses?

Two desks each have their own worst market. If those markets occur at different times, the worst combined outcomes can be smaller than the sum of the two standalone worst outcomes. Coherence includes a subadditivity requirement that formalizes this intuition. For expected shortfall, the same selected slice of scenarios must be applied to the combined book. Adding separately chosen worst slices lets each desk select a different disaster, implicitly constructing a joint event that may never occur.

How the result is built

ES꜀(X + Y) ≤ ES꜀(X) + ES꜀(Y)

X and Y are random monetary losses over the same horizon, c is confidence, ES꜀ the probability-weighted mean of the worst fraction 1 − c, and ≤ denotes an upper bound.

  1. Consider four equally likely states. Desk A losses are €8m, €8m, zero, zero. Desk B loses zero, zero, €6m, €6m in those same states.
  2. At 50% confidence take the worst two states. Standalone ES values are €8m and €6m, giving a €14m sum.
  3. Combined losses are €8m, €8m, €6m, €6m. The common worst two produce €8m ES, leaving €6m measured diversification.

When the reasoning holds

  • Both desks need the same confidence, horizon and joint scenarios for a valid comparison. Unmatched historical dates can create artificial offsets.
  • Losses must have a finite tail mean. Extremely heavy-tailed models and a tiny sample make the estimate unstable even when the theoretical property holds.
Terms in this lesson
Value at risk (VaR)
For a chosen horizon and probability, a threshold in the loss distribution. Larger losses remain possible.
Expected shortfall
The average loss in a specified worst fraction of outcomes.
Diversification
Combining exposures that do not always move together to reduce dependence on a single outcome.
First example

Two books each show a 95th-ranked loss of €10,000 in 100 scenarios. The worst five for one are all €12,000. For the other they are four at €12,000 and one at €72,000. Their 95% expected shortfalls are €12,000 and €24,000.

Averaging a fixed slice of the distribution

Expected shortfall at confidence c is the average loss in the worst 1 − c portion by probability. For continuous distributions this can often be expressed as a conditional tail mean. For discrete distributions a tied observation may straddle the boundary and must be fractionally included.

Order from worst down, fill the desired probability mass and divide the weighted loss by that mass. Extreme outcomes receive weights proportional to their probabilities within the tail. A severe but very rare loss can matter materially even when it lies beyond the VaR cutoff.

What changes when portfolios combine

Expected shortfall on combined positions is generally no larger than the sum of separate expected shortfalls for the same definition and joint distribution with finite losses. Intuitively, the combined portfolio's worst scenarios need not coincide with each standalone position's worst scenarios.

The amount of diversification depends on joint outcomes. Estimating each book's losses independently and pairing the worst observations by rank silently imposes a very strong dependence assumption. Use synchronized historical shocks or a justified common scenario model.

Uncertainty about the tail

At 97.5% confidence, a dataset of 200 equally weighted observations contributes only five full observations to a historical tail estimate. Adding ten years of calm prices can change the weights without introducing examples of the crisis being studied.

Check how much one observation influences the answer, how portfolios were revalued in that scenario and whether missing instruments were proxied. A scenario beyond observed history can complement the estimate without pretending its probability is known.

Method

Computing a discrete tail mean

  1. Set the tail mass

    Convert confidence to the remaining probability and arrange losses from largest to smallest.

  2. Fill the mass exactly

    Include all of the highest-loss probabilities until the boundary, then only the portion needed from the next outcome.

  3. Normalize the sum

    Divide total probability-weighted loss within the selected slice by its probability. Report the most influential scenario.

Worked case

A cyber outage changes a reserve

A payment firm models daily losses of €0m with 92%, €3m with 6% and €15m with 2% probability. Its risk committee requests both 95% VaR and 95% expected shortfall.

Working

  1. Cumulative probability through €0m is 92% and through €3m is 98%. The 95% VaR is €3m.
  2. The worst 5% contains all 2% of the €15m state and only 3% of the €3m state. Expected shortfall is (0.02 × 15 + 0.03 × 3)/0.05 = €7.8m.
  3. If the extreme state doubles in size but retains its probability, VaR remains €3m. Expected shortfall becomes (0.02 × 30 + 0.03 × 3)/0.05 = €13.8m.

Interpretation

The larger expected shortfall reflects a revised severity assumption. It does not establish how much cash the firm legally has to hold or how quickly the loss would settle.

A common mistake

Averaging every outcome equal to or above VaR may accidentally average much more than the worst 5%.

How to correct it

Include precisely the tail mass, with partial weight on a tied boundary if necessary. Explain how ranks and weights were handled.

Quick check
In 100 equally weighted scenarios the five largest losses, in thousands, are 15, 15, 20, 30 and 70. What is the 95% expected shortfall?

The worst five sum to 150 thousand. Dividing by five gives 30 thousand. Both moderate tail outcomes and the largest loss enter the average.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

Loss is zero with 90% probability, €4m with 8%, and €20m with 2%. Use the worst 5% for expected shortfall.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate 95% VaR and expected shortfall.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    If the €20m loss becomes €25m, explain each measure's response without changing probabilities.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

VaR is €4m. The tail contains 2% at €20m and 3% at €4m. Expected shortfall is (0.40 + 0.12)/0.05 = €10.4m.

Part B 3 marks

VaR stays at €4m. Expected shortfall rises by 0.02 × €5m/0.05 = €2m to €12.4m.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why can expected shortfall reward diversification even when the separate desks have identical tail losses?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would calculate risk on synchronized portfolio scenarios. Here expected shortfall at 50% is €8m for A and €6m for B but only €8m jointly, since the bad states do not coincide. Subadditivity allows that reduction. The other standard coherence properties are monotonicity, positive homogeneity and cash translation. Adding a certain euro loss, for example, increases the loss measure by that euro.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if desk B's €6m losses move into the same two states as A's €8m losses?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The combined losses become €14m, €14m, zero and zero. Joint 50% ES rises to €14m and equals the standalone sum. The distinction comes entirely from dependence because neither desk's own loss distribution changed.
