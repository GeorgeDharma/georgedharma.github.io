Credit and counterparty risk

Separate expected credit loss from variability around it. For a derivative, future exposure and counterparty credit quality can worsen together.

The starting point

On a loan, a bank can lose the part of its exposure it fails to recover if the borrower defaults. A simple expected loss multiplies the estimated probability of default by exposure at default and the loss fraction in that event. Losses in an actual year can differ sharply from that average.

With a derivative, the amount owed may change tomorrow. Wrong-way risk arises when exposure tends to increase exactly when the counterparty's ability to pay weakens. Assuming average exposure and default are independent may understate losses.

Theory and method

Unilateral credit valuation adjustment

CVA · Discounted expected counterparty loss

A derivative has substantial exposure in year two. Should a two-year cumulative default probability be multiplied by both annual exposures?

A supplier owes a bank on a contract only when the contract's replacement value is positive for the bank. A counterparty default can occur once. One valuation approach allocates losses to the time intervals in which a first default occurs, discounts the losses and adds them. Using cumulative default probability twice counts first-year defaults again in year two. The resulting adjustment is especially sensitive when exposure tends to be large precisely in the states when the counterparty is weak.

How the result is built

CVA ≈ Σₜ Dₜ × LGDₜ × EEₜ × ΔPDₜ

Dₜ is the discount factor to today, LGDₜ loss fraction at default, EEₜ positive exposure in euros for interval t, ΔPDₜ unconditional probability of first default in that interval, and Σₜ sums intervals. For pricing, use market-consistent risk-neutral inputs.

  1. Cumulative default probabilities are 2% by year one and 5% by year two, so increments are 2% and 3%. Assume exposure independent of default timing within each bucket.
  2. With €3m and €5m exposure, 60% severity and discount factors 0.98 and 0.95, first contribution is 0.98 × 0.60 × €3m × 0.02 = €35,280.
  3. Second contribution is 0.95 × 0.60 × €5m × 0.03 = €85,500. Total unilateral CVA is €120,780.

When the reasoning holds

  • Use conditional exposure given default when exposure and default are dependent. A simple unconditional expected exposure can materially understate wrong-way risk.
  • This unilateral case ignores the bank's own default and assumes specified netting, collateral and recovery are already reflected. Different enforceable terms change the exposure.
Terms in this lesson
Counterparty
The other party to a financial contract or trade.
Expected credit loss
For a simple single-period example, default probability multiplied by exposure and the share lost if default occurs.
Derivative
A contract whose value depends on something else, such as a currency, an interest rate or a share price.
Probability of default (PD)
The chance that the borrower meets the specified default definition over the stated horizon.
Exposure at default (EAD)
Amount at risk when a borrower defaults. It can include draws on a previously unused facility.
Diversification
Combining exposures that do not always move together to reduce dependence on a single outcome.
First example

For a €1m loan, a 2% default probability and a 40% loss conditional on default imply €8,000 expected loss over the stated year. If a default occurs, the corresponding €400,000 loss is much larger than the average.

Expected loss and capital for surprises

Credit analysis separates the probability that a borrower fails, exposure outstanding if it fails, and the fraction of that exposure lost after recovery. With one fixed exposure and a specified conditional loss fraction, expected loss is their product.

A portfolio can have modest expected loss but substantial uncertainty because defaults cluster in downturns. An expected-loss allowance answers a different question from a buffer for severe outcomes. Model the distribution of correlated losses rather than assuming diversification from the number of names alone.

A derivative's replacement value

When a derivative counterparty defaults, the surviving party may lose the cost of replacing a favourable contract. A contract worth less than zero to the survivor normally creates no positive current credit exposure in that direction. Values can change before default or during collateral closeout.

Enforceable netting allows eligible positive and negative values with the same counterparty to offset within a netting set. Collateral may reduce unsecured exposure, subject to haircuts, timing and legal terms. Separate counterparties cannot be netted merely because their names appear in the same trading book.

Dependence and wrong-way exposure

For uncertain exposures, calculate the expected product of default, exposure and loss severity under their joint distribution. Multiplying unconditional averages implicitly assumes away important dependence or requires suitably conditional inputs.

A collateral security issued by the counterparty itself may also lose value when that party weakens. Simulate collateral and the contract together, including time between the last margin call and closeout. A low observed exposure today says little about peak future exposure.

Method

Building a counterparty loss table

  1. Identify legal sets

    Group contracts by eligible master agreement and counterparty and check what collateral is actually available.

  2. Calculate positive exposure

    Revalue each state, net eligible contracts, subtract realizable collateral and floor unsecured repayment exposure at zero.

  3. Weight conditional losses

    Apply default probabilities and recovery assumptions appropriate to each state, then aggregate their probability-weighted contributions.

Worked case

When averaging understates risk

A market is calm with 80% probability and stressed with 20%. A derivative's unsecured exposure is €1m in calm conditions and €5m in stress. Conditional default probabilities are 1% and 10%. Loss on default is 60% of exposure.

Working

  1. The calm contribution is 0.80 × 0.01 × €1m × 0.60 = €4,800. The stress contribution is 0.20 × 0.10 × €5m × 0.60 = €60,000.
  2. Total expected loss is €64,800. Multiplying unconditional averages would use €1.8m exposure and 2.8% default probability, giving €30,240 after severity.
  3. The €34,560 difference arises because high exposure and high default probability occur together. An increase in diversification across unrelated names would not by itself change this particular contract's conditional dependence.

Interpretation

Sensitivity tests should vary both the stress frequency and the exposure conditional on stress. The probabilities here are teaching assumptions rather than estimates of a specific counterparty.

A common mistake

Taking a single net portfolio value across unrelated counterparties can hide the person who actually owes money.

How to correct it

Calculate replacement value within each enforceable netting set, then add the positive exposures for the report.

Quick check
A fund has a contract with an oil producer that owes the fund more when oil prices fall. The producer also becomes less able to pay when oil falls. Which risk is present?

The contract becomes more valuable to the fund in the states where collecting that value becomes less likely. A test should model prices and the producer's credit condition together.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

Two contracts with bank A are worth +€7m and −€3m to a firm. Bank B owes the firm €2m. A provides €1m realizable collateral, B none. Assume enforceable netting within bank A only.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find total unsecured current exposure.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    If A and B have respective default probabilities 2% and 4%, both with 50% loss given default, estimate total one-period loss on fixed exposures.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Bank A exposure is max(€7m − €3m − €1m, 0) = €3m. Bank B adds €2m. Total unsecured exposure is €5m.

Part B 3 marks

A contributes €3m × 2% × 50% = €30,000. B contributes €2m × 4% × 50% = €40,000. Expected losses add to €70,000 without needing independence.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A derivative has substantial exposure in year two. Should a two-year cumulative default probability be multiplied by both annual exposures?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would use increments. The two-year 5% cumulative figure includes the first year's 2%, so the second interval contributes 3% probability of first default. Discounting positive exposure times severity gives €35,280 and €85,500, a €120,780 unilateral adjustment. For a commodity contract with a distressed producer I would also examine whether exposure conditional on its default exceeds the unconditional average.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The expected exposure in the second interval conditional on default is €7m instead of €5m. What is the revised adjustment?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The second contribution becomes 0.95 × 0.60 × €7m × 0.03 = €119,700. Keeping €35,280 in year one produces €154,980 total. The difference comes from dependence between exposure and default, with default probabilities unchanged.
