Contribution and break-even

Contribution is revenue left after variable costs. Compare it with fixed costs and identify how sales mix affects a multiproduct break-even point.

The starting point

A café sells a sandwich for €8 and spends €3 on ingredients and packaging for each sale. The €5 left is its contribution per sandwich. That money must cover the rent, salaries and other fixed costs before the café earns a profit.

Break-even volume equals fixed costs divided by contribution per unit if price and unit cost stay constant. When products earn different contributions, calculate the contribution of a bundle reflecting the expected sales mix. A change in that mix changes break-even volume.

Theory and method

Degree of operating leverage

Cost–volume–profit analysis · Margin of safety

Why does a 10% drop in volume cut profit by 50% for one manufacturer?

A bicycle shop earns €30 on a sale after paying the unit's variable cost. That money first covers the rent and salaried staff. Once those costs are covered, every further euro of contribution reaches operating profit. A small volume change therefore represents a much larger fraction of profit when sales are only slightly above break-even. The degree of operating leverage compares the entire contribution base with the thin remainder after fixed costs and explains that sensitivity.

How the result is built

DOL = Qc/(Qc − F), and Δπ/π = DOL × ΔQ/Q

Q is units sold, c contribution in euros per unit, F fixed cost in euros, π operating profit in euros, Δ a change from the starting volume and DOL the unitless degree of operating leverage.

  1. Start with profit π = Qc − F. If volume falls by ten units and c and F stay fixed, profit changes by ten times c.
  2. Divide the profit change by starting profit and the volume change by starting volume. Their ratio is Qc/π.
  3. At 400 units, €30 unit contribution and €9,600 fixed cost, contribution is €12,000 and profit €2,400. DOL equals five and 40 fewer units remove €1,200 of profit.

When the reasoning holds

  • Prices, sales mix, unit variable costs and capacity costs must stay constant over the comparison. A promotion can alter both price and units.
  • Starting profit must be nonzero for the percentage measure. Close to break-even, report absolute euro changes alongside the very large ratio.
Terms in this lesson
Revenue
Amount earned by selling goods or services. The customer may pay later.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Margin
In business accounts, profit expressed relative to sales. In derivatives trading, margin is collateral required to support a position.
First example

A stall sells two coffees contributing €2 each for every snack contributing €5. That three-item bundle contributes €9. With €900 fixed costs, it needs 100 bundles, comprising 200 coffees and 100 snacks.

From revenue to operating profit

Cost–volume–profit analysis writes operating profit as total contribution less fixed costs. Each extra unit adds selling price minus variable cost so long as price, unit variable cost and capacity remain unchanged. Positive contribution is compatible with an operating loss when volume is too low.

Break-even solves for zero profit. A target profit changes the numerator to fixed costs plus that target. A contribution margin ratio expresses contribution per euro of sales, which lets a manager convert a profit objective into revenue when the product mix is stable.

A mix has its own economics

If a business sells basic and premium memberships with different contributions, an observed average embeds how many of each were sold. Replacing premium sales with basic ones reduces profit even if total subscribers and capacity costs are unchanged.

Write one representative bundle using the expected mix and compute its contribution. To reach a target with indivisible products, round the bundle count upward. If the mix is flexible, there can be many combinations that reach the same profit.

The cushion before losses begin

Margin of safety measures the difference between planned sales and break-even sales. Express it in units, money or a fraction of planned sales, and specify which. A narrow cushion makes profits sensitive to even a small forecasting error.

Operating leverage describes that sensitivity. At a profitable starting volume, total contribution divided by operating profit approximates the percentage change in profit per percentage change in sales volume under fixed prices, mix and costs. The ratio becomes unstable near zero profit.

Method

Tracing a profit target

  1. Pin down the mix

    Use actual quantities in one bundle so the denominator is a contribution per identifiable item or bundle.

  2. Solve for contribution

    Add the desired operating profit to fixed expenses and divide by contribution per bundle.

  3. Check rounded quantities

    Multiply final unit counts by their respective margins, deduct fixed costs and compare the actual profit with the target.

Worked case

Two plans at a design school

Each weekday class seat contributes €20 and each weekend seat €35. Expected sales are three weekday seats per weekend seat. Fixed teaching space and administration cost €7,600 for the term.

Working

  1. A four-seat bundle contributes 3 × €20 + €35 = €95. The school breaks even at 80 bundles, or 240 weekday seats plus 80 weekend seats.
  2. For €1,900 of profit it needs €9,500 of contribution, or 100 bundles. That represents 300 weekday and 100 weekend seats.
  3. At 100 bundles, losing 20 bundles wipes out the €1,900 profit. The volume margin of safety is 20%. Replacing weekend seats with weekday seats would erode the margin sooner.

Interpretation

Before promoting a cheaper class, management should test whether it attracts new students or diverts the same students from higher-contribution seats.

A common mistake

Treating the sales mix as a constant law can make a precise break-even target misleading.

How to correct it

Recalculate contribution per bundle when promotions or capacity constraints materially change which products sell.

Quick check
A ticket costs €30, serving one visitor costs €12 and an event has €1,800 in fixed costs. How many visitors cover those costs?

Each visitor contributes €18. Dividing €1,800 by €18 gives 100 visitors. Using the full ticket price would leave the visitor costs unpaid.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A venue contributes €12 per standard ticket and €30 per premium ticket. It expects two standard tickets per premium ticket, has €5,400 fixed costs, and plans to sell 120 mixed bundles.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate break-even bundles and profit at the plan.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Find the volume margin of safety and the impact of replacing ten premium tickets with ten standard ones at the planned headcount.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

The three-ticket bundle contributes €54. Break-even is 100 bundles. At 120 bundles profit is €6,480 − €5,400 = €1,080.

Part B 3 marks

The cushion is 20 bundles, or 20/120 = 16.67% of plan. Each substitution loses €18, so profit falls €180 to €900.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why does a 10% drop in volume cut profit by 50% for one manufacturer?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

The shop starts with €12,000 of contribution but only €2,400 of profit. Losing 10% of its units loses 10% of the contribution, or €1,200. That is half its profit because fixed commitments continue. I would also show the break-even threshold of 320 units. The comparison helps the manager see why an apparently modest sales miss has such a large effect.

Interviewer’s follow-up

At 440 sales, would another 10% decline still halve profit?

Compare with the follow-up answer

At 440 units contribution is €13,200 and profit €3,600. The new leverage is about 3.67. A 44-unit fall removes €1,320, roughly 36.7% of this higher starting profit. Operating leverage belongs to a particular starting volume.
