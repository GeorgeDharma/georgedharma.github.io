Relevant cost and capacity

A special order can be attractive when there is spare capacity. If capacity is scarce, include the contribution lost on displaced business.

The starting point

A printer has one afternoon free. A low-priced special order can still add cash if it covers the ink, labour and other costs caused by accepting it. A share of last month's rent in the product accounts will be the same whichever choice the printer makes.

A scarce machine changes the comparison. Taking one order may mean giving up another. The contribution from the best alternative forgone is an opportunity cost, even though no supplier sends an invoice for it.

Theory and method

Contribution per unit of limiting factor

Bottleneck analysis · Shadow price

A premium order contributes twice as much per item as a standard order. Which should get the final machine-hour?

An artisan factory has more orders than finishing-machine hours. The useful comparison is the contribution it sacrifices when one hour moves from the best remaining alternative. An item with a high margin may use so much finishing time that several cheaper items earn more in the same slot. Ranking by contribution per bottleneck hour yields a marginal value of capacity while demand remains available. The value changes when the high-return order book fills or another resource becomes scarce.

How the result is built

mᵢ = cᵢ/hᵢ; incremental gain = new contribution − hours displaced × m*

i identifies a product, cᵢ is euro contribution per item, hᵢ bottleneck hours per item, mᵢ euros per constrained hour, and m* the available marginal alternative's contribution per hour.

  1. A standard item yields €24 in half an hour, or €48 per hour. A premium item yields €48 in two hours, or €24 per hour.
  2. Fill standard demand first if either product can use the final hours. After all standard orders are satisfied, the marginal alternative falls to €24 per hour.
  3. A special order using four otherwise standard hours displaces €192 of contribution. If two hours genuinely idle, displacement falls to two times €48, or €96.

When the reasoning holds

  • There must be one binding capacity constraint and comparable divisible production. Indivisible batches or several bottlenecks may require comparing complete schedules.
  • Only sales that could actually be made enter the opportunity cost. Unsold theoretical capacity has no forgone contribution.
Terms in this lesson
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Revenue
Amount earned by selling goods or services. The customer may pay later.
How to read the notation
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.

All notation examples

First example

A rush order pays €1,300 and adds €800 of cash costs. It adds €500 with spare capacity. If it displaces an order contributing €650, accepting it instead reduces total contribution by €150.

Designing the right comparison

A relevant cost is a future cash flow that differs between feasible alternatives. Past research spending is sunk, but an asset already paid for might still fetch a resale price or free a scarce machine-hour. Its original invoice alone cannot determine today's choice.

Start by describing what happens if the order is rejected. Unsold materials might expire, be sold, or be used in ordinary production. Each alternative produces a different opportunity cost, even though the inventory appears at a single carrying amount.

The bottleneck determines the ranking

When one resource limits production, contribution per unit of that resource measures the gain from its next available hour or kilogram. A high contribution per finished item can hide an inefficient user of the bottleneck. Demand limits matter because high-margin work may run out.

This ranking solves a simple divisible-product problem with one binding constraint and no setup effects. A second bottleneck, indivisible orders or minimum runs can change which feasible combination is best. Compare whole schedules when the simple rule no longer applies.

What can actually be avoided

Annual rent allocated to a product may continue after that product disappears. Conversely, a contract cancellation fee, extra quality test or associated repeat purchase may be genuinely incremental. Identify whether removal of an overhead line frees cash, capacity or neither.

In a temporary order decision, a lower price can be attractive without being sustainable as a regular list price. Repeated discount orders can crowd out ordinary demand, change customer expectations or require a larger permanent plant.

Method

Calculating the reservation price

  1. Identify displaced work

    Deduct genuinely unused hours first, then identify the best contribution the business would lose on the remaining hours.

  2. Include order-specific outlays

    Count cash materials, extra labour, transport and setup relevant to the whole order, independently of how the accounts classify them.

  3. Price the complete commitment

    Add lost contribution to new cash cost, divide by accepted units and state what would invalidate that minimum.

Worked case

A testing bench at full stretch

An engineering shop has 18 testing hours spare. A special batch of 30 pieces will need one testing hour each. Conventional work contributes €40 per testing hour and can fill any time the special batch displaces.

Working

  1. The special batch costs €22 per piece plus €180 one-time preparation. Its cash outlay is 30 × €22 + €180 = €840.
  2. Twelve testing hours displace regular work, costing 12 × €40 = €480 of contribution. The lowest price preserving current profit is (€840 + €480)/30 = €44 per piece.
  3. A €42 offer would yield €1,260 revenue against €1,320 incremental economic cost. Refusing it preserves €60 even though revenue exceeds the batch's direct cash costs.

Interpretation

The minimum changes if the conventional customer cancels. The released hours would remove the lost contribution, bringing the cash-only floor to €28 per piece.

A common mistake

Allocating all factory expenses to an order and then also charging displaced contribution can count the same cost in different forms.

How to correct it

Construct cash flows for accept and reject. Retain costs that differ and the gain available from the best work given up.

Quick check
An order earns €600 above its extra operating costs. It would displace normal work contributing €450 and requires a €100 permit. What is the gain from acceptance?

Add the €600 contribution, subtract the €450 sacrificed and the extra €100 permit. The remaining €50 is the improvement relative to keeping the normal work.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A factory has 10 machine-hours free. An indivisible order of 20 units uses 30 hours, costs €300 in material and €100 in special shipping. Ordinary work contributes €25 per displaced hour.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Compute the minimum total and unit price.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    If ordinary demand disappears before the order arrives, find the new minimum and explain the change.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Twenty hours displace €500. The minimum total is €300 + €100 + €500 = €900, equivalent to €45 per unit.

Part B 3 marks

All hours are now free under the assumptions. The minimum falls to €400 or €20 per unit because the forgone contribution becomes zero.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A premium order contributes twice as much per item as a standard order. Which should get the final machine-hour?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would allocate time to standard orders while customers will still buy them. They produce €48 per finishing hour, compared with €24 for premium. The €150 special batch yields €37.50 an hour and belongs between those two options. Its total effect depends on which orders it actually displaces and whether any finishing hours would otherwise remain unused.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Only two standard items remain in demand. A four-hour special batch competes with those and otherwise available premium work. What does it displace?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Two standard items use one hour and earn €48. The other three hours could earn €72 at the premium rate if divisible scheduling is feasible. The alternative total is €120, so a €150 batch adds €30. With indivisible premium batches I would compare feasible schedules directly.
