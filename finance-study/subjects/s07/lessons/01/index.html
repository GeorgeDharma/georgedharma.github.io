Cost behaviour

Separate fixed, variable and mixed costs within a stated operating range. A fixed cost can become variable when the business adds capacity.

The starting point

A bakery pays rent even on a quiet Monday and buys flour for every loaf. To forecast costs, first identify the activity that causes them to change. A variable cost rises with activity. A fixed cost stays the same in total over a stated range and period.

Some bills mix both patterns. A delivery contract may include a monthly fee and a charge per parcel. Capacity matters too. An extra shift or a second oven can make a previously fixed monthly total jump.

Theory and method

Ordinary least squares cost estimation

Least-squares cost function · Cost-driver analysis

Would you quote the next 100 testing hours at the historic average cost when the monthly equipment lease stays fixed?

A laboratory observes both monthly test hours and electricity bills. The bill it would have received with fewer tests depends on the standing charge and how much power extra tests use. Estimate a line through several months by choosing the intercept and slope that minimize squared prediction errors. The slope describes the additional spend associated with another hour inside the observed operating range. Looking at which months miss the line can uncover maintenance or tariff changes before the estimate is used for a quotation.

How the result is built

Ĉ = a + bh, with b = Σ[(h − h̄)(C − C̄)] / Σ(h − h̄)² and a = C̄ − bh̄

C is observed monthly euro cost, Ĉ fitted euro cost, h machine-hours, bars sample means, a euros per month, b euros per hour and Σ sums across months.

  1. Centre the observations. For 100, 200 and 300 hours and bills of €900, €1,300 and €1,700, the means are 200 hours and €1,300.
  2. Cross-products sum to 80,000 euro-hours and squared hour deviations to 20,000 hours squared. The fitted slope is €4 per hour.
  3. Subtract 200 × €4 from €1,300 to recover €500 fixed cost. Moving from 200 to 250 hours adds €200 although average spend at 200 hours is €6.50.

When the reasoning holds

  • Hours must vary across observations. With identical hours the data cannot identify slope separately from the standing charge.
  • Stable work content and tariffs are needed for a marginal interpretation. If peak hours also attract a higher tariff, a pooled slope mixes two changes.
First example

A courier charges €300 per month plus €2 per delivery. Moving from 100 to 150 deliveries raises the bill from €500 to €600. The extra 50 cost €2 each even though the average cost at 100 was €5.

Why the activity measure matters

A cost driver is something that causes resource use. The number of parcels can explain packaging consumption while the number of delivery stops explains a driver's hours. Orders, customers and units are different measures even when all three grew last month.

Use the same period and activity definition for all observations. If a business outsources long-distance deliveries in one month, distance covered by its own vehicles changes. Comparing total deliveries with its own fuel bill across both months mixes different operations.

Estimating a mixed cost

Suppose total monthly cost equals a standing charge plus a constant price for every machine-hour. Subtracting costs in two months removes the common standing charge. Divide the difference in cost by the difference in hours to estimate the variable rate.

This high–low estimate depends entirely on two observations. Take the highest and lowest activity levels rather than the most expensive and cheapest months. Compare the predicted bills with the remaining observations. A scatterplot can reveal a tariff change or an unusual repair.

When another unit needs another shift

The relevant range is the activity interval in which a given staffing plan, contract and production method apply. Within that range each extra unit can cost the same amount, while average cost per unit falls as more output shares the standing charge.

Beyond the range, an extra supervisor or a second machine can make fixed cost rise in a step. The relevant decision then concerns a block of capacity. Ask how long the extra commitment lasts and how much output can actually use it.

Method

Building a defensible cost forecast

  1. Identify the unit

    Choose a causal activity measure and state the time interval for which costs are observed.

  2. Estimate the slope

    Divide the change in comparable costs by the change in activity, then substitute one observation to infer the standing charge.

  3. Test the range

    Compare the forecast with a third observation and the contract or staffing arrangement before extending it beyond previous activity.

Worked case

A laboratory adds a weekend shift

A lab spent €9,200 at 400 tests and €12,800 at 700 tests. Its current staffing agreement covers up to 800 tests monthly. Above that level, one extra technician costs €2,000 per month while the variable test cost stays constant.

Working

  1. The €3,600 cost difference over 300 extra tests implies €12 per test. At 400 tests the estimated standing cost is €9,200 − €4,800 = €4,400.
  2. The 780-test estimate is €4,400 + 780 × €12 = €13,760. Crossing the limit, 850 tests cost €4,400 + €2,000 + 850 × €12 = €16,600.
  3. The extra 70 tests therefore cost €2,840, including the shift. Their average incremental cost is €40.57, well above the €12 consumables charge.

Interpretation

A manager quoting a one-off batch needs to consider how much of the new capacity other orders could use during the same month.

A common mistake

Using a falling average cost as proof of efficiency confuses greater utilization with better production.

How to correct it

Compare physical input or spending at the same output level. Show the changed standing charge separately if the staffing agreement changed.

Quick check
A workshop pays €900 a month for a machine and €3 in materials per unit. Within its existing capacity, what does producing 20 more units add?

The machine bill is payable at either production level. Only materials increase, by 20 × €3. Revisit the calculation if a second machine becomes necessary.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A distribution centre reports €13,000 for 1,000 parcels and €17,500 for 1,500. Its existing contract supports up to 1,600 parcels. At 1,601 and above, a €1,200 monthly capacity charge applies.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Estimate its ordinary fixed and variable charges.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Estimate costs at 1,550 and 1,650. Explain why the difference exceeds €9 per added parcel.

Part A 3 marks

The slope is €4,500 ÷ 500 = €9 per parcel. Subtract €9,000 from the first bill to find the fixed charge of €4,000.

Part B 3 marks

They are €17,950 and €20,050. The €2,100 difference includes €900 of parcel charges and the €1,200 capacity step.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Would you quote the next 100 testing hours at the historic average cost when the monthly equipment lease stays fixed?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would begin with the incremental €4 per hour. Another 100 hours within the fitted range adds €400 if the same equipment and tariff apply. At 200 hours the average includes €2.50 of monthly capacity charge per hour. Before promising a price I would check staffing limits, tariff changes and whether taking this work would displace a more profitable order.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The laboratory needs an extra €350 standby contract at 301 hours. How does an increase from 250 to 350 hours change the bill?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The 100 hours add €400 at the continuing usage rate, and crossing 300 adds €350. Total incremental cost is €750. The historical line did not observe that change in capacity, so I would append a separate contractual step.
