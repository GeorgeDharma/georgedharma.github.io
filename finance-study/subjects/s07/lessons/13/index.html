Target costing and lifetime product economics

Convert a target selling price and required margin into an allowable lifetime cost.

Theory and terminology

Target costing and life-cycle costing

allowable cost · required margin · lifetime product cost

What you should be able to explain

  • Convert a target selling price and required margin into an allowable lifetime cost.
  • Use volume sensitivity and value engineering to test a proposed redesign.

Start with the customer’s price

Market research and comparable products suggest the price buyers will actually pay. Management specifies a return on sales, meaning profit divided by sales. Subtract the required profit from the expected price to find the highest cost the product can carry. A markup is instead profit divided by cost and would give a different target.

Product teams can then work backward through the design. They look for a cheaper way to provide a valued function, such as a replaceable seal that prevents a warranty call. A cheaper component that raises later returns can increase total cost.

Decide whose costs are in scope

Life-cycle costing gathers costs from design, production, distribution, service and disposal over the product’s life. The case uses the producer’s costs and comparable undiscounted euros. A customer’s energy bill matters to willingness to pay but only enters the manufacturer’s cost sheet if the manufacturer bears it.

Combine expected per-unit costs with one-off spending divided by expected lifetime units. Volume connects the sales forecast to the cost target. A drop in volume raises the fixed cost per expected sale even if manufacturing has become more efficient.

Turn a cost gap into design decisions

Identify the required customer functions and their failure consequences before cutting cost. Cost engineering can compare alternative designs against both the allowable cost and the service standard. Keep the volume, failure-rate and lifetime support assumptions visible.

This is an early design and feasibility tool. For a long project with meaningful up-front outlays, time all cash flows and calculate NPV as a second decision. An accounting margin can be met even when slow revenues produce an inadequate return on capital.

Method

Build the result

Allowable cost per unit = P × (1 − m). Estimated lifetime unit cost = v + F/Q. Cost gap = estimated cost − allowable cost.

P is expected euros of sales per unit. m is target profit divided by sales. v is lifetime variable cost per unit, F total one-off euro cost and Q lifetime units sold. A positive gap requires savings or revised commercial terms.

  1. Convert the target margin to euros per sale and subtract it from the feasible price.
  2. Estimate variable lifetime costs and spread one-off design and tooling costs across the same expected sales volume.
  3. Compare proposed changes over all stages, then recompute cost at a lower volume or a higher claim rate before sign-off.
Worked case

A laboratory pump redesign changes warranty and design cost

A pump can sell for €100. The required profit margin is 20% and expected volume is 20,000. Manufacturing costs €66 per sale, expected support €8 and disposal €2. Initial design costs €120,000. A redesign costs a further €60,000, saves €5 manufacturing per sale and saves €1 in expected support.

Working

  1. Target cost is €100 × 0.80 = €80. Original product cost is €66 + €8 + €2 + €120,000/20,000 = €82.
  2. The original plan therefore misses the cost target by €2 a unit, or €40,000 in total across the planned sales.
  3. The redesign reduces variable lifetime cost to €61 + €7 + €2 = €70. Total design cost rises to €180,000, or €9 per unit. Revised unit cost is €79.
  4. At 12,000 sales the same design adds €15 per unit, giving €85 and a €5 cost gap. The redesigned plan needs at least €180,000/(€80 − €70) = 18,000 sales to reach the margin.

Decision and interpretation

Release the design only if there is credible demand near 18,000 or more, and verify that the support savings are compatible with warranty quality. A weak demand forecast weakens the case despite the cheaper manufacturing process.

Quick check
A product sells for €50 and must earn a 20% return on sales. It has €32 variable lifetime cost and €96,000 in one-off design spending. How many sales just achieve the target?

Allowable cost is €40, leaving €8 per sale for design. €96,000 divided by €8 gives 12,000 units. A margin on sales uses the selling price as its base.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

A meter is expected to sell for €80 with a required 30% margin. Sales are 15,000, design costs €90,000 and the manufacturing, support and disposal costs are €42, €5 and €2 per unit. A new disposal rule in the scenario would add €3 per unit. Assess both designs without discounting.

Reveal the worked solution
  1. Allowable cost is €80 × 0.70 = €56. Design recovery is €90,000/15,000 = €6 per unit.
  2. Current lifetime cost is €42 + €5 + €2 + €6 = €55, giving €1 headroom per unit.
  3. With the change, cost is €58. The gap of €2 per unit totals €30,000 at expected volume. Document how savings, a higher feasible selling price or increased volume would cover it.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Can a redesign that makes each unit cheaper still fail the launch profitability target?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Yes. A redesign can cut the variable cost but raise fixed design spending that fewer sales must recover. The pump costs €70 per unit before design and its allowable cost is €80. Recovering €180,000 from the €10 allowance requires 18,000 sales. At 12,000 units the redesigned pump costs €85, beyond the launch target.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If the €90,000 was already spent and the customer offers a one-off repeat order, should €6 of design recovery automatically set the order floor?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The launch review measures lifetime economics. A subsequent order decision uses the future cash and capacity the order changes. Past design spending is sunk. The new order still needs to cover its avoidable manufacture, service, disposal and any opportunity cost of scarce capacity.
