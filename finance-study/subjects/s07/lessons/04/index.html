Allocation and activity costing

Allocating overhead by volume can misrepresent products that consume different support activities. Compare product rankings under two allocation bases.

The starting point

Two products may use the same quantity of steel but different amounts of factory support. One long production run may need one machine setup while several short runs need five. Dividing setup costs by units made hides that difference.

Activity-based costing groups costs by activity and assigns each pool using an observable driver such as setups, inspections or orders. A driver should reflect how the work arises. Allocated cost is helpful for understanding resource use, even when the total would take time to change.

Theory and method

Kaplan–Anderson time-driven activity-based costing

TDABC · Capacity cost rate

What prevents a department from assigning every euro of idle staff cost to this month's active customers?

A payments team employs staff for a whole month while individual transactions take different amounts of time. A payroll estimate tells us what time costs to supply. A time equation tells us how much practical staff time a particular transaction consumes. Dividing by practical capacity lets a complex transaction bear its extra resource use and also reveals unused staff time. A low-demand month can then show spare capacity without silently raising the cost estimate for every normal transaction.

How the result is built

k = C/T; t = b + ax; assigned cost = kt

C is supplied department cost in euros, T practical available minutes, k euros per minute, b basic minutes, a extra minutes for an exception, x equals one for an exception and zero otherwise, and t minutes consumed.

  1. €36,000 of monthly staff capacity supplies 12,000 practical minutes, so k = €3 per minute after normal breaks and training.
  2. Each routine case takes 4 minutes. An exception adds 6, so the assigned costs are €12 for routine and €30 for exceptional cases.
  3. Serving 1,500 routine and 300 exceptional cases consumes 9,000 minutes, assigning €27,000. The remaining 3,000 minutes reconcile to €9,000 idle capacity.

When the reasoning holds

  • Practical minutes must measure sustainable available time. Using theoretical clock time understates the capacity rate.
  • Time equations need validation against observed work. If exception types require very different effort, one six-minute increment may misclassify accounts.
First example

A €9,000 setup pool covers 15 setups. Each setup receives €600. A custom line using ten setups receives €6,000, even if it accounts for only one fifth of unit volume.

Unit, batch and customer-level support

A cost hierarchy distinguishes work performed for each unit, each production batch, each product line or the whole facility. Factory cleaning may depend on production days while a regulatory design review can support an entire product regardless of its unit volume.

Allocating all support by units implicitly assumes each unit consumes the same resources. High-volume simple products can then subsidize complex short runs. The total company cost is unchanged, but apparent product margins and price decisions can change.

Building and interpreting activity rates

In activity-based costing, group similar support costs and choose an observable driver for each group. A rate is budgeted pool cost divided by the amount of driver capacity used in the model. Multiply each product's driver usage by that rate.

Using practical capacity rather than actual low demand exposes idle capacity. The unassigned cost then has an explanation. Spreading the same capacity bill over unusually few jobs can suggest raising prices just when demand is already weak.

A cost assignment and a cash decision

An ABC calculation helps identify which customers or products require complex work. It does not establish that allocated resources could be eliminated immediately. Staff may stay on the payroll while learning to support other products.

Ask what decision is being made. For a long-run product redesign, reducing setups may avoid an extra team. For a one-off order with existing idle capacity, the incremental setup cash can be far less than its fully assigned cost.

Method

Rebuilding product margins

  1. Split incompatible pools

    Separate resources that behave differently, such as per-batch setups and per-order dispatch.

  2. Select causal drivers

    Check timing and effort per driver occurrence. If one inspection is ten times longer, consider technician hours instead of counts.

  3. Reconcile assignments

    Sum charges to products and separately report any cost of unused capacity. Compare both product and total company results.

Worked case

Hidden support in custom orders

A plant spends €24,000 on setups and €12,000 on dispatch. There are 60 setups and 120 shipments. A custom order needs six setups and two shipments for 20 items.

Working

  1. The setup rate is €400 and the shipment rate is €100. The order receives 6 × €400 + 2 × €100 = €2,600, or €130 per item.
  2. A volume-only method spreads both pools over 1,200 items, charging €30 each. It assigns €600 to this order, understating its support consumption by €2,000 relative to the two-driver model.
  3. If engineering reduces setups from six to two, the assigned cost falls €1,600. That reduction saves cash only if capacity commitments or additional purchases can change.

Interpretation

A customer negotiation might combine batches or accept larger shipments. Both parties can then share an actual saving when operational capacity is released or used more profitably.

A common mistake

Assuming a new product allocation changes the company's total historical expense confuses attribution with resource use.

How to correct it

Reconcile both allocation methods to the same pools, then analyse which changes in production could alter future cash.

Quick check
An inspection team spends most of its time checking batches. Two lines have equal output. One has four batches and the other has twelve. How should a €4,000 inspection pool be assigned if batches take equal time?

There are sixteen inspections, costing €250 each on average. Multiplying by each line's batch count reflects the activity that consumed the inspectors' time.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

A setup team costs €18,000 for practical capacity of 60 setups. Only 45 are used, 15 on line X and 30 on line Y. Assume setups require equal effort.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Compute the practical-capacity rate, charges to each line and unassigned cost.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Compare a rate based on actual usage and explain one planning implication.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Rate €300 per setup. X receives €4,500 and Y €9,000. The remaining €4,500 represents 15 unused setups.

Part B 3 marks

Actual usage gives €400 per setup, allocating €6,000 to X and €12,000 to Y. It conceals idle capacity by loading the entire commitment onto current jobs.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

What prevents a department from assigning every euro of idle staff cost to this month's active customers?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would use the capacity rate of €3 per practical minute and assign the time each job consumes. Routine cases receive €12 and exceptional ones €30. The team has €9,000 of unused capacity this month. Customers should see a rate grounded in the operating capacity we chose to supply, while the department head can see whether to redeploy or eventually resize the unused time.

Interviewer’s follow-up

A new tool cuts the extra exception time from six minutes to two. What saving would you report?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Each of 300 exceptions releases four minutes, so 1,200 minutes or €3,600 of assigned capacity moves to unused or alternative work. Payroll remains €36,000 unless staffing commitments change. I would show time released separately from any demonstrated cash saving.
