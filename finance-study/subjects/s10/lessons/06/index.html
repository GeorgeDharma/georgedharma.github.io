Allocation and guarantees

Allocate a risk to a party able to control or absorb it. A public guarantee changes who bears a loss and may create a contingent fiscal obligation.

The starting point

A toll-road contractor can influence how efficiently it builds the road. It has little influence over whether the government acquires land on time. Assigning every risk to the contractor may raise its price or make its promise impossible to honour.

Compare who can prevent a loss, absorb it and document when it occurred. A guarantee shifts specified losses to the guarantor. If a public authority gives one, payment may be uncertain in normal times yet become a substantial call on public funds in a downturn.

Theory and method

Minimum-revenue guarantee as a put spread

Contingent fiscal liability · Capped guarantee

Why can paying a fraction of revenue shortfalls produce a surprisingly large budget exposure for a city?

A city promises to compensate a concession if demand falls below an agreed revenue floor. The city pays nothing in strong markets, then pays a rising share down to the point where its cap binds. This piecewise shape resembles a put option, which pays when a measured value falls below a threshold. The cap subtracts a second, lower-strike put. Evaluating the payout in each state captures the asymmetry hidden by a calculation using only average revenue.

How the result is built

G(R) = α[(K−R)⁺ − (K−C/α−R)⁺]

R actual annual revenue, K minimum-revenue threshold, C cap in euros, α guaranteed fraction between zero and one, (x)⁺ means max(x,0), and G is public payout in euros.

  1. Set K = €20m, α = 0.5 and C = €4m. Payout starts below revenue of €20m and the cap binds at €20m − €4m/0.5 = €12m.
  2. Revenue of €16m creates a €4m gap and €2m payment. Revenue of €8m would generate €6m before the cap but still pays €4m.
  3. In an equally likely €8m or €24m year the mean revenue is €16m and expected payout €2m. Other state mixes can make the payout at average revenue substantially different, as the follow-up shows.

When the reasoning holds

  • A guarantee needs a verifiable revenue definition, trigger, cap and collection date. Changes to the definition can change the public liability.
  • The option analogy describes the state payoff. Market value requires an appropriate discounting and risk model, separate from an undiscounted budget forecast.
Terms in this lesson
Revenue
Amount earned by selling goods or services. The customer may pay later.
First example

A government promises to cover half the annual revenue shortfall below €10m, capped at €2m. If actual revenue is €4m, half the €6m gap is €3m. The cap limits the public payment to €2m.

Allocating control and bearing capacity

A risk can be managed by the party best placed to prevent it, observe it, insure it or finance the residual loss. Transferring a large risk to a contractor with little control may produce an expensive bid or a promise the contractor cannot honour.

A risk matrix should identify trigger, allocation, mitigation, evidence, limits and residual exposure. Separate events by cause. Delays caused by the contractor, land acquisition or a general disaster can be subject to quite different rights even if all postpone opening.

The value and cost of a guarantee

A guarantee is a contingent claim. Its expected payout is the sum of each scenario's probability times its contractual payout. A capped minimum-revenue guarantee often pays only on the shortfall below a threshold. Apply the covered fraction and cap to each scenario before averaging.

A guarantee's expected payout is distinct from its maximum legal amount, a fair market premium and the cash authorities must have during a severe downturn. Public revenues may fall precisely when several project guarantees become payable together.

Incentives and verification

Covering all demand losses can weaken incentives to advertise or improve service. Sharing a fraction, including a deductible or requiring independently verified performance preserves some incentive. Overly narrow triggers can instead leave the project unable to raise finance.

Compare a guarantee with alternative contract designs, including an availability payment or a smaller project. Fiscal commitments include expected base payments and contingent claims. A transparent stress calendar is useful even where no current cash payment appears in the budget.

Method

Valuing scenario payouts

  1. Define the trigger

    Identify the measured quantity, independent verifier, threshold, covered fraction, cap, expiry and payment lag.

  2. Calculate by state

    Find the non-negative shortfall in each state and apply the share and cap in the specified order.

  3. Examine fiscal concentration

    Average for expected cash but also report the worst allowed draw and other obligations likely to occur at the same time.

Worked case

A municipal revenue guarantee

A city pays 50% of annual revenue below €12m, capped at €3m. Revenue is €15m with probability 50%, €10m with 30% and €4m with 20%. All states refer to the same payment date.

Working

  1. At €15m no payment is due. At €10m the €2m gap gives €1m. At €4m half the €8m gap is €4m but the cap reduces payment to €3m.
  2. The expected payout is 0.50 × 0 + 0.30 × €1m + 0.20 × €3m = €0.9m. The project still bears €5m of the €8m gap in the lowest state.
  3. The city's maximum annual cash obligation under this guarantee is €3m, over three times the expected amount. Several transport projects could reach their caps in the same recession.

Interpretation

Depending on the perspective, a guarantee can support lender confidence while shifting a correlated burden onto taxpayers. Those are separate conclusions requiring separate measures.

A common mistake

Applying the guarantee to expected revenue generally gives the wrong expected payout because the threshold and cap create nonlinear payments.

How to correct it

Calculate a payout in every state first and weight those resulting amounts by their probabilities.

Quick check
A city guarantees 40% of a project's revenue gap below €15m, up to €3m. Revenue is €10m. How much does the city owe under this promise?

The shortfall is €5m. Forty percent gives €2m, below the contractual cap. The guarantee transfers only that amount of the demand loss to the city.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

A state covers 40% of revenue below €20m, capped at €4m. Revenue is €24m (50%), €15m (30%) or €5m (20%).

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Find each payout and the expected payout.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    What remains of the €15m shortfall in the low state, and why might budget planners stress multiple guarantees together?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Payouts are €0m, €2m and €4m. Expected payout is 0.30 × €2m + 0.20 × €4m = €1.4m.

Part B 3 marks

€11m remains uncovered. Shared economic or currency shocks can trigger payouts on multiple projects when public revenue is also weak.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why can paying a fraction of revenue shortfalls produce a surprisingly large budget exposure for a city?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would show the city the complete payout shape and its joint exposure to other guarantees. At €16m revenue this promise pays €2m, at €8m it pays €4m, and above €20m it pays zero. Averaging income before applying that formula can miss claims that occur in weak states. Budget planning also needs the payment date and correlated reduction in municipal revenue.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Revenue is €10m or €30m with equal probabilities. Comparing expected payout with payout at expected revenue, what error results?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The mean revenue is €20m, which generates zero if inserted directly. State payouts are €4m and zero, so their actual expected value is €2m. Using the mean before applying the threshold understates expected fiscal cash by €2m.
