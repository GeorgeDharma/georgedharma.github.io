Conventional loans, murabaha sales and ijara sukuk

Theory and terminology

Murabaha financing and ijara sukuk

Islamic finance · cost-plus sale · lease certificates · asset recourse

What you should be able to explain

  • Reconstruct who owns the asset and who owes each payment under a loan, a disclosed cost-plus sale and a lease-based certificate.
  • Compare financing returns on dated cash flows and calculate default recovery under expressly stated rights.

A loan funds the buyer. A murabaha financier first buys the goods

With a conventional equipment loan, the lender advances funds, the customer buys the machine and the loan agreement determines principal and interest. A security agreement can give the lender rights over the machine. Financing its purchase alone does not transfer ownership to the lender.

Islamic finance prohibits riba, which includes a stipulated interest return on a money loan. It permits profit from a genuine sale and rent for the use of an asset under the applicable contract conditions. Murabaha, also written murabahah, is a sale at disclosed acquisition cost plus agreed profit. In this equipment example the financier acquires the specific machine and takes possession, including valid constructive possession where permitted, before selling it to the customer on deferred terms. It faces ownership risk while it holds the machine. After the sale the customer owns the machine and the financier holds a sale receivable. A customer acting as buying agent needs evidence separating the agency purchase from the subsequent resale.

Rent buys the use of an asset

In ijara, or ijarah, a lessor grants the right to use an asset for a specified rent. In a lease-based sukuk, investors subscribe for certificates and a trustee or special-purpose entity acquires the asset or specified rights. The operator uses the asset and pays rent. The documentation allocates ownership-related costs, ordinary operating costs and any loss caused by misuse.

A service agent may perform maintenance on behalf of the owner. Responsibility for a cost and the person carrying out the repair can be different. Rent collected can exceed the amount distributable to investors after expenses. A separate purchase undertaking can fund final redemption when it is enforceable and the undertaking provider can pay.

Follow the claim through distress

A conventional lender relies on the loan claim and any valid security. A murabaha seller that has transferred the machine relies on its outstanding sale claim and any separately valid security. Titles, enforceable collateral and creditor priority determine access to proceeds. Always distinguish a missed instalment from the face amount still owing.

For sukuk, read the trust deed, transfer, lease, servicing agreement and purchase undertaking together. An effective asset transfer with a sale remedy can produce a different recovery from a claim on an insolvent undertaking provider. Calculation conventions, early-settlement rebates, treatment of late payment, Shariah governance and insolvency rules depend on the documents and jurisdiction. The RM figures below are teaching terms, not a Malaysian legal determination.

Method

Build the result

Murabaha sale price = C + M = Σₜ Iₜ. Effective annual return r solves C = Σₜ Iₜ/(1+r)ᵗ. Net lease distribution = collected rent − expenses payable by the trust.

C is the financier’s asset purchase cost, M the disclosed total markup and Iₜ the deferred sale instalment at year-end t, all in one currency. Σₜ sums all instalments. r is the annual investment return implied by that cash schedule, before fees and losses.

  1. Identify the dated ownership transfer or advance, then identify the legal source of the payment at each subsequent date.
  2. Compare the actual instalments and effective return on matching dates. A total markup as a percentage of purchase cost does not by itself give an annual return.
  3. For a lease certificate, deduct documented costs and test the specific maturity and default remedies. Credit a recovery route only to the extent proceeds are collectible and cannot be counted twice.
Worked case

Two financing files — a production machine and a freight depot

File A uses euros. A machine costs €100,000. A two-year conventional loan at 10% charges interest on opening principal and repays €50,000 of principal annually. A murabaha financier buys the same machine for €100,000 and sells for €115,000 in instalments of €60,000 and €55,000. File B uses Malaysian ringgit (RM). A trustee raises RM12m, buys the depot and leases it for three years at RM1m annual rent. Annual ownership costs and trust fees total RM0.20m. A performing year-three purchase undertaking pays RM12m.

Working

  1. File A, loan. Year one pays €50,000 principal + €10,000 interest = €60,000. Year two pays €50,000 + €5,000 = €55,000. The borrower bought the machine from the supplier.
  2. File A, sale. The financier pays the supplier, acquires the machine, then resells it for €100,000 cost plus €15,000 profit. The dates match the loan. Discounting €60,000/1.10 + €55,000/1.10² gives €100,000. Both schedules imply a 10% annual return before costs although the sale markup is 15% of purchase cost over both years.
  3. File A, missed final payment. Suppose both creditors have separately enforceable first security, net proceeds are €42,000 and no further recovery applies. The €55,000 payment due at year two leaves a €13,000 shortfall under either file. For the sale, the security agreement supplies the remedy after the financier transferred ownership.
  4. File B, normal operations. Each annual distribution is RM1m − RM0.20m = RM0.80m, or 6.67% of subscription. Performing receipts are RM0.80m, RM0.80m and RM12.80m in years one through three. The final RM12m comes from the purchase undertaking, separately from rent.
  5. File B, default after the second distribution. Assume no further rent. In one alternative deed a trustee can sell the depot for RM8.9m less RM0.6m enforcement costs and RM0.3m prior claims, recovering RM8.0m. In another it can enforce only an admitted RM12m undertaking claim that pays 45%, recovering RM5.4m. Lifetime cash including the two earlier distributions is RM9.6m or RM7.0m.

Decision and interpretation

A matching payment schedule permits a price comparison. It does not establish the same ownership, Shariah treatment or remedies. Obtain the transfer records, security and trustee powers, and test the relevant local-law and governance conclusions before assigning recovery.

Quick check
A murabaha financier buys equipment for RM80,000 and resells it on deferred terms for RM92,000. The buyer owns it after sale. RM30,000 is still due when the buyer defaults. There is no security and ordinary creditors recover 40% of allowed claims. What does the financier receive under these assumptions?

The allowed unpaid sale claim is RM30,000. A 40% distribution pays RM12,000. The customer’s ownership after sale and the absence of security leave no additional equipment-sale recovery for this creditor.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

A financier buys a refrigeration unit for RM240,000, then sells it for RM276,000 payable in three annual RM92,000 instalments. The customer pays the first two and defaults at the last due date. Security yields RM68,000 net and there is no further recovery. Separately, a lease trust earns RM450,000 annual rent, pays RM90,000 owner costs and has RM6m of certificates. Quantify the price, loss and lease distribution.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked solution
  1. The sale price is RM276,000 and its disclosed markup is RM36,000, or 15% of cost over the entire three-year schedule. Drawing an annual return comparison would require discounting the dated instalments.
  2. After two instalments, RM276,000 − RM184,000 = RM92,000 is unpaid. Net security recovery of RM68,000 leaves a RM24,000 loss on the final contractual amount.
  3. The separate trust distributes RM450,000 − RM90,000 = RM360,000 annually, or 6.00% of face for that period. Ranking that investment against the sale requires its subscription date, redemption terms and risk.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

If three structures finance a real asset and can produce similar annual payments, what would you inspect to tell which risk the funder actually owns?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would trace who bought the asset, who bore its ownership risks, and which agreement created the payment claim. The machine loan and deferred sale both produce €60,000 and €55,000, but one creditor advanced money and the other sold goods it acquired. For the depot certificates, I would verify the trustee’s rights and the undertaking provider’s credit. The stated default produces RM8.0m from an enforceable sale or RM5.4m from the alternative claim.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Suppose the refrigeration seller never acquired the equipment and the customer signed the final sale document before the supplier purchase. Are the same numerical instalments sufficient evidence of a properly constituted murabaha?

Compare with the follow-up answer

No. The sequence and genuine ownership or possession are material to the stated sale structure. Obtain dated purchase, agency, possession and resale records and refer the actual contracts to the relevant Shariah governance and legal reviewers. The arithmetic can describe payments, but it cannot confirm that the required sale occurred.
