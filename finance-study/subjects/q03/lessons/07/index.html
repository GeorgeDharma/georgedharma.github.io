Self-financing and replication

Changes in portfolio value arise from gains on existing holdings and their financing. Match a contingent payoff with a dynamic strategy when the model permits it.

The starting point

A self-financing portfolio pays for each rebalance from its existing cash and holdings. Its value changes through gains, losses and contractual cash flows. A replicating portfolio is self-financing and delivers the same future payoff as the claim being priced in every modeled state.

If one could buy the cheaper of two identical future payoffs and sell the more expensive with no impediment, the price difference would be an arbitrage. In a frictionless model that logic ties the claim’s present price to the cost of replication. The holdings must be chosen before learning which state occurs.

Theory and method

Black–Scholes–Merton replication equation

Delta hedging · Black–Scholes PDE

A trader says a stock’s large physical growth forecast must increase a hedgeable European option’s model value. Which term would reveal the mistake?

A dealer selling a smooth stock claim carries an exposure to the same price shock as the stock. Buying the right number of shares makes the random first-order price moves offset locally. The remaining cash invested or borrowed must earn the locally risk-free rate under the model. The stock’s physical growth forecast enters both the option and the matching stock holding with equal sensitivity, so it cancels in the replication argument.

How the result is built

Vₜ + ½σ²S²Vₛₛ + rSVₛ − rV = 0

V is euro claim value at calendar time t, S euros per share, Vₛ delta in shares, Vₛₛ gamma per euro, Vₜ euro change per year, σ annual stock volatility, and r the annual continuously compounded cash rate. The share pays no dividends.

  1. Itô’s formula gives V drift Vₜ + μSVₛ + ½σ²S²Vₛₛ and shock σSVₛ. A replicating portfolio holds Vₛ shares to match that shock.
  2. The cash position is V − SVₛ. Its drift combined with the stock is r(V − SVₛ) + μSVₛ. Equate both drifts and cancel μSVₛ.
  3. At V=8, S=100, delta 0.5, gamma 0.02, σ=0.20 and r=0.03 the model requires Vₜ = 0.24 − 1.50 − 4.00 = −€5.26 per year.

When the reasoning holds

  • The same diffusion shock drives the replicable claim and stock, with frictionless continuous rebalancing. A distinct jump-size risk can leave an unhedged state.
  • Specify exercise, terminal payoff and boundaries. The equation describes continuation values for this European setup, and a dividend yield would modify the stock-carry term.

Terms in this lesson
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Replicating portfolio
A combination of traded claims that delivers the same future cash flows as a contract in every state covered by the model.
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
Derivative
A contract whose value depends on something else, such as a currency, an interest rate or a share price.
How to read the notation
A change · Δ
ΔS commonly means the new asset price less the old price. A move from €50 to €53 gives ΔS = €3. In options, delta can also name a sensitivity. A delta of 0.6 means a €1 rise in the underlying predicts about a €0.60 rise in the option, for a small move.
Subscripts · S₀ and S₁
A small index names the time or item. If S₀ = €40 and S₁ = €42, the price moves from €40 at time 0 to €42 at time 1. A subscript i can label a company instead of a date.
Time and random increments · dt, dW
Over a short interval Δt, a standard Brownian increment has mean zero and standard deviation √Δt. If a year is one time unit, a quarter-year increment has standard deviation 0.5. A drift term scales with elapsed time itself.
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.

All notation examples

First example

A share at €100 will finish at €120 or €80. At zero interest, a call with strike €100 pays €20 or zero. Buy ½ share and borrow €40. The terminal values are €60 − €40 = €20 and €40 − €40 = zero. Replication costs €50 − €40 = €10 today.

A cash account completes a trading strategy

A position list becomes a self-financing strategy when each purchase is paid for from portfolio resources and each sale returns cash to the portfolio. In a discrete no-dividend model, wealth at t after rebalancing equals the previous holdings valued at current prices. External cash flows must be identified separately.

For stock and a bank account B, wealth is V = ΔS + θB. Self-financing gains satisfy dV = ΔdS + θdB. Changes in stock and cash holdings fund one another. Differentiating the accounting identity without imposing this financing condition can create a spurious source of value.

Solve a state-by-state hedge

In a one-step model a claim pays Cᵤ or C𝒹. Let Sᵤ and S𝒹 be final stock prices and R the gross cash growth factor. Solving ΔSᵤ + bR = Cᵤ and ΔS𝒹 + bR = C𝒹 gives Δ = (Cᵤ − C𝒹)/(Sᵤ − S𝒹) and b = (C𝒹 − ΔS𝒹)/R.

Both terminal equations concern cash at maturity. The cash amount b in today’s portfolio is discounted. Replicating either an unexpectedly cheap or expensive claim gives an arbitrage argument for equality of today’s claim price and ΔS₀ + b under frictionless trading assumptions.

From replication to the pricing equation

In continuous time, apply Itô’s formula to a smooth derivative V(t,S). Set the stock quantity to Vₛ to match its Brownian exposure. The remaining locally riskless position must earn the cash rate. Matching the drift yields Vₜ + ½σ²S²Vₛₛ + rSVₛ − rV = 0 for a non-dividend-paying stock.

This argument assumes continuous rebalancing, sufficient smoothness and no transaction costs or unhedged jumps. The physical drift cancels when the stock exposure matches. A terminal payoff and appropriate boundaries are still needed to specify the solution.

Method

Construct a hedge

  1. Calculate the payoff slope

    Divide the difference in the two state payoffs by the difference in stock prices.

  2. Fund the residual

    Use either state to find terminal cash, then discount it to today using the same cash accumulation factor.

  3. Verify both states

    Calculate portfolio value at each terminal node, followed by the net cost of the initial stock and cash holdings.

Worked case

Financing a capped claim

A stock costs €40 and can finish at €54 or €34. Cash grows 2%. A contract pays €8 in the upper state and €3 in the lower state. Fractional stock positions and borrowing are possible.

Working

  1. The payoff slope is (8 − 3)/(54 − 34) = 0.25 shares. At the low node the shares are worth €8.50, so terminal borrowing must be €5.50.
  2. Today’s borrowing is €5.50/1.02 ≈ €5.3922. The replicating cost is 0.25 × 40 − 5.3922 ≈ €4.6078.
  3. At the upper node shares are worth €13.50 and repaying €5.50 leaves €8. At the lower node €8.50 − €5.50 leaves €3.

Interpretation

A change in terminal cash interest would alter today’s borrowing and the no-arbitrage price, even with identical two-state claim payoffs.

At the board

Replicate one option with a share and a loan

A share at 100 finishes at 120 or 80 after one year. A call with exercise price 100 pays 20 or zero. The annual risk-free rate is 5%. Assume frictionless trading, borrowing and lending at the stated rate, and no dividends.

The hedge ratio is (20 − 0) / (120 − 80) = 0.5 share per option. Borrow just enough for the down-state payoff to be zero. The net cost 50 − 38.0952 is the no-arbitrage call price in this two-state model. If it were possible to buy the same two payoffs more cheaply elsewhere, the expensive position could be sold against the cheaper one.

A common mistake

The hedge ratio alone is the option’s price.

How to correct it

Delta is a quantity of shares. Value the shares at today’s price and include the financed residual. Check that both holdings together reproduce all contractual payoffs.

Quick check
On the same price tree, what replicates a put that pays zero at €120 and €20 at €80?

At the upper node −€60 + €60 = zero. At the lower node −€40 + €60 = €20. The initial net cost is €60 − €50 = €10.

Written practice · 9 marks

Independent exam problem

A stock costs 60, ends at 72 or 48, and cash grows by 5%. A put has strike 55.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Derive the replicating stock quantity and cash position.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Compute the put price and verify both terminal states.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

Payoffs are 0 and 7. Delta = −7/24. Terminal cash from the low node is 7 − (−7/24 × 48) = 21, so current cash is 21/1.05 = 20.

Part B 4 marks

Cost is 20 − (7/24) × 60 = 2.50. At 72 the short stock owes 21 and cash pays 21. At 48 the short owes 14, leaving 7.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A trader says a stock’s large physical growth forecast must increase a hedgeable European option’s model value. Which term would reveal the mistake?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

In the model, the same physical drift term appears on the option side through its delta and on the stock side through our hedge. Matching exposures eliminates it. The remaining time decay, gamma and financing terms must reconcile. At the stated point I obtain a calendar-time derivative of −€5.26 per year. A forecasting claim about the stock alone cannot set the price of this replicable payoff.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What changes with continuous proportional dividend yield q?

Compare with the follow-up answer

A shareholder earns the dividend on stock held in the hedge. The pricing equation’s carry coefficient becomes (r − q)S in front of delta for an ex-dividend spot price. Keep the payoff unchanged unless the contract itself passes dividends to its holder.
