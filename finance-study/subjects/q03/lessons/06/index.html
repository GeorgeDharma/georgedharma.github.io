Geometric Brownian motion

Model proportional price changes with drift and volatility. The log price has a drift adjustment of minus half the variance rate.

The starting point

A €2 move has different significance for a €10 share and a €200 share. Geometric Brownian motion models percentage changes instead. In dS/S = μ dt + σ dW, S is the current price, μ the instantaneous expected proportional growth rate and σ its volatility. Constant coefficients give a positive future price when the initial price is positive.

Applying Itô’s formula to ln S gives a log-price drift of μ − ½σ². Under this model the log price is normally distributed, so that drift controls median compound growth. A minority of large upward outcomes pulls the arithmetic mean above the median.

Theory and method

Geometric Brownian motion and a terminal threshold probability

Lognormal diffusion · Geometric Brownian motion

A firm can meet a €110 covenant in one year only if the asset value exceeds it. How would you translate a geometric price model into a probability?

A credit analyst may care more about crossing a specified value at a reporting date than about the average asset value. Under geometric Brownian motion the logarithm of the value has a normal distribution. The covenant can therefore be expressed as a cutoff for a standardized normal shock. An expected asset value above the covenant can coexist with a substantial chance of missing it because the terminal distribution is asymmetric.

How the result is built

P(Sₜ > K) = 1 − Φ([ln(K/S₀) − (μ − σ²/2)t]/[σ√t])

S₀ and Sₜ are current and terminal asset value in euros, K is the terminal euro threshold, t is years, μ is the assumed annual physical proportional drift, σ is annual volatility, Φ is the standard normal cumulative probability, and P is physical probability.

  1. Integrate the log dynamics to obtain ln(Sₜ/S₀) = (μ − σ²/2)t + σ√t Z with Z standard normal.
  2. Because log is increasing, the event Sₜ > K is equivalent to the log return exceeding ln(K/S₀). Subtract its mean and divide by its standard deviation.
  3. At S₀ = 100, K = 110, μ = 0.08, σ = 0.20 and t = 1, z = [ln(1.10) − 0.06]/0.20 ≈ 0.177. The upper-tail probability is about 43.0%.

When the reasoning holds

  • The threshold is checked only at the terminal date. A covenant checked daily requires a path or first-passage calculation and may trigger before a later recovery.
  • Constant coefficients, continuous paths and an appropriate physical law support this forecast. Equity-pricing probabilities answer a different question concerning traded prices.

How to read the notation
Time and random increments · dt, dW
Over a short interval Δt, a standard Brownian increment has mean zero and standard deviation √Δt. If a year is one time unit, a quarter-year increment has standard deviation 0.5. A drift term scales with elapsed time itself.
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.
Natural logarithm · ln
The log return is ln(ending price ÷ starting price). From €100 to €105 it equals ln(1.05) ≈ 0.04879. Log returns add across successive periods. The ordinary percentage gain for this period is 5%.
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.

All notation examples

First example

With S₀ = €100, μ = 6%, σ = 20% and one year, expected price is 100 × exp(0.06) ≈ €106.18. Median price is 100 × exp(0.06 − 0.5 × 0.20²) ≈ €104.08. Both figures come from the same model for a share paying no dividends.

Solve the proportional price model

Geometric Brownian motion models a price with dS = μSdt + σSdW and positive S₀. The absolute shock grows with the current price. Applying Itô’s formula to ln S uses derivative 1/S and curvature −1/S², which cancel the S factors in both coefficients.

This gives d ln S = (μ − σ²/2)dt + σdW. With constant coefficients, integrate to get Sₜ = S₀ exp[(μ − σ²/2)t + σWₜ]. The exponential preserves positivity for finite horizons, a property an arithmetic Brownian price does not share.

Mean, median and variance

The log price is normal with mean ln S₀ + (μ − σ²/2)t and variance σ²t. Its median is the exponential of its log mean. For a normal variable Z with mean m and variance v, E[exp(Z)] = exp(m + v/2), giving E[Sₜ] = S₀exp(μt).

The difference between mean and median arises from the right tail. Even a positive expected growth rate can coexist with a falling median if volatility is sufficiently large. The statement concerns typical versus average paths under an assumed process, without promising a realized return.

Forecasting assumptions versus pricing assumptions

Under an empirical or physical measure μ represents expected proportional growth before any dividend adjustments specified by the model. Under a cash-numeraire pricing measure, a non-dividend-paying traded stock under standard assumptions uses the risk-free rate in its drift. The physical estimate is still relevant to forecasting.

The model assumes continuous paths and constant proportional volatility here. Actual stocks can jump, pay dividends and experience changing volatility. Those departures can matter little for a short illustrative calculation yet matter greatly for tail exposures or a barrier option.

Method

Calculate a horizon distribution

  1. Convert time units

    Use annual drift and volatility with a year fraction T. Distinguish instantaneous continuously expressed drift from an effective annual growth percentage.

  2. Build log moments

    Set the log growth mean to (μ − σ²/2)T and standard deviation to σ√T.

  3. Choose the requested statistic

    Exponentiate the log median for a median price. Add half the log variance inside the exponential for the mean.

Worked case

Two investment horizons

A hypothetical asset starts at 80, with μ = 0.05 and σ = 0.30 per annual model conventions. It pays no dividends. Consider the two-year distribution under the stated physical measure.

Working

  1. The log-growth drift per year is 0.05 − 0.5 × 0.09 = 0.005. Two-year log growth has mean 0.01 and standard deviation 0.30√2 ≈ 0.4243.
  2. The median terminal value is 80exp(0.01) ≈ 80.80. The mean is 80exp(0.10) ≈ 88.41.
  3. A low terminal outcome can still occur. The event S₂ < 80 is equivalent to a normal log return below zero, roughly 0.024 standard deviations below its mean, giving probability just under one half.

Interpretation

A positive mean gain alone can conceal a wide distribution with a median close to the starting value.

A common mistake

Insert volatility 30 into the log-drift correction when other returns are written as decimals.

How to correct it

Use σ = 0.30 in a consistent decimal model. The volatility adjustment is half of 0.09, or 4.5 percentage points per year, before multiplying by horizon.

Quick check
If μ stays 6% and volatility rises to 40%, what becomes of the annual log-price drift?

The volatility correction is half of 0.40², or 8 percentage points. The log-price drift becomes 6% − 8% = −2%, although expected proportional growth remains 6%.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

Set S₀ = 50, μ = 0.04, σ = 0.40 and T = 0.5. Assume constant coefficients and no dividends.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate log-growth mean and standard deviation and the median price.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Calculate the mean price and explain its position relative to the median.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Log mean is (0.04 − 0.08) × 0.5 = −0.02 and standard deviation is 0.40√0.5 ≈ 0.2828. Median is 50exp(−0.02) ≈ 49.01.

Part B 4 marks

Mean equals 50exp(0.04 × 0.5) ≈ 51.01. The large positive right tail pulls the arithmetic expectation above the median, even though typical compounded growth is negative here.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A firm can meet a €110 covenant in one year only if the asset value exceeds it. How would you translate a geometric price model into a probability?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would start with the terminal log-return distribution under the physical model, convert the covenant to a log-return hurdle and standardize. At these inputs the €110 hurdle sits about 0.18 standard deviations above expected log growth. The modeled chance of finishing above it is roughly 43%. I would also ask whether the covenant applies at one date or throughout the year.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Can I use the terminal probability below €90 as the probability of ever falling below €90 during the year?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Every path ending below €90 has reached the threshold, but some paths reach it and recover. Starting from €100 under continuous paths, the terminal shortfall probability is a lower bound on the crossing probability. Use a barrier or simulated first-passage model for monitoring throughout the year.
