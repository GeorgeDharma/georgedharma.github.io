Completeness and limitations

Link absence of arbitrage with a pricing measure under standard conditions. Jumps, untraded risks and trading costs can make perfect replication impossible.

The starting point

A complete market has enough traded positions to reproduce every modeled contingent payoff. In a finite arbitrage-free model with standard trading assumptions, an equivalent pricing measure exists. Completeness makes that measure unique. The number of independent state risks relative to traded payoff directions matters.

An untraded source of risk, a jump with several possible sizes or trading frictions can disrupt a simple replication argument. In an incomplete market, traded assets constrain the prices of new claims, but additional assumptions or quoted instruments may be needed for a unique value.

Theory and method

Fundamental theorems of asset pricing in finite markets

Equivalent martingale measure · Completeness and spanning

How could two desks agree on every traded price and disagree on an unlisted claim without either creating arbitrage?

Cash and listed instruments determine the prices of their tradable combinations. A new claim can pay in a pattern those combinations cannot reproduce. An arbitrage-free finite market admits strictly positive state prices, but it may admit several such price vectors. Each agrees on existing instruments and can give a different value to an unspanned claim. That distinction separates existence of a viable pricing rule from uniqueness across all possible state payoffs.

How the result is built

Aᵀπ = p, π > 0, and an attainable payoff H = Ah costs pᵀh

A is a matrix of final euro payoffs with states as rows and traded assets as columns, π a vector of positive state prices, p current euro asset prices, h signed asset holdings, H terminal euro claim vector and ᵀ transpose.

  1. Positive state prices assign positive value to a nonnegative nonzero payoff. A zero-cost portfolio cannot deliver such a payoff at those traded prices.
  2. For an attainable claim Ah, compute πᵀAh = pᵀh. Hence its price agrees across every consistent π, even if the market is incomplete.
  3. If the asset columns span all state payoffs, the pricing rule is unique. In the stated finite frictionless setup, no arbitrage and completeness together imply unique equivalent martingale probabilities for a chosen traded numeraire.

When the reasoning holds

  • The elementary equivalences assume finitely many positive-probability states and unconstrained frictionless trading. General continuous-time versions require stronger no-free-lunch and admissibility conditions.
  • The comparison is within one model and one numeraire. Distinct measures associated with different numeraires can coexist even in a complete market.
Terms in this lesson
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
Derivative
A contract whose value depends on something else, such as a currency, an interest rate or a share price.
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
How to read the notation
Subscripts · S₀ and S₁
A small index names the time or item. If S₀ = €40 and S₁ = €42, the price moves from €40 at time 0 to €42 at time 1. A subscript i can label a company instead of a date.

All notation examples

First example

With zero interest, a €100 stock can end at €80, €100 or €120. Both state-weight sets (0.40, 0.20, 0.40) and (0.20, 0.60, 0.20) price the stock at €100. A new claim pays €10 only in the middle state. The two sets give competing values of €2 and €6.

Count independent state payoffs

Put modeled terminal states on matrix rows and traded assets on columns. A portfolio’s terminal payoffs are the matrix times its holdings. A target is attainable if its vector lies in the columns’ span. If this span covers all states, the market is complete in this finite model.

Having as many listed securities as states is insufficient if some payoffs duplicate combinations of others. Conversely, cash and one stock span any two-state payoff if the stock ends at different prices and cash pays the same positive amount in both states.

Price bounds in an incomplete market

No arbitrage requires a consistent positive state-price vector under the usual finite-state assumptions. In a complete nonredundant setting prices pin down that vector. In an incomplete setting multiple positive vectors can price all traded assets exactly, potentially disagreeing on an unspanned claim.

A portfolio dominating the new claim in every state supplies an upper bound and a portfolio dominated by it supplies a lower bound. Distinguish strict prices compatible with positive probability in each modeled state from endpoints obtained only as limits with zero state weights.

Limits in actual hedging

A model may be mathematically complete under frictionless continuous rebalancing while implementation remains costly or uncertain. Discrete hedging, funding constraints, uncertain volatility and jump size can leave exposures. Document which uncertainties are spanned by which tradable instruments.

A single quoted new option can add another independent payoff direction. Whether it completes the market depends on its payoff structure and on which states remain distinct. A derivative exactly equal to the stock plus a constant adds no new spanning power.

Method

Test attainability

  1. Write terminal vectors

    Use identical state order, payment dates and currency for cash, securities and the proposed claim.

  2. Solve the linear conditions

    Check whether the required holdings reproduce all nodes. Failure at one node suffices to reject a proposed replication.

  3. Examine price implications

    Try distinct positive state prices satisfying all traded-price equations, then value the new claim under each set.

Worked case

A payout for staying in range

With zero interest, a stock worth 10 can finish at 8, 10 or 12. A claim pays 4 only in the middle state. Assume all three outcomes have positive probability and stock and cash are the only traded assets.

Working

  1. For probabilities p₁, p₂, p₃, the stock equation 8p₁ + 10p₂ + 12p₃ = 10 and normalization imply p₁ = p₃ = a, p₂ = 1 − 2a.
  2. Strict positivity requires 0 < a < 0.5. The claim price 4(1 − 2a) can therefore take any value strictly between 0 and 4 while keeping stock and cash correctly priced.
  3. A direct hedge fails. Matching zero at both endpoint stock prices requires slope zero and cash zero, which gives zero in the middle instead of four.

Interpretation

Holding cash of 4 covers the payout and holding zero lies below it. The strict positivity of the middle and endpoint states excludes the two endpoint prices under no arbitrage.

A common mistake

An incomplete market gives total freedom to name any price for a new contingent payoff.

How to correct it

Traded prices and state dominance still constrain valuation. Derive the range from positive state weights or explicit hedge portfolios. State which additional pricing assumptions select a point.

Quick check
What does the disagreement between €2 and €6 demonstrate?

Both positive state-weight sets reproduce stock and cash prices. They disagree on the middle payoff, revealing a risk exposure the available stock and cash positions cannot span.

Written practice · 9 marks

Independent exam problem

Cash yields zero. A share costs 50 and finishes at 40, 50 or 60. One claim pays (0,2,4). Another pays (1,0,1).

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Show which claim is replicated by stock and cash and give its price.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Give two admissible prices for the second claim and verify your weights.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

The first equals 0.2S − 8 in all three states and therefore costs 0.2 × 50 − 8 = 2. Matching the two equal endpoints of the second claim forces stock holding zero and constant cash one, which fails in the middle.

Part B 4 marks

Both (0.2,0.6,0.2) and (0.4,0.2,0.4) are positive, sum to one and produce expected stock 50. They value the second claim at 0.4 and 0.8. Thus stock and cash alone do not select one.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How could two desks agree on every traded price and disagree on an unlisted claim without either creating arbitrage?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would compare the two desks’ prices only after checking that they use the same numeraire and agree on the traded payoff matrix. In this three-state example both systems price stock and cash identically. They give 0.6 and 0.2 for the middle-state digital because the market cannot isolate that state. A quoted independent digital could resolve the missing degree of freedom.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if a second listed security pays (4,5,6)?

Compare with the follow-up answer

It equals half the stock payoff (8,10,12) in every state. Its consistent price is five and its column adds no rank. It cannot determine the middle-state price, even though the number of named securities now equals the number of states.
