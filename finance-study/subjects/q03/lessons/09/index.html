Changing the measure

Under suitable assumptions, change the drift to price risk without changing the observed payoff definition. Underlying physical probabilities serve different empirical questions.

The starting point

An analyst forecasting how often prices rise and a trader pricing a replicable option can use different probabilities for the same possible future states. The physical measure describes beliefs or evidence about observed outcomes. A pricing measure reweights those states so traded gains, expressed in the chosen numeraire, have the required martingale property.

Changing the measure leaves each promised payoff unchanged. In standard diffusion models an equivalent measure change can alter drift while preserving local volatility. Risk premiums are reflected in the weighting, which is why averaging a risky payoff under ordinary probabilities and discounting it at the cash rate usually gives the wrong price.

Theory and method

Girsanov’s theorem and the market price of diffusion risk

Equivalent change of measure · Radon–Nikodym density

Under which sign convention does a positive market price of risk remove excess stock drift, and what ensures the new probability law exists?

A stock is modeled to grow faster than cash in investors’ physical forecast. To price hedgeable payoffs, the desk reweights paths so the cash-discounted stock becomes a martingale under suitable integrability. Girsanov gives the Brownian motion under this new measure. Paths and instantaneous volatility remain the same objects, while the conditional drift written under the new law changes. In multiple-factor markets, a cash-stock equation may constrain only some risk prices.

How the result is built

λ = (μ−r)/σ, Zₜ = exp(−λWₜᴾ−½λ²t), dWᵠ = dWᴾ + λdt

μ and r are annual stock drift and cash rate, σ annual volatility, λ diffusion-risk price per square root of year, Wᴾ and Wᵠ Brownian motions under physical and pricing laws P and Q, t years, and Zₜ the density dQ/dP up to t for constant λ.

  1. Choose λ so that μ − σλ = r. Substitute dWᴾ = dWᵠ − λdt into dS/S = μdt + σdWᴾ to get drift r.
  2. For constant λ, the normal exponential moment gives Eᴾ[exp(−λWₜᴾ)] = exp(½λ²t). The compensating exponential makes Eᴾ[Zₜ]=1.
  3. At μ=9%, r=3% and σ=20%, λ=0.30. At one year Z is exp(−0.30W − 0.045), which gives larger relative weight to down-shock states.

When the reasoning holds

  • For a time-varying adapted λ, use stochastic and time integrals in the exponential. Novikov’s condition E[exp(½∫λ²dt)] < ∞ is sufficient for the required true martingale density under standard assumptions.
  • Volatility must be nonzero to solve this one-factor equation. An additional unspanned Brownian factor can admit multiple consistent changes of measure.

Terms in this lesson
Martingale
A process whose expected future value, given current information, equals its current value under the specified probability measure.
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
Risk premium
Extra expected return for bearing risk, or the amount a risk-averse person would give up to replace a gamble with certainty, depending on context.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Discount factor
The price today of one unit of currency payable at a particular future date under specified risk assumptions.
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
How to read the notation
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.
Summation · ∑
The symbol asks you to add the terms for the listed items. For weights of 0.6 and 0.4 and returns of 5% and 2%, ∑ wᵢrᵢ means 0.6 × 5% + 0.4 × 2% = 3.8%. A standalone capital sigma (Σ) can also name a covariance matrix in portfolio equations.
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.
Time and random increments · dt, dW
Over a short interval Δt, a standard Brownian increment has mean zero and standard deviation √Δt. If a year is one time unit, a quarter-year increment has standard deviation 0.5. A drift term scales with elapsed time itself.

All notation examples

First example

A €100 share might reach €110 or €90 next period, and cash earns 2%. The pricing weight solves 110q + 90(1 − q) = 102, giving q = 0.60. A call paying €10 only if the share rises costs 6/1.02 ≈ €5.88, even if historical data suggest a 70% chance of a rise.

Reweight states without changing the payoff

An investor can use historical evidence to forecast stock outcomes while the option desk uses state prices implied by traded assets. The two measures attach different weights to the same possible paths. An equivalent measure preserves which events are impossible, while changing the weight assigned to events of positive probability.

For discrete states with physical probabilities pⱼ and desired pricing probabilities qⱼ, define likelihood ratios Lⱼ = qⱼ/pⱼ. They satisfy Eᴾ[L] = 1. Then Eᵠ[X] = ΣpⱼLⱼXⱼ = Eᴾ[LX]. The ratio changes the averaging rule without editing the payoff function X.

Discounting and risky outcomes

If a deterministic discount factor is D, the stochastic discount factor under the physical measure is Mⱼ = DLⱼ. An attainable claim costs Eᴾ[MX]. States providing insurance against other losses can carry a high state price relative to their physical probability.

Simply discounting Eᴾ[X] at the cash rate assumes Eᴾ[MX] = D Eᴾ[X]. That requires zero covariance of M and X under these conditions. A risky claim paying mainly in states with different valuation weights generally violates this requirement.

Translate the diffusion drift

For dS/S = μdt + σdWᴾ with constant nonzero σ, a cash-numeraire pricing measure for a non-dividend-paying stock has dS/S = rdt + σdWᵠ. Writing dWᵠ = dWᴾ + (μ − r)dt/σ shows how the drift shifts while local variance remains σ²dt.

The existence of a valid measure change requires mathematical conditions, and a single traded stock may not span additional risk drivers. A different forecast of μ can coexist with the same price of a replicable claim in the simple complete model.

Method

Build a finite-state change of measure

  1. Find both weight sets

    Estimate or specify physical probabilities, then derive pricing weights independently from traded prices and the cash account.

  2. Construct likelihood ratios

    Divide pricing weights by physical weights in each state and verify their physical expectation is one.

  3. Price the payoff

    Calculate either D Eᵠ[X] or Eᴾ[DLX] and compare. Use Eᴾ[X] separately when answering an outcome-forecast question.

Worked case

Market insurance across two states

Assume good and bad states have physical probabilities 0.80 and 0.20. Market-implied pricing weights are 0.60 and 0.40, and the one-period discount factor is 0.95. Insurance pays 100 only in the bad state.

Working

  1. Likelihood ratios are 0.60/0.80 = 0.75 and 0.40/0.20 = 2. Their physical mean is 0.8 × 0.75 + 0.2 × 2 = 1.
  2. The physical expected payoff is 20. The pricing expected payoff is 40, giving price 0.95 × 40 = 38.
  3. Under physical probabilities the same price is 0.20 × (0.95 × 2) × 100 = 38. The bad state’s stochastic discount factor is 1.90.

Interpretation

Insurance pays in precisely the state with extra valuation weight. Its high price compared with discounted physical expectation can reflect a price for risk.

A common mistake

Read a pricing-measure probability as the analyst’s historical event probability.

How to correct it

Identify how each probability was recovered and what decision it answers. Keep forecasting measures, state-price weights and quoted contract probabilities clearly labeled.

Quick check
Under the analyst’s physical estimate of 70% up, what is the call’s expected undiscounted payoff?

The physical expectation is 0.70 × €10. The €6 uses pricing weights, and approximately €5.88 is today’s replication-consistent price under the model.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

Physical up probability is 0.50. Pricing up probability is 0.30, discount factor 0.90, and a claim pays 10 up and 2 down.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Compute likelihood ratios and price using both measures.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Compare the discounted physical expected payoff. Explain the gap.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

Ratios are 0.6 up and 1.4 down. Under Q, price is 0.90(0.3 × 10 + 0.7 × 2) = 3.96. Under P, 0.90(0.5 × 0.6 × 10 + 0.5 × 1.4 × 2) also yields 3.96.

Part B 3 marks

The discounted physical mean is 0.90 × 6 = 5.40. The claim pays most in the up state, whose market valuation weight is lower than its physical probability. The physical mean alone overprices it under the stated weights.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Under which sign convention does a positive market price of risk remove excess stock drift, and what ensures the new probability law exists?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would define the sign convention first. Taking λ = (μ − r)/σ and adding λdt to the physical Brownian increment gives the pricing Brownian motion. Substitution subtracts σλ from the drift and leaves r. A valid density must have unit expectation and remain positive. For a stochastic λ I would check a sufficient condition such as Novikov’s or another argument establishing a true martingale.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If I reverse the sign in the density but leave the Brownian shift unchanged, can I still price this stock?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The density and shift would no longer correspond under Girsanov. Reversing the density’s sign requires reversing the Brownian shift or redefining the risk-price convention consistently. Check by substituting back into the stock equation and verifying the discounted drift vanishes.
