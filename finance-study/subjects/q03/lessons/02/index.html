Random walks and Brownian motion

Brownian increments have mean zero and variance equal to elapsed time. Typical movement grows with the square root of the time interval.

The starting point

Imagine shortening the time step of a fair random walk. If steps shrink like the square root of elapsed time, uncertainty over a fixed horizon remains finite. Standard Brownian motion is the continuous-time model that results in the usual scaling limit. It starts at zero and has continuous paths with independent, normally distributed increments.

Over an interval of length h a standard Brownian increment has mean zero and variance h. A constant multiplier σ scales its standard deviation to σ√h. Here h and σ must use matching time units. The square-root scaling describes dispersion under these assumptions, rather than a bound on realized moves.

Theory and method

Donsker’s invariance principle and Brownian scaling

Functional central limit theorem · Diffusive scaling

Why does quadrupling the number of independent trading intervals double the volatility under a diffusion model?

A market maker imagines an increasingly fine sequence of small independent surprises. A useful approximation preserves total risk over a year as the number of intervals changes. That requires each step’s size to shrink with the square root of its length. Donsker’s theorem extends the usual endpoint central limit idea to the shape of the entire scaled path, under stated assumptions. It motivates a Brownian approximation without asserting that observed markets actually follow one.

How the result is built

Wₙ(t) = [Z₁ + ⋯ + Z_floor(nt)]/√n ⇒ W(t), and SD[σ(W(t+h)−W(t))] = σ√h

n is the number of steps per year, t and h are years, floor(nt) counts elapsed steps, Zᵢ are independent identical standardized surprises, W is standard Brownian motion, ⇒ denotes convergence in distribution of paths, and σ is annual volatility.

  1. A standardized step has mean zero and variance one. Dividing it by √n gives variance 1/n.
  2. After approximately nh independent steps the variances add to h. The square root produces dispersion √h, and multiplying path increments by σ scales their dispersion by σ.
  3. At annual volatility 16%, horizons of 1/16, 1/4 and one year give standard deviations of 4%, 8% and 16%. A fourfold increase in horizon doubles the scale.

When the reasoning holds

  • The basic convergence theorem needs mean-zero independent identically distributed steps with finite variance after standardization. Infinite variance or long-range dependence can lead to different limits.
  • Constant underlying volatility and a consistent trading clock are needed for this simple numeric scaling. A scheduled announcement can concentrate substantial uncertainty into one interval.

How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
How to read the notation
Time and random increments · dt, dW
Over a short interval Δt, a standard Brownian increment has mean zero and standard deviation √Δt. If a year is one time unit, a quarter-year increment has standard deviation 0.5. A drift term scales with elapsed time itself.
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.
Subscripts · S₀ and S₁
A small index names the time or item. If S₀ = €40 and S₁ = €42, the price moves from €40 at time 0 to €42 at time 1. A subscript i can label a company instead of a date.

All notation examples

First example

A model gives a return shock annual volatility of 20%. Over one month, h = 1/12, so the shock’s standard deviation is 20%/√12 ≈ 5.77%. Over one quarter it is 20% × √0.25 = 10%.

Scale a sequence of fair bets

Take n equal steps across one year. If each shock is ±1 with equal probability, the sum has variance n. Scale each step by 1/√n and the sum has variance one for every n. Increasing the step count now improves time resolution while maintaining the same annual shock scale.

Under standard assumptions the scaled walks converge in distribution as processes to standard Brownian motion. The limiting process has continuous paths, independent increments and normal increments with mean zero and variance equal to elapsed time. Its paths are highly irregular despite continuity.

Use the increment distribution

For times t < T, Wₜ is already known at t. The increment Wₜ₊ₕ − Wₜ is an independent normal draw with mean zero and variance h. Thus the conditional mean remains Wₜ, but the conditional standard deviation grows as √h.

For Xₜ = X₀ + at + σWₜ, over h years the increment has mean ah and variance σ²h. Drift accumulates proportionally to time and standard deviation grows with the square root. Halving a horizon therefore affects drift and dispersion differently.

Know the model’s boundaries

Brownian increments have continuous distributions with unbounded support, even over short intervals. A two-standard-deviation move is possible, and a larger one is possible as well. The model excludes jumps and assumes conditional independence and a stable variance scale for its standardized increments.

Data can exhibit volatility clustering, price limits and market closures. A Friday-to-Monday holding period may need an empirically chosen risk clock. The mathematical square-root rule follows model time and requires careful interpretation when applied to observed calendar time.

Method

Convert a horizon

  1. Set the clock

    Express h in years when σ is quoted per square root of a year.

  2. Transform the distribution

    Calculate conditional mean ah and standard deviation σ√h, then convert a proposed change into a standardized shock.

  3. Check independence

    When combining adjacent intervals, add variances only when cross-covariance terms vanish under the model.

Worked case

Quarter-year price increments

A local arithmetic model for a price change uses drift €4 per year and volatility €12 per square root of a year. Consider a quarter-year horizon with constant coefficients.

Working

  1. The expected change is 4 × 0.25 = €1 and its standard deviation is 12 × √0.25 = €6. The variance is 36 squared euros.
  2. A change of +€7 is one standard deviation above the mean, since (7 − 1)/6 = 1. Under the normal model its upper-tail probability is about 15.9%.
  3. Two adjoining quarters have expected total change €2 and standard deviation √(36 + 36) ≈ €8.49, provided increments are independent.

Interpretation

An arithmetic price model can assign negative prices after sufficiently large drops. Its scale calculation does not establish that it suits a nonnegative share price for long horizons.

A common mistake

Multiply daily standard deviations by the number of days when aggregating independent shocks.

How to correct it

Variances add for independent increments. Sum squared daily scales and take a square root. If shocks are correlated, include their covariance contributions before taking that root.

Quick check
Under the same constant-volatility Brownian model, how does shock standard deviation change when the horizon grows from one month to four?

Variance is proportional to elapsed time. Four times the horizon gives four times the variance and √4 = 2 times the standard deviation.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

An arithmetic return model uses annual drift 8% and annual volatility 24%. Assume independent Brownian increments.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    For a quarter, give the mean, standard deviation and standardized value corresponding to a −10% return.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    If adjacent quarters’ realized shocks had positive conditional correlation, why might the independent-scale calculation understate half-year risk?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Mean = 8% × 0.25 = 2%, standard deviation = 24% × 0.5 = 12%. The standardized move is (−10% − 2%)/12% = −1.

Part B 3 marks

The sum’s variance includes twice the covariance. Positive covariance adds to the two quarterly variances and implies a greater standard deviation than the independent calculation.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why does quadrupling the number of independent trading intervals double the volatility under a diffusion model?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

The independent increments add variance. Four intervals each with variance v have combined variance 4v and standard deviation 2√v. Donsker supplies a pathwise limit after appropriate scaling of many small shocks. For an actual book I would test whether there is serial dependence or a scheduled event before using that approximation for a longer holding period.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Does the same rule hold when adjacent daily surprises have positive covariance?

Compare with the follow-up answer

For two days the sum has variance v₁ + v₂ + 2Cov₁₂. Positive covariance raises the total above the independent calculation. Use the covariance structure and changing daily variance estimates to calculate the holding-period risk.
