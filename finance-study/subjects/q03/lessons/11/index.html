Price a jump exposure without double-counting its drift

Convert an arrival intensity and mean jump into a compensating risk-neutral drift.

Theory and terminology

Risk-neutral jump-diffusion pricing

compensated jump process · Merton jump diffusion

What you should be able to explain

  • Convert an arrival intensity and mean jump into a compensating risk-neutral drift.
  • Price a contingent payoff from jump-count probabilities and use the discounted stock to check the sign.

Two kinds of uncertainty

In a diffusion, the price moves continuously and its small movements can still accumulate substantial risk. A jump model adds discontinuous events. A Poisson count N over a horizon T has probability exp(−λT)(λT)ⁿ/n! of n events, for constant arrival intensity λ. Independent event sizes multiply the pre-event stock by a positive random number J. A fall of 20% means J=0.80 and relative jump J−1=−0.20.

A compound Poisson process combines stochastic timing and jump sizes. Adding Brownian fluctuations between events gives a jump-diffusion. Merton’s familiar specification uses normally distributed log jump sizes, independent of Brownian shocks and event counts. The fixed-size example below isolates the effect of jump compensation. A general Lévy model can have infinitely many small jumps and needs a different representation.

Compensate the expected discontinuity

Over a short interval dt, a jump arrives with probability about λdt. Its expected fractional stock move is κ=E[J−1]. The jump contribution to expected growth is therefore λκdt. Under the chosen risk-neutral law for a non-dividend-paying stock, the expected growth of the entire stock must equal rdt, where r is the risk-free rate.

Consequently its between-jump drift coefficient is r−λκ. When jumps are negative on average, κ is negative and subtracting it raises between-jump drift. The same statement can be expressed by subtracting the mean from the jump component and leaving total drift r. Using both a compensated jump component and the adjusted drift would count the adjustment twice.

Price state by state and check a security with a known price

Conditional on a jump count, the terminal stock contains n jump multipliers and the growth between events. With no Brownian term and constant jump size, the count determines the terminal price exactly. With Brownian motion or random jump sizes the conditional payoff must itself be averaged. Finally weight conditional payoffs by risk-neutral count probabilities and discount.

An observed historical frequency need not equal the pricing intensity for jump-sensitive claims. With Brownian risk and unspanned random jump sizes, a stock delta that offsets infinitesimal diffusion moves generally leaves jump exposure. Stock and money-market prices alone need not identify the pricing law. State the parameter source and stress both intensity and size.

Method

Build the result

Sₜ=S₀ exp[(r−λκ−σ²/2)t+σWₜ] × ∏ⱼJⱼ; V₀=exp(−rT) Eᴽ[g(S_T)]

S₀ and Sₜ are prices at dates 0 and t, with T the option horizon in years and S_T denoting the stock at expiry. r and λ are measured per year. Volatility σ is per square-root year and Wₜ is pricing-measure Brownian motion. The product covers jumps by t. Q is the pricing measure and g the payoff.

  1. Conditional on n independent jumps, the mean product of multipliers is E[J]ⁿ. Averaging over Poisson probabilities gives exp[λt(E[J]−1)]=exp(λκt).
  2. The independent Brownian exponential has mean one after its −σ²t/2 adjustment. The remaining expected growth is exp[(r−λκ)t]exp(λκt)=exp(rt).
  3. Discount the result to recover S₀. For a nonlinear g, apply g to the state price first and then average the cash payoffs.
Worked case

A one-year call when event days can knock 20% off the stock

Spot is 100, strike 100, continuously compounded r=3%, risk-neutral λ=0.5 per year and every jump multiplies price by 0.80. Set σ=0 and assume no dividends. The possible counts are 0, 1, 2, and so on.

Working

  1. κ=−0.20, so the continuous growth coefficient between jumps is 0.03−0.5×(−0.20)=0.13 per year.
  2. With zero jumps the stock ends at 100 exp(0.13)=113.883. With one it ends at 91.106. Any further jump lowers it again. Only the zero-jump state pays, delivering 13.883.
  3. The chance of zero jumps is exp(−0.5)=0.60653. Present call value is exp(−0.03)×0.60653×13.88284=8.1715 per share.
  4. For the stock, average all counts using E[0.8ᴺ]=exp(−0.1). Its expected terminal value is 100 exp(0.13−0.10)=103.045, discounted back to 100.

Decision and interpretation

The stock check catches an incorrect sign before the call enters the book. The call is sensitive to the assumed frequency and severity of events, which would need separate calibration.

Quick check
A non-dividend-paying stock has r=4%, λ=0.4 per year and a mean relative jump of −10% under Q. What is the between-jump drift coefficient?

Expected jump growth is −4% per year. Adding it to a between-jump drift of 8% gives the required total of 4%.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

Spot is 80. A one-year cash digital pays 12 if the terminal stock exceeds 80. Under Q, upward jumps have J=1.10, λ=0.25 per year, r=2%, σ=0 and no dividends. Price the digital.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked solution
  1. The mean relative jump is +0.10. Between events the drift is 0.02−0.25×0.10=−0.005 per year.
  2. No jumps give 80 exp(−0.005)=79.601 and one jump gives 87.561. Any positive count therefore triggers the payment.
  3. The event probability is 1−exp(−0.25)=0.22120. Value is 12 exp(−0.02)×0.22120=2.602.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

If the stock can drop suddenly, why might its model have to grow faster between jumps under the pricing measure?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Expected downward jumps subtract from total expected return. For a stock without dividends, its expected growth under the money-market pricing measure must equal the short rate. I increase the between-jump growth enough to offset the expected jump loss, then check the discounted expected stock against spot.

Interviewer’s follow-up

How would the valuation change if positive Brownian volatility were introduced?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The count would cease to determine whether the strike is crossed. Conditional on each count, calculate the probability of finishing above the threshold under the Brownian movement, then weight all conditional probabilities by their jump-count probabilities.
