Quadratic variation

Squared Brownian increments accumulate over time. That surviving second-order effect explains why a curvature term appears in Itô’s formula.

The starting point

A smooth curve moves about proportionally to a very short time step. Brownian motion moves on the larger square-root-of-time scale. Squaring each tiny Brownian increment therefore produces a contribution on the scale of the time step itself. These contributions survive when added over many small intervals.

Quadratic variation is the limiting sum of those squared increments as a partition becomes finer. For standard Brownian motion it equals elapsed time. For a constant multiple σW it equals σ² times elapsed time. Quadratic variation measures accumulated local movement even when signed moves cancel by the end.

Theory and method

Quadratic covariation and the polarization identity

Realized covariance · Quadratic variation

Can you infer the covariance contribution between two hedge legs from their individual and combined realized variance?

Consider a desk that observes two synchronized return series and the returns of their sum. When both legs rise on the same dates the combined squared changes tend to exceed the individual squared changes. When their moves offset, the difference can be negative. The cross term in the square of a sum lets the desk extract covariation from these three measured series. The exact algebra holds on each grid before any continuous-time limit.

How the result is built

[X,Y]ₜ = ½([X+Y]ₜ − [X]ₜ − [Y]ₜ)

X and Y denote cumulative return processes in decimal units, t is the common end date, [X] and [Y] are their quadratic variations, and [X,Y] is quadratic covariation in decimal-return-squared units.

  1. At each observation expand (ΔX + ΔY)² = (ΔX)² + 2ΔXΔY + (ΔY)².
  2. Sum over identical intervals, subtract the individual squared sums and divide by two. The result equals the sum of synchronized increment products.
  3. If the individual sums are 0.012 and 0.020 and the combined sum is 0.024, cross-variation is (0.024 − 0.012 − 0.020)/2 = −0.004. It reduces combined variation by 0.008.

When the reasoning holds

  • Use synchronous time intervals and consistent return scales. Stale prices can shift moves into different intervals and distort an empirical estimate.
  • The continuous-time interpretation needs an appropriate process and refining observation partitions. For jump processes simultaneous jumps also contribute products of their sizes.

How to read the notation
Time and random increments · dt, dW
Over a short interval Δt, a standard Brownian increment has mean zero and standard deviation √Δt. If a year is one time unit, a quarter-year increment has standard deviation 0.5. A drift term scales with elapsed time itself.
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.
Subscripts · S₀ and S₁
A small index names the time or item. If S₀ = €40 and S₁ = €42, the price moves from €40 at time 0 to €42 at time 1. A subscript i can label a company instead of a date.

All notation examples

First example

Divide one year into 100 equal intervals. Each standard Brownian increment has expected square 0.01, making the expected sum 1. With 1,000 intervals the expected square is 0.001 and the expected sum remains 1. One simulated finite sample can fall above or below that value.

Small squares survive aggregation

For a deterministic straight line Xₜ = at on a uniform partition of [0,T], the sum of squared steps equals n(aT/n)² = a²T²/n, which tends to zero. Brownian increments, by contrast, have standard deviation √(T/n), so their expected squared sum stays at T.

Quadratic variation is the limit of these sums under an increasingly fine partition in the relevant convergence sense. It records locally accumulated fluctuation, which may be considerable even when the endpoint returns to its starting value.

Show convergence for Brownian motion

On a uniform grid a standard Brownian increment is normal with variance Δt. Its squared increment has variance 2(Δt)² because a standard normal has fourth moment 3. Independent increments therefore give a squared-sum variance of n × 2(T/n)² = 2T²/n.

The squared sum has mean T and variance tending to zero. This proves convergence in mean square, hence in probability, to T. For X with local Brownian coefficient bₜ and regular finite-variation drift, the continuous quadratic variation is ∫bₜ²dt under standard assumptions.

Covariation and jumps

Covariation sums products of two processes’ changes on matching intervals. If two Brownian drivers have instantaneous correlation ρ, the covariation of constant-volatility returns over T is ρσ₁σ₂T. It enters the cross-derivative term in multivariate Itô calculus.

Jumps also contribute their squared size to quadratic variation. Very high frequency observed prices can contain bid–ask bounce and measurement noise, so a realized sum from transaction prices may differ from the idealized latent process limit.

Method

Distinguish path movement from endpoint change

  1. Define the grid

    Use returns on the same chosen time grid, in consistent price or log-return units.

  2. Accumulate squares

    Square each increment before summing. For cross-variation multiply matching increments of two paths.

  3. Interpret the limit

    Identify continuous variance, possible jumps and observation noise. Compare across sampling frequencies when interpreting market data.

Worked case

Same endpoint, different path

Two sample log-price paths both start and end at zero over four intervals. Their increments are (+0.02, −0.02, +0.02, −0.02) and (+0.04, −0.04, 0, 0). Consider the finite-grid squared sums.

Working

  1. Each path has cumulative log change zero, so squaring the total change gives zero for both.
  2. The first path’s sum of squared increments is 4 × 0.02² = 0.0016. The second’s is 2 × 0.04² = 0.0032.
  3. The second displays twice as much measured squared movement on this grid. Neither finite sequence alone demonstrates a Brownian limit or reveals unobserved movement between grid times.

Interpretation

A variance-linked payoff can therefore distinguish the paths even though a security whose payoff depends only on the final price cannot.

A common mistake

Set every individual Brownian increment squared equal to its elapsed time in a finite simulation.

How to correct it

That equality belongs to accumulated quadratic variation in the limit. Finite increments have random squares. Use their distribution when simulating, and use the limit only in the appropriate calculus.

Quick check
Under this idealized model, what is the quadratic variation of 0.30W over two years?

Scaling the process by 0.30 scales squared increments by 0.30². Multiplying by the two-year interval gives 0.09 × 2 = 0.18.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

A diffusion has volatility 0.2 for half a year and 0.4 for the remaining half. A separate instantaneous log-price jump of −0.10 occurs once.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Compute continuous quadratic variation.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Add the jump contribution and explain why its sign does not subtract.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Integrating squared volatility over time gives 0.2² × 0.5 + 0.4² × 0.5 = 0.10. Adding volatilities before squaring would ignore changing exposures.

Part B 3 marks

A jump contributes its squared size, 0.10² = 0.01, bringing total quadratic variation to 0.11. Squared path variation records magnitude, regardless of the direction of the jump.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Can you infer the covariance contribution between two hedge legs from their individual and combined realized variance?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Yes, provided the inputs refer to the same grid, units and linear combination. I would subtract the two individual squared sums from the sum for the combined legs and divide by two. Here the answer is −0.004. With arbitrary position weights I would multiply this by twice the product of the weights to determine its contribution to portfolio variance.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Would summing the two separately computed daily log returns give the log return on a 50/50 equity portfolio?

Compare with the follow-up answer

For each discrete interval first combine gross asset returns with the beginning portfolio weights, then take the portfolio logarithm if needed. The polarization identity is exact for the specified arithmetic linear combination of increments, which must correspond to the actual quantity being analyzed.
