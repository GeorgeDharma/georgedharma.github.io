Fund economics

Distinguish the operating company, the fund and the fund’s investors. Fees, carried interest and the timing of capital calls change investor returns.

The starting point

The operating company makes and sells products. A private investment fund owns stakes in companies. Investors in the fund promise capital and supply cash when the manager calls it. A company’s gross investment return can differ substantially from the cash its fund investors finally receive.

Management fees pay for running the fund. Carried interest is a manager’s contractual share of eligible profits. The agreement defines when it is earned and in what order cash is distributed. Write the actual payment sequence before calculating any investor return.

Theory and method

Private-fund distribution waterfall with GP catch-up

Preferred return · Carried interest · Catch-up tier

A fund pays an 8% preferred return, then a 100% GP catch-up. Does the manager simply receive 20% of everything above the preference?

Investors supply €100m and prefer that managers share in profits only after capital and an agreed return have been delivered. A catch-up tier can give the general partner all of the next cash until the wider profit split reaches its contractual target. The early preference then changes the sequence and downside exposure without necessarily permanently excluding that preferred amount from the manager’s eventual profit share. Read the agreement and the amount of cash actually available at every tier.

How the result is built

Full catch-up C = cH/(1 − c)

H is preferred profit already paid to investors, c the general partner’s target fraction of cumulative eligible profit, and C the further cash required for a complete 100% catch-up. Capital returned is excluded from eligible profit.

  1. After €100m capital is returned, pay investors their €8m preferred profit. To reach a 20% share of cumulative profit for the manager, solve C/(€8m + C) = 20%.
  2. The full catch-up is €2m. With €130m exit cash, €20m remains after €100m capital, €8m preference and €2m catch-up.
  3. Split the last €20m 80/20. Investors receive €124m and the manager €6m. Check €6m equals 20% of €30m total profit after a full catch-up.

When the reasoning holds

  • This is a full 100% catch-up with one fixed preferred amount and a one-year simplified example. Other agreements cap, omit or phase the catch-up.
  • Only available proceeds can be distributed. At a €109m exit the manager receives €1m partial catch-up, not the €2m theoretical full tier.
Terms in this lesson
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
First example

A fund calls €10m to invest and €1m in fees. It exits for €16m. Its simplified agreement repays €11m of contributed capital first, then gives the manager 20% of remaining profits. Carry is €1m and investors receive €15m, about 1.36 times their €11m contribution.

Follow money from investors to companies

Fund investors promise a commitment. A capital call requests part of that commitment for an investment, fees or other permitted uses. Uncalled capital remains an obligation but has not yet been paid into the fund. A portfolio company’s sale price belongs first to the fund, which then allocates the cash under its agreement.

General partners or managers operate the fund, while limited partners generally supply much of its capital. A management fee pays for running the fund. Carried interest gives the manager a contractual share of eligible profits. Ownership of a portfolio company and the manager’s right to carry are separate claims.

Read the order of distribution

A waterfall specifies who receives cash first. A simplified fund may return all contributed capital, then pay a preferred return or hurdle to investors, then share incremental profits. Some agreements include a catch-up paying the manager a larger share over an intermediate range. Never infer those details from a headline carry rate.

Fund-wide waterfalls assess all investments together. A deal-by-deal waterfall can pay carry on an early winner before later losses appear, potentially requiring a later repayment under a clawback. Distinguish cash distributed now from the ultimate entitlement under the actual agreement.

Why gross returns can look very different

A gross deal multiple usually compares investment proceeds with capital placed in the company. A net investor multiple includes cash actually contributed for investment and expenses, and distributions after carry. Timing of capital calls and distributions also changes annualized returns even with the same eventual multiple.

An ongoing fund may report remaining asset values. Cash returned divided by paid-in capital measures realized distributions. Adding remaining net asset value estimates total value relative to cash paid in. Report both where a large share of the result still depends on uncertain valuations.

Method

Trace a fund waterfall

  1. Build dated cash flows

    List commitment, called investment capital, called fees, interim distributions and residual asset values separately. Keep the investors’ perspective consistent going down the schedule.

  2. Apply tiers in order

    Return capital and apply any specified hurdle, catch-up and sharing percentages in the contractual sequence. Cap each tier at the remaining available proceeds.

  3. Reconcile the investors

    Calculate investor cash returned and manager receipts separately. Their combined cash distributions must equal distributable exit cash, and uncalled commitments do not belong in the paid-in denominator.

Worked case

A small fund exits its last asset

Investors commit €30m, contribute €20m for investments and €2m for fees. The exits yield €32m cash. The simplified agreement returns all €22m paid in, pays investors a fixed €2m preferred amount, then grants the manager 25% of any remainder. There is no catch-up.

Working

  1. The gross investment multiple is €32m/€20m = 1.60. The first two waterfall tiers return €24m to investors, leaving €8m to share.
  2. Carry is €2m and investors receive the other €6m, for €30m investor distributions. The distributor checks €30m + €2m = €32m.
  3. The net investor multiple is €30m/€22m = 1.364. Subtracting the fees again from the €30m would count the expenditure twice because they were already paid in separately.

Interpretation

The €30m commitment equals the investor distribution by coincidence. A commitment is a legal funding ceiling, not the amount actually paid in to earn this return.

A common mistake

Calculating carry as a percentage of the whole sale price or dividing investor returns by unused commitments.

How to correct it

Read the profit tiers first and trace real contributions. Report clearly whether fees have already been paid or remain due from distributable proceeds.

Quick check
A fund returns €25m before carry. The agreement first returns €20m of contributed capital and gives 20% of remaining profits to the manager. With no other fees, what do investors receive?

The profit pool is €5m. The manager receives €1m, leaving the investors their original €20m plus €4m of profit.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

Investors call €12m for shares and €1m for fees. The exit pays €19m. Return €13m first, then a fixed €1m preferred amount, then split the remainder 80% investor and 20% manager.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate carry, investor distributions and net multiple.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Compare the gross deal multiple and explain two sources of difference.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

€5m remains after the first €14m. Carry is €1m. Investors receive €18m and their net multiple is €18m/€13m = 1.385.

Part B 3 marks

Gross deal multiple is €19m/€12m = 1.583. The net denominator includes the €1m fees, and the net numerator excludes €1m carry. Timing would also matter to an annualized measure.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A fund pays an 8% preferred return, then a 100% GP catch-up. Does the manager simply receive 20% of everything above the preference?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would calculate the tiers in order. The €8m preference goes to investors first. The manager then needs €2m because €2m out of €10m cumulative eligible profit equals 20%. On a €130m exit the last €20m splits 80/20, so carry totals €6m. At a weaker exit there may be too little cash for the catch-up tier to reach its target.

Interviewer’s follow-up

With €109m to distribute, compare returns under this agreement and one without a catch-up that splits only above the preference.

Compare with the follow-up answer

With full catch-up terms the first €108m goes to investors and the last €1m to the manager. With no catch-up, that remaining €1m splits €0.8m to investors and €0.2m to the manager. The difference illustrates why a headline 20% carry rate cannot price the contract alone.
