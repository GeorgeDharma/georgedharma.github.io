Opportunities and screening

Private markets have limited public information and uncertain exit routes. Connect the screening process to adverse selection and verification costs.

The starting point

A founder generally knows more about the business than a prospective investor. Adverse selection is the risk that firms most eager to accept the investor’s terms are disproportionately those with less attractive prospects. A persuasive pitch and a fast-growing market cannot resolve that information gap.

Screen in stages. An early test should be inexpensive and capable of changing the decision. For a subscription company, verify a sample of contracts and cash receipts, examine renewals and compare customers acquired in different months. A valuable product can still be an unattractive investment at the offered price.

Theory and method

Bayesian screening under adverse selection

Posterior probability · Positive predictive value

A product test identifies 90% of strong startups. Why might fewer than half the companies passing it actually be strong?

A venture investor sees a platform’s “verified” label on a company. The label’s value depends both on how reliably it detects strong businesses and on how often weak companies also pass. If strong companies are rare in the screened pipeline, even a modest false-positive rate can produce many weak companies among the passes. Use the applicant pool as the starting population and update the investment decision with the observed result and the costs of being wrong.

How the result is built

Pr(strong given pass) = sπ/[sπ + f(1 − π)]

π is the prior fraction of strong applicants, s the probability a strong company passes, and f the probability a weak company passes. All are probabilities between zero and one.

  1. Out of 1,000 applicants, a 10% prior gives 100 strong and 900 weak. At 90% sensitivity, 90 strong pass. At a 15% weak-company pass rate, another 135 pass.
  2. Among all 225 passes only 90 are strong. Conditional probability is 90/225 = 40%, lower than the test’s 90% sensitivity.
  3. If investing after a pass gains €4m when strong and loses €2m when weak, expected gain is 0.40 × €4m − 0.60 × €2m = €0.40m before diligence cost.

When the reasoning holds

  • The prevalence and test accuracy must describe the population currently applying. Referrals from a strong technical partner may have a different prior.
  • Test outcomes must relate to the economically relevant state. Detecting repeat logins can be a poor proxy for durable paid renewals.
Terms in this lesson
Adverse selection
A contract can attract a different mix of applicants when people know more about their own quality or risk than the other side knows.
Moral hazard
A contract can change behaviour after it is signed when the other party cannot fully observe or control those actions.
How to read the notation
Subscripts · S₀ and S₁
A small index names the time or item. If S₀ = €40 and S₁ = €42, the price moves from €40 at time 0 to €42 at time 1. A subscript i can label a company instead of a date.
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.

All notation examples

First example

A software seller claims 100 paying customers. Testing ten randomly selected contracts reveals two unpaid pilots counted as subscribers. This flags a definition problem and justifies expanding the test. It does not prove exactly 20 of the 100 are unpaid.

Why screening a private company is costly

Public prices and audited disclosures may be limited for a young business. Founders know more about their customers, product reliability and financing needs than outside investors. Adverse selection arises before investment when unattractive prospects may be especially willing to sell at a price based on the investor’s average expectations.

After funding, incentives can change effort, risk taking or disclosure. This is moral hazard. Screening before a deal and monitoring after it address different problems. A rigorous investment proposal identifies what information genuinely changes the estimate and what behaviour a contract can influence afterward.

Separate traction from an attractive investment

Screen market demand, ability to deliver, unit economics and ownership of the product. A growing company can still destroy cash if the cost of acquiring and serving each customer exceeds the contribution it will produce over its lifetime. Historical results need a defensible link to future retained customers.

Then assess price, terms, future funding and exit. A company with valuable technology can be overpriced if the present investment buys too little of the future proceeds or junior rights with weak protection. A staged cheque can buy time for learning, but can also leave a good project underfunded.

Use evidence that can change the decision

Start with an inexpensive, discriminating test such as matching a sample of customer contracts to invoices, cash and renewals. Sampling only the founder’s favourite accounts introduces selection bias. Sampling randomly estimates a population more credibly, while deliberately testing unusual cases exposes hidden failure modes.

The expected value of an information exercise is the gain from making a better decision after the information arrives, less its cost. An impressive report is worthless to the decision if every possible outcome would lead to exactly the same action and terms. State the decision rule before commissioning it.

Method

Design a screening test

  1. State the uncertainty

    Phrase the risk as competing hypotheses with distinct observable implications. Identify which outcome would change price, financing amount, terms or the decision to invest.

  2. Acquire independent evidence

    Specify a representative population for estimating prevalence and purposeful samples for investigating mechanisms. Obtain documents outside the dashboard where feasible.

  3. Update the capital decision

    Compare expected value before and after the test, including verification cost, the probability of each result and the ability to walk away or renegotiate.

Worked case

A pilot can prevent a bad investment

Investing now costs €4m. The investor’s stake would be worth €8m in a strong product and €2m in a weak one. Each state has 50% probability. A perfect independent pilot costing €0.5m reveals the state before the irrevocable investment. Ignore time value.

Working

  1. Without testing, expected stake value is €5m, so the investment has €1m expected net value. Walking away immediately gives zero.
  2. With the pilot, invest only if strong. Expected gain before the pilot fee is 50% × (€8m − €4m) = €2m. After the €0.5m cost the decision is worth €1.5m.
  3. The pilot improves expected value by €0.5m versus investing immediately. Its maximum economic price is €1m, the difference between €2m informed value and €1m uninformed value.

Interpretation

A real pilot can misclassify customers or delay entry. Lower signal quality or a lost opportunity reduces the fee justified by this idealized comparison.

A common mistake

Comparing the cost of diligence with the total size of the investment instead of the value of the decision it can improve.

How to correct it

Model how the investment action changes after each possible test result. Compare that policy with the best feasible action without the test.

Quick check
A startup reports that every customer renews. Which early test gives the investor the most informative independent evidence?

Renewal is a claim about specific customers over time. Matching contracts and payments checks the claim more directly than traffic or management assurances.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A €3m investment produces a stake worth €6m or €1m, equally likely. A perfect test costs €0.4m and leaves the investor free to decline.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate the uninformed and tested investment values.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    How much could an imperfect test be worth?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Uninformed expected net value is (€6m + €1m)/2 − €3m = €0.5m. With testing, invest only in the good state for 0.5 × €3m − €0.4m = €1.1m net.

Part B 3 marks

A perfect test improves value before its own fee by €1.5m − €0.5m = €1m. An imperfect test cannot exceed that benchmark with the same actions and costs. Estimate false positives and negatives before quoting a bid.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A product test identifies 90% of strong startups. Why might fewer than half the companies passing it actually be strong?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would separate sensitivity from the probability I really care about as an investor. The test catches 90 strong companies, but it also passes 135 weak companies because weak applicants are much more common. Only 40% of passes are strong. With a €4m upside and €2m downside that still produces €0.4m expected value per pass before fees. I would base the next decision on that figure and further evidence.

Interviewer’s follow-up

A different referral route has 25% strong companies with the same test accuracy. How much does a pass change the case?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The posterior is 0.90 × 0.25 divided by 0.90 × 0.25 + 0.15 × 0.75, giving two-thirds. Expected gain on the stated payoff becomes two-thirds × €4m minus one-third × €2m = €2m. Confirm that test performance transfers to the new channel.
