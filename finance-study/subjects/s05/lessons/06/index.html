Buyout sources and uses

Reconcile purchase consideration, fees and refinancing with new debt and sponsor equity. Keep transaction costs and ongoing operating effects distinct.

The starting point

A leveraged buyout funds an acquisition with both equity and debt. A sources-and-uses table must balance at closing. Uses include cash for selling shareholders, existing debt that must be refinanced, fees and any initial cash required to operate. Sources identify who supplies each euro.

Keep enterprise and equity prices straight. If an operating-business price already includes the value attributable to existing lenders, first translate it to the sellers’ equity proceeds. Adding the full operating price and all existing debt as separate uses can count that debt twice.

Theory and method

Reverse leveraged-buyout valuation

Financial sponsor bid ceiling · Target equity return

A private-equity sponsor requires a 20% return. How do you work backward from an exit to the operating price it can afford today?

A sponsor expects a buyer to pay a multiple of earnings several years from now. Debt still outstanding at the sale takes priority, leaving exit equity proceeds. Discount those proceeds at the sponsor’s required return to find the maximum initial equity cheque. Add closing borrowing available and subtract fees to infer an entry enterprise-price ceiling under explicit cash and debt assumptions. The ceiling can be below standalone fundamental value simply because this sponsor needs a different return or financing structure.

How the result is built

Entry EV ceiling = (mₓEₓ − Dₓ)/(1 + h)ⁿ + D₀ − F

mₓ is exit enterprise multiple, Eₓ exit annual EBITDA, Dₓ exit net debt, h annual sponsor hurdle, n holding years, D₀ entry financing and F one-time entry fees. All money is in euros. Target is acquired cash-free and debt-free.

  1. Exit EBITDA €12m at 7 times gives €84m enterprise value. Less €20m net debt leaves €64m equity for the sponsor.
  2. At a 20% hurdle for three years, initial sponsor equity cannot exceed €64m/1.20³ = €37.037m if there are no intermediate cash flows.
  3. With €30m new debt and €2m fees the maximum entry enterprise price is €37.037m + €30m − €2m = €65.037m. Fees consume funding capacity without buying operations.

When the reasoning holds

  • Exit debt must be rebuilt from cash generation and loan terms. Higher entry debt does not increase the bid dollar for dollar if more debt remains at exit.
  • The sponsor owns all exit equity and makes one contribution and receives one distribution. Interim capital or dividends require discounting every dated equity cash flow.

Terms in this lesson
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Leveraged buyout (LBO)
A purchase funded partly with debt, usually with the acquired business’s cash helping repay it.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
Settlement
The final transfer of cash or securities needed to complete a transaction.
Revenue
Amount earned by selling goods or services. The customer may pay later.
Enterprise value
Value of the business operations attributable to its capital providers. A bridge adjusts it to the amount due to shareholders.
First example

An operating business is priced at €70m, with €20m existing debt and no cash. Sellers receive €50m. Repaying the old debt and paying €3m fees takes total uses to €73m. If a new loan supplies €40m, the sponsor must supply €33m.

Where the closing cash goes

A leveraged buyout combines sponsor equity with borrowing to acquire a business. The sources-and-uses table is a cash settlement for closing. It answers who provides each euro and who receives it. The operating company’s income statement answers a different question about future revenues and costs.

If a price is quoted for enterprise value, first convert it to equity consideration using the old debt, available cash and other specified items. If the old debt must be refinanced, paying that lender is a use. Paying both full enterprise value to the seller and full old debt to the lender generally counts the same value twice.

Be precise about target cash

Target cash can increase the price sellers receive if it is delivered with the company. The same eligible cash may then help fund closing if the transaction permits a cash sweep. These are two sides of a consistent cash bridge. Cash that must remain to fund operations cannot also pay the seller.

Fees, taxes and minimum cash requirements often create the balancing sponsor equity need. If the existing loan stays outstanding, show clearly whether it is assumed or refinanced. Counting it as a new funding inflow when no money moves obscures closing liquidity and can distort leverage.

Evaluate leverage after the table balances

A balancing table proves the cash adds up, not that the borrower can service the new loan. Compare gross and net debt to sustainable operating earnings, interest coverage and the cash left after necessary investment. Use closing balances after any cash sweep and fees.

A sponsor may prefer debt because less sponsor equity is initially at risk, but the loan has priority over equity and can force a restructuring in a downside. Ask how marginal changes in fees, available debt or the minimum cash cushion change the required equity cheque.

Method

Assemble the transaction settlement

  1. Compute shareholder proceeds

    Start from the agreed operating price and translate it to the exact amount paid for the shares. Show whether existing cash is delivered and old debt is paid or assumed.

  2. List real closing uses and sources

    Enter seller proceeds, actual refinancings, fees and cash top-ups. Separate permitted cash sweep, new borrowings and sponsor equity, with clear opening and closing cash.

  3. Balance and stress

    Solve sponsor equity as remaining uses less available sources. Recalculate a case with reduced debt or unavailable target cash and then check post-close debt service.

Worked case

An acquisition with a minimum cash floor

A target has €75m enterprise value, €18m old debt and €6m cash. Sellers receive equity value including all cash. The old loan must be repaid. At least €2m of the target’s cash must remain after closing. Fees are €3m and the new loan provides €40m.

Working

  1. Equity consideration is €75m − €18m + €6m = €63m. Uses are €63m sellers, €18m old lenders and €3m fees, totaling €84m.
  2. Only €4m of target cash may be swept. Combined with €40m new debt, other sources supply €44m. Sponsor equity must cover €40m.
  3. Post-close gross debt is €40m and cash is €2m, so net debt is €38m. If the lender offers only €36m, sponsor equity must rise to €44m if other terms stay fixed.

Interpretation

An alternative presentation may add the full cash sweep as a source and a separate €2m cash replenishment as a use. The result should match as long as both sides are counted consistently.

A common mistake

Treating all target cash as closing funding while still claiming it remains available to fund the company on the next morning.

How to correct it

Reconcile opening target cash, the actual sweep and the minimum closing balance. An unchanged closing bank balance means that cash was not used to pay another party.

Quick check
A buyer pays shareholders €30m, refinances €12m of old debt, and pays €2m of fees. New borrowing is €25m. What sponsor equity balances the closing table?

Total uses are €44m. New debt contributes €25m, leaving €19m sponsor equity. Refinanced old debt is a closing use in this example because the shareholder price excludes it.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A buyout values operations at €50m. Old debt is €15m, cash €4m, and sellers receive equity value including cash. Repay old debt, pay €2m fees, leave €1m cash and borrow €26m new.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Derive uses, available target cash and sponsor equity.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Compute closing net debt and show the equity sensitivity if the cash minimum rises to €2m.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Sellers receive €39m. Total uses are €39m + €15m + €2m = €56m. Cash sweep is €3m, so equity is €56m − €26m − €3m = €27m.

Part B 3 marks

Initially net debt is €26m − €1m = €25m. The higher minimum permits only €2m sweep, increasing sponsor equity to €28m. Net debt falls to €24m because the business retains the extra euro.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A private-equity sponsor requires a 20% return. How do you work backward from an exit to the operating price it can afford today?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would start from the equity the sponsor can actually collect on exit. Here a €64m exit cheque supports only about €37m of sponsor equity three years earlier at a 20% hurdle. New debt provides €30m and fees absorb €2m, so the operating bid ceiling is about €65m. I would test entry debt and the exit multiple together before attributing any extra purchasing power to leverage.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The lender offers €5m more at entry but the full €5m remains outstanding at exit. Holding other forecasts fixed, how much does the bid ceiling rise?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The sponsor can add €5m debt at closing but loses €5m exit equity, worth €5m/1.20³ = €2.894m at its hurdle. Net entry ceiling rises only about €2.106m before accounting for the new interest or covenants. A complete schedule may reduce the gain further.
