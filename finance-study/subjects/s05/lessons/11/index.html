Rebuilding the cap table in a down round

Compute conversion-price adjustments under two down-round clauses.

Theory and terminology

Venture capital anti-dilution protection

full ratchet · broad-based weighted average · down round

What you should be able to explain

  • Compute conversion-price adjustments under two down-round clauses.
  • Reconcile how founder, protected investor and new investor ownership changes.

Locate the protection in the contract

A preferred investor buys a fixed number of legal preferred shares. The contract also specifies how many common shares each preferred share can become. Initially this is often one for one. If the original issue price is $4 and the conversion price falls to $2, each preferred share can convert into two common shares. The firm receives no additional cash merely because this ratio changes.

Price-based anti-dilution is different from a pro rata participation right. A pro rata right lets the investor spend fresh cash to maintain ownership. A conversion adjustment changes economic participation under an existing security. The liquidation preference and voting rules may have separate definitions. An as-converted ownership table measures just one aspect of the bargain.

Read the denominator before using the formula

A full ratchet lowers the protected conversion price to the new low issue price regardless of the financing size, subject to the agreed exceptions. A broad-based weighted average compares what the new cash could have purchased at the old price with the actual number issued. A larger financing at a deep discount generally causes a larger adjustment.

“Broad-based” refers to the securities included in the pre-issue capitalization. Employee options and reserves may be included, depending on the document. A narrow denominator gives the cheap issuance a larger weight. Employee plan grants, approved acquisitions or other issuances may be exempt. Establish the exact trigger before calculating anything.

Reconcile shares and interpret the transfer

Build a column for each security on a common-equivalent basis immediately before and after the round. Price paid by new investors determines their actual purchased shares. Reprice only the classes the clause protects. Sum the revised common equivalents and calculate every holder’s percentage. Percentages must total 100%.

An adjustment does not restore a fixed percentage. The investor can still be diluted because outside money buys additional shares. The burden can fall on founders, option holders and even the incoming investor if it priced the round without anticipating the adjustment.

Method

Build the result

CP₂ = CP₁ × (A + B)/(A + C); common equivalents = preferred shares × original issue price/CP₂.

CP₁ and CP₂ are old and adjusted conversion prices in currency per share. A is the pre-round common-equivalent share count under the contract, B is new cash divided by CP₁ and C is the actual number of new shares sold. Under the illustrated full ratchet CP₂ equals the new issue price.

  1. Convert the financing proceeds into shares at the old price to obtain B. Compare B with actual C.
  2. Compute the adjusted conversion price and convert the remaining protected preferred shares using it.
  3. Add founder shares, adjusted common equivalents and new shares. Reconcile percentages using one post-round denominator.
Worked case

A component start-up raises a down round

Founders have 8m common shares. Series A has 2m preferred issued at $4 and initially convertible one for one. There are no other securities. The company issues 4m new shares at $2, raising $8m. Compare unprotected, weighted-average and full-ratchet outcomes.

Working

  1. The broad base A is 10m, hypothetical B is $8m/$4 = 2m and actual C is 4m. The weighted price is $4 × 12/14 = $3.4286.
  2. Series A converts into 2 × 4/3.4286 ≈ 2.333m shares. Total common equivalents are 14.333m. Founders own 55.81%, Series A 16.28% and new investors 27.91%.
  3. Without price protection the 14m shares split 57.14%, 14.29% and 28.57%. The weighted clause transfers some ownership to Series A while it still falls below its original 20%.
  4. The full ratchet resets the price to $2. Series A converts into 4m shares, taking its stake to 25% of 16m. Founders and new investors own 50% and 25%.

Decision and interpretation

Show all three outcomes when negotiating the clause. In a tiny cheap issuance the full ratchet could still create the same conversion-price change, which is a reason to examine permitted exceptions.

Quick check
A protected preferred share was issued at $6. Its conversion price falls from $6 to $4. How many common shares can it now receive?

The conversion ratio equals original issue price divided by current conversion price, or 6/4 = 1.5.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

There are 9m founder shares and 3m preferred shares issued at €3 with an initial €3 conversion price. A company sells 2m new shares at €1.50. Calculate adjusted common equivalents for the protected class under both clauses.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked solution
  1. Cash is €3m. A = 12m, B = 1m and C = 2m. The weighted price is 3 × 13/14 = €2.7857.
  2. The protected shares convert into 3m × 3/2.7857 ≈ 3.231m common equivalents. Total shares are about 14.231m.
  3. Under a full ratchet the conversion price falls to €1.50. The preferred converts into 6m common equivalents and the total reaches 17m.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why does an early investor sometimes receive more common shares on conversion after a cheap financing?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

A price-based anti-dilution clause can reduce the conversion price when a company issues shares below the protected price. The same preferred share then converts into more common shares. A full ratchet uses the new low price; a weighted formula also considers how much stock was issued.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Suppose the same new shares are issued at €3 instead. What happens to the weighted conversion adjustment?

Compare with the follow-up answer

B and C become equal. The ratio in the formula is one, so the €3 conversion price stays intact. Existing ownership still dilutes because new shares were issued.
