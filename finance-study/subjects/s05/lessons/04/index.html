Preferred rights

Trace liquidation preferences and conversion rights across possible exits. Two investors with equal headline ownership can have different cash payoffs.

The starting point

Preferred shares can give investors a first claim on sale proceeds. A one-times, non-participating preference typically allows the investor to take back its original investment, subject to available proceeds, or convert to ordinary shares and receive its ownership percentage under the specified agreement.

Draw the payout for several sale prices. The preference protects some downside while conversion captures upside. Read whether the investor also shares in any remaining proceeds, whether priority is capped and whether other classes rank ahead. These contract details can change the distribution dramatically.

Theory and method

Option-like payoff of convertible preferred equity

Non-participating liquidation preference · Conversion threshold

How would you describe convertible preferred to a founder who thinks an investor’s 20% means it always receives one-fifth of a sale?

A preferred investor can have the right to recover up to its contribution before ordinary shareholders and the option to convert into a percentage of the company. It will compare those routes at exit. At very low proceeds it can receive all the cash. At intermediate proceeds its preference stays flat. At high proceeds it converts and shares in further gains. This shape can make preferred and ordinary investors disagree on the price or timing of a sale.

How the result is built

Preferred(V) = max[min(I,V), fV]

V is net sale proceeds available to equity, I the one-times preference in euros, and f the investor’s ordinary ownership fraction after conversion. The expression assumes a single non-participating class.

  1. For I = €4m and f = 20%, sales below €4m give the investor all available proceeds. At €10m its preference remains €4m while conversion gives €2m.
  2. Set I equal to fV to find the switch at €20m net proceeds. At €30m the investor converts for €6m and ordinary holders receive €24m.
  3. If €5m of debt must be paid at exit, translate the €20m equity threshold into €25m operating sale proceeds before fees. Compare each term sheet on the relevant basis.

When the reasoning holds

  • The class may choose conversion and cannot stack its preference with ordinary participation. An uncapped participating class produces a different residual.
  • Equity proceeds come after debt, fees and higher senior classes. Using the gross enterprise sale price can trigger fictitious conversion in the model.
Terms in this lesson
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
Enterprise value
Value of the business operations attributable to its capital providers. A bridge adjusts it to the amount due to shareholders.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
First example

An investor contributes €3m for a 20% stake with a one-times non-participating preference, no senior claims. A €10m exit gives it €3m because conversion pays €2m. At a €25m exit, conversion gives €5m. It cannot take both the preference and the converted amount.

A percentage alone cannot price a preference

A liquidation preference gives a class a contractual priority when the stated sale or liquidation event occurs. Read the base amount, multiple, seniority and whether dividends count. Under a simple one-times preference a €5m investor may claim the first €5m available, capped by proceeds if less is available.

Non-participating preferred typically chooses the better of its preference or conversion to an ordinary share fraction under the assumed terms. A participating class may first take its preference and then share some remaining proceeds. Caps and conversion options can change that calculation again. Use the agreement in the question.

Find the conversion threshold

With one non-participating preferred class invested amount I, an as-converted ownership fraction f and net exit proceeds V, its simple payout is the larger of min(I,V) and f × V. The two uncapped alternatives match when V = I/f. Above this level the percentage claim is more valuable.

Apply the formula to proceeds available after senior debt and transaction costs. If the question gives enterprise value, first convert it to the distributable equity pool. A price stated for all company assets cannot be allocated directly among equity classes while debt remains unpaid.

Why the shape of the return matters

Preferences protect part of the downside while preserving the option to join the upside. Founders can retain a majority of shares but receive little at a modest sale. These different payoffs can produce disagreement over an early exit, a new risky project or a future financing round.

Multiple classes complicate the election because one class’s conversion can change the share fraction and available pool for others. Work through feasible conversion choices and check individual incentives under the stated rules. A shortcut assuming every preferred class converts can fail in a low or medium exit.

Method

Draw a payout table

  1. Set the pool

    Start from net equity proceeds and write the order of all senior claims. Use the investment amount and fully diluted fraction specified for this class.

  2. Compare elections

    For each exit value calculate the preference payoff and the converted payoff. Choose the larger where the agreement allows election. Add participation only if the class expressly has it.

  3. Check the residual

    Allocate remaining proceeds to ordinary owners and ensure the total matches distributable cash. Identify the break-even exit at which the class changes its election.

Worked case

An investor offered two term sheets

An investor puts in €4m for 25% as converted. Sheet A gives a one-times non-participating preference. Sheet B gives one-times uncapped participation, receiving the first €4m and 25% of the balance. There are no other claims. Compare net equity sales of €10m and €24m.

Working

  1. Under A at €10m, the preference €4m exceeds €2.5m conversion. At €24m conversion is €6m and beats €4m. The A threshold is €4m/0.25 = €16m.
  2. Under B, the €10m exit pays €4m + 25% × €6m = €5.5m. At €24m the investor gets €4m + 25% × €20m = €9m.
  3. Founder proceeds at €10m are €6m under A and €4.5m under B. The same 25% headline ownership produces a €1.5m difference for founders at that exit.

Interpretation

At proceeds below €4m the investor cannot receive more than the entire available pool. Check results at zero, the preference amount and the conversion threshold before relying on the table.

A common mistake

Applying 25% to the full pool after paying a participating preference when the contract specifies 25% of the remaining pool.

How to correct it

Subtract all prior distributions first, then apply participation to the residual. For non-participating terms choose one route rather than adding both.

Quick check
An investor paid €4m for a 25% stake with a one-times non-participating preference. Net available sale proceeds are €12m. What does the investor take under those terms?

Converted shares would pay €3m. The €4m preference is larger and funded by available proceeds. Non-participating terms do not add the two amounts together.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A preferred investor paid €3m for 20% as converted. Sale proceeds are €17m before repaying €3m secured debt. Compare one-times non-participating and uncapped participating terms, with no other claims.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    What is the net equity pool, conversion threshold and non-participating payout?

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    What does the participating version pay and what is left for ordinary owners?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

After debt, equity gets €14m. Conversion threshold is €3m/20% = €15m. At €14m conversion pays €2.8m so the investor takes its €3m preference.

Part B 3 marks

The investor gets €3m + 20% × (€14m − €3m) = €5.2m. The remaining €8.8m goes to ordinary owners. The check is €5.2m + €8.8m = €14m.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How would you describe convertible preferred to a founder who thinks an investor’s 20% means it always receives one-fifth of a sale?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would draw the exit waterfall. The investor with a €4m preference and 20% conversion takes €4m on a €10m equity sale, twice what its percentage suggests. It starts converting only above €20m. This makes the marginal benefit of a slightly higher bid depend on the current sale range. Founders should negotiate rights and model actual exits alongside their headline percentage.

Interviewer’s follow-up

A proposed sale price rises from €11m to €13m with €3m debt unchanged. Who captures the extra €2m?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Net equity proceeds rise from €8m to €10m, both inside the flat preference range. Investor payout remains €4m and ordinary holders receive the whole €2m increase. Additional debt repayment, fees or another senior preference could move those boundaries.
