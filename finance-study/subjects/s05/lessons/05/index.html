Staging and dilution

Later rounds can dilute earlier investors while funding activities that increase total company value. Model both effects in a capitalization table.

The starting point

A startup can raise money in stages as it reaches milestones. This lets investors learn before committing the next amount, but the company faces the risk that new funding will be unavailable. New shares spread ownership across a larger number of shares, diluting earlier percentages.

Build a capitalization table listing share counts before and after each issue. A smaller percentage need not mean a smaller euro value if the company raises cash at a higher valuation. Conversely, a high ownership percentage gives little protection if the business loses value.

Theory and method

Staged venture financing as a real option

Option to abandon · Milestone financing

How would you value the ability to delay a second financing cheque until a technical milestone is observed?

A startup needs a small engineering trial before an expensive plant. Funding everything today commits money even if the trial reveals the product cannot be made reliably. Splitting the funding may allow abandonment after a bad signal. Compare the value of the information-contingent action with the best feasible upfront plan, including the cost of delay and any change in financing terms. An investor must also ensure the first round is large enough to produce a meaningful milestone.

How the result is built

Staged value = −I₀ + Σₛ pₛ max(Vₛ − K, 0)/(1 + r)ᵀ

I₀ is cash for the initial experiment today, K the optional second investment at the decision date, Vₛ the project value at that date under state s, pₛ its probability, r an appropriate annual rate for the expected payoff and T elapsed years.

  1. Pay €1m now for a one-year trial. If successful with probability 50%, the resulting project is worth €10m at decision time; otherwise €2m. The second cheque costs €4m.
  2. After success, exercising creates €6m. After failure, exercising destroys €2m so the investor walks away. Discount expected €3m at 10% and subtract the initial €1m for €1.727m.
  3. A binding obligation to spend the same €4m next year in both states gives expected net €2m at that date and €0.818m today after the first cheque. The option to abstain adds €0.909m.

When the reasoning holds

  • The decision can truly be abandoned after observing the signal. A binding take-or-pay contract or rescue obligation limits flexibility.
  • Both strategies receive comparable technical information and are valued on matched dates. Repricing the second round or losing customers during the wait alters Vₛ or K.
Terms in this lesson
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
First example

A founder owns 800 of 1,000 shares. A later investor buys 250 new shares at €8,000 each. The founder still owns 800 but now has 64% of 1,250 shares. The funding adds €2m to company cash. At the issue price the founder’s paper stake is €6.4m.

Learning has an economic value

Instead of paying the entire development budget now, investors can commit enough to reach a milestone and decide later whether to continue. Staging creates an option to stop funding a weak project after receiving information. The option has value only if the milestone conveys information and the investor can genuinely change course.

Staging has costs. A company with too little runway may raise money under pressure, delay a valuable launch or lose bargaining power. Investors and founders can choose milestones that look impressive without measuring durable value. Link the release of money to a verifiable result close to the commercial or technical risk.

Track shares and percentages separately

A new issue dilutes existing holders’ percentages because total shares rise. An individual’s share count ordinarily stays the same unless that person sells, buys or exercises rights. A reduced fraction can be worth more in euros if the issue adds resources on attractive terms and creates value.

A pro-rata participation right allows existing investors to buy a portion of a round to maintain their percentage under the agreement. Maintaining a percentage requires fresh cash and should be assessed as a new investment. The legal ability to participate is different from having the money or wanting the exposure.

Understand financing sequence

In a down round, the new price per share is below the previous round’s issue price. The changed price can reflect weaker prospects, changed market conditions or bargaining. Anti-dilution rights and convertible instruments can alter existing share counts, so a simple unchanged-count calculation requires those rights to be absent.

Prepare a capitalization table for each round rather than multiplying percentages informally. Track founders, early investors, option pools and new financiers in shares and cash. Separately value each owner’s new stake and show the additional cash they supplied to obtain it.

Method

Model a staged issue

  1. Estimate the decision option

    Compare funding all stages now with funding later only after the stated milestone. Include the cost of learning, any delay and the terms of follow-on capital.

  2. Roll the ownership register

    Start with absolute share counts per owner and add only actual issues or transfers. Recalculate percentages after each transaction and check they total 100%.

  3. Price the participation right

    Determine how many new shares an existing investor must buy to keep its original percentage. Compare the required cash with the new information and possible future exits.

Worked case

A follow-on round after a successful trial

Founders have 600,000 ordinary shares and an early investor 400,000. After a trial, a new round will issue 250,000 shares at €12. The early investor may buy a pro-rata share of this fixed-size round. There are no options, preference changes or transfers.

Working

  1. Without participating, founders retain 600,000/1.25m = 48% and the early investor falls from 40% to 32%. The new investor would hold 20%.
  2. To remain at 40% the early investor needs 500,000 total shares. It buys 100,000 new shares for €1.2m. Other buyers take the remaining 150,000 for €1.8m, keeping total financing €3m.
  3. At the issue price, early investor’s non-participating stake has a €4.8m paper value. Participating gives €6m of paper value after paying €1.2m extra. The higher gross stake is not an immediate free gain.

Interpretation

If the early investor’s purchase increases the round size rather than replacing some external allocation, the denominator changes. Read whether the total issue is fixed.

A common mistake

Multiplying the new company valuation by the old ownership percentage after a round without checking whether the holder buys additional shares.

How to correct it

Use the closing share counts and report any additional investment separately. Old percentage is preserved only under specified rights and sufficient participation.

Quick check
You own 100 shares out of 500. The company issues 125 new shares to someone else. What is your new ownership?

Your share count stays 100 but the total rises to 625. Dividing 100 by 625 gives 16%, down from the original 20%.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A founder owns 300,000 of 500,000 shares. A fixed round issues 100,000 new shares at €5. The founder can participate pro rata.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    What is founder ownership without participating, and what purchase maintains it?

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Compare staged financing with a full upfront round when a technical test may fail.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Without buying, ownership is 300,000/600,000 = 50%. Keeping the original 60% requires 360,000 shares, hence 60,000 new shares costing €300,000.

Part B 3 marks

Staging lets investors abandon the later cheque if the test reveals failure, reducing wasted capital. Against that benefit, assess whether enough funds exist to reach a credible test and whether future fundraising risk could halt a good project.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How would you value the ability to delay a second financing cheque until a technical milestone is observed?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would map who has the decision right at the milestone. Here the investor spends the second €4m only after a successful test because the project then produces €6m net value. In failure the option avoids a €2m loss. Discounting gives about €0.91m of flexibility value compared with compulsory follow-on funding on the same date. If staging weakens the commercial launch, I would deduct that separately.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Waiting forfeits €2m of project value only in the successful state. Is the flexibility still better than the original compulsory plan?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The staged success payoff drops from €6m to €4m. Its expected year-one payoff falls by €1m, exactly the expected abandonment benefit. At 10% both effects are worth about €0.909m today. The revised staged value ties the original compulsory plan, before any extra financing or delay costs.
