Evidence and selection

Compare private and public performance using matched cash-flow dates and benchmarks. Consider survivorship, valuation smoothing and the choice of vintage.

The starting point

Private fund returns depend on when investors supply and recover cash. An index return measured over calendar years is a poor comparator if the fund calls capital gradually. A public-market comparison can invest and withdraw matching amounts at the index levels on the actual cash-flow dates.

A vintage groups funds that began investing around the same time, exposing them to similar market conditions. Check whether failed funds disappear from the sample and how much value remains in unsold holdings. Infrequent manager valuations can make reported returns appear smoother than underlying economic risk.

Theory and method

Kaplan–Schoar public market equivalent

KS-PME · Cash-flow-matched benchmark

What does a KS-PME above one establish, and what does it leave unresolved?

A private fund calls capital at several dates and may distribute cash before its final exit. An investor could instead have bought index units on the contribution dates and reinvested early distributions in the same market. The Kaplan–Schoar public market equivalent carries both sides to the same date using a total-return public index. It tells how much wealth the private cash-flow sequence supplied relative to that dated public-market comparison. Manager skill requires additional evidence about risk and selection.

How the result is built

KS-PME = [Σₜ Dₜ(Bₙ/Bₜ) + NAVₙ] / [Σₜ Cₜ(Bₙ/Bₜ)]

Cₜ and Dₜ are positive euro contributions and distributions at date t, Bₜ the total-return index then, Bₙ the index at final date n, and NAVₙ the remaining net portfolio value then. The ratio has no units.

  1. Pay €80 at index 100 and €60 at index 150. At final index 180 their public equivalents are €144 and €72, totaling €216.
  2. An early €25 distribution at index 150 is worth €30 on the common date. Add €150 final distribution and €45 remaining portfolio value to obtain €225.
  3. The ratio is €225/€216 = 1.0417. Break-even residual value is €216 − €30 − €150 = €36. A €9 cut in the remaining mark removes the apparent excess.

When the reasoning holds

  • Use investor-net cash including relevant fees and an investable total-return index in the same currency. Gross fund cash and a price-only index bias the comparison.
  • The numerator can include subjective unsold holdings. Look at realised distributions, reporting selection, vintage, leverage and sector differences before attributing excess to skill.
Terms in this lesson
Benchmark
A reference chosen in advance for comparison, such as a market index or an equal-weight portfolio.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
First example

An investor contributes €100 when the index is 100 and €60 when it is 120. The matched index purchase owns 1.5 index units. At an exit index of 140 the benchmark is worth €210. If the fully realized fund returns €225 at that date, it exceeds this simple matched comparison by €15.

Match the dates of private and public investment

A private fund calls cash and returns it at irregular dates. A comparison with the full market index over the fund’s life can credit the index with capital before the investor actually supplied it. A dated public-market benchmark buys index units at each capital call and models withdrawals or equivalent value at distribution dates.

One common public-market equivalent carries all cash flows to a common date at index returns. Multiply each contribution or distribution by terminal index divided by index on its flow date. Compare the sum of scaled distributions plus terminal net asset value with scaled contributions. A ratio above one means outperformance of this particular cash-flow-matched index comparison.

Read realized and unrealized values separately

Distributions are cash investors have received. Net asset value estimates remaining holdings after relevant obligations. A fund can appear successful largely because its remaining companies are marked at optimistic private prices. Compute a ratio both with reported remaining value and with plausible discounts where the uncertainty matters.

Series of private valuations can move more slowly than traded prices because they are updated on different dates or rely on model estimates. Low measured short-run volatility need not indicate genuinely lower economic risk. Check the valuation dates, methods and proportion backed by recent independent transactions.

Selection changes which funds you observe

A vintage groups funds beginning in similar periods. Market entry price, financing conditions and exit markets can vary by vintage. Compare compatible strategy, leverage, geography, currency and fee basis. A benchmark with the wrong industry or risk exposure can leave apparent outperformance unrelated to manager skill.

Survivorship bias occurs when only funds still operating or voluntarily reporting remain observable. Backfill can arise if a successful manager joins a database and provides earlier good outcomes selectively. Neither a large sample nor a precise average corrects a selection process tied to performance.

Method

Construct a transparent comparison

  1. Align flows

    Record dates, investor-paid fees, calls and distributions on one investor basis. Select a relevant total-return public index that includes dividends and use consistent currency.

  2. Carry cash to one date

    Scale each contribution and distribution by the change in the index since that date. Add any end-date residual net asset value before dividing the relevant totals.

  3. Challenge the comparison

    Calculate realized and unrealized shares of the result. Test alternative index, vintage and residual assumptions and state which funds or exits are missing from the sample.

Worked case

A fund still owns an unsold company

Investors contribute €100 when the index is 100 and €60 when it is 120. They receive €30 when the index is 150. At the final date the index is 180, they receive another €150 and the remaining net asset value is €90.

Working

  1. Scaled contributions are €100 × 180/100 + €60 × 180/120 = €270. This uses the market growth actually available after each contribution.
  2. The earlier €30 distribution scales to €36. Terminal distributions and residual value are €150 + €90, giving numerator €276. The public-market equivalent is €276/€270 = 1.022.
  3. If the unsold stake is worth only €70, the ratio falls to €256/€270 = 0.948. The small apparent advantage depends on the terminal valuation and cannot establish repeatable skill.

Interpretation

The index return used here is a market opportunity cost, not proof the two portfolios bore equal risk. Compare leverage, industry exposures and liquidity before interpreting the excess as manager value added.

A common mistake

Scaling investor contributions forward but leaving early distributions at their original cash amount.

How to correct it

Scale both sides to the same date. A distribution received early could itself have earned the index return after it was paid back to the investor.

Quick check
A survey reports only funds that still volunteer results ten years later. What is the most direct risk to its performance estimate?

Selection affects who is observed. If weak funds drop out disproportionately, the returns of surviving reporters can overstate those of the full original group.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

Investors contribute €80 at index 100 and €50 at index 125. They receive €20 at index 125 and €130 at terminal index 150, with €30 residual net asset value. Use a total-return index.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Compute the cash-flow-matched public-market equivalent.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    What terminal residual value just matches the index and what should be checked?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Scaled contributions are €120 + €60 = €180. The €20 distribution scales to €24. Numerator is €24 + €130 + €30 = €184. The ratio is 1.022.

Part B 3 marks

Scaled distributions excluding residual equal €154, so break-even residual is €26. Compare the €30 mark with recent third-party prices and valuations, and check if the index reflects comparable leverage and sector exposure.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

What does a KS-PME above one establish, and what does it leave unresolved?

Compare with a spoken answer

I would say the fund delivered 1.042 units of terminal-equivalent value for each unit of the matched public benchmark. I would immediately qualify that €45 is still an estimate for unsold assets. A €9 markdown removes the advantage. I would also match currency, fees, vintage and industry risk, then investigate reporting coverage before interpreting the ratio as evidence of skill.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The manager reports an attractive annual IRR. Can that replace the public-market equivalent in the comparison?

Compare with the follow-up answer

IRR answers the discount rate that balances the fund’s own cash flows. It does not measure what the same dated investments could have earned in the selected public market. I would report both, together with actual distributions and residual reliance, because a high absolute IRR can accompany a strong public-market opportunity cost.
