Case 1 of 4 · 10 marks

The round that funds a milestone

Founders own 800,000 shares at €8m pre-money. A new investor contributes €2m for primary shares and pays founders €0.5m for secondary shares at the same price. Later, a fixed €3m round issues 250,000 shares at €12 and all holders may participate pro rata. Before the original offer a pilot could have revealed whether a separate €2m-only primary investment would be worth €4m or €1m, equally likely, before any fees. Treat the pilot comparison as an independent decision.

Case 2 of 4 · 10 marks

The preferences and the fund’s own waterfall

A fund invests €5m for 25% of a startup using one-times non-participating preferred. Investors separately contribute €0.5m of fees. There are no senior claims. Exit company proceeds are €16m or €32m. After an exit the fund returns up to €5.5m paid-in capital to investors first, then grants the manager 20% of any remaining positive profit. There is no other preferred return or catch-up.

Case 3 of 4 · 10 marks

Closing and servicing a buyout

Operations are bought for €64m enterprise value. Old debt is €18m and cash €4m. Sellers receive equity value including cash, old debt is refinanced, and €1m cash must remain in the business. Fees are €2m. New debt is €30m at 5% on opening principal. First-year cash before debt service, after the operating buffer, is €6.5m. Scheduled principal is €2m and 50% of remaining cash is swept. The covenant tests closing debt net of €1m eligible cash against €7m EBITDA and a maximum 3.8 times.

Case 4 of 4 · 10 marks

Fund performance and an exit choice

Fund investors contribute €90 when a total-return index is 90 and €60 when it is 120. They receive €24 at index 144. At the reporting date the index is 180, additional cash distributions are €140 and the only unsold position is marked at €110. A buyer offers €100 net today for that position. The manager expects €122 net in two years. Discount that delayed cash at 12% and assume other portfolio cash flows do not change.

Case 1 · 10 marks

The round that funds a milestone

Part A 3 marks

Calculate original investor ownership after the first transaction and company cash raised.

Price is €10 (1). The investor receives 200,000 new and 50,000 transferred shares, or 250,000/1m = 25% (1). The company receives €2m. €0.5m goes to founders (1).

Part B 3 marks

Calculate ownership without follow-on participation and the amount needed to maintain the original percentage.

Without participation ownership falls to 250,000/1.25m = 20% (1). Maintaining 25% requires 312,500 shares, so buy 62,500 at €12, costing €0.75m (2).

Part C 4 marks

What is the maximum price of a perfect pilot for the independent primary-only decision and why might a real test merit less?

Without testing, expected gain is €0.5m (1). With perfect information, invest only in the good state, for €1m expected gain before fees (1). The maximum pilot fee is €0.5m (1). Misclassification or delay reduces that value. An irrelevant pilot does not create the option assumed (1).

Case 2 · 10 marks

The preferences and the fund’s own waterfall

Part A 3 marks

Calculate the fund’s company-level proceeds in each state and the conversion threshold.

At €16m, take the €5m preference rather than €4m conversion (1). At €32m, convert for €8m (1). Threshold is €5m/25% = €20m (1).

Part B 4 marks

Calculate net fund-investor multiple and manager carry in each state.

In the low state there is no carry and €5m/€5.5m = 0.909 investor multiple (2). In the high state, profit above contributions is €2.5m, carry €0.5m, €7.5m distributed and net multiple €7.5m/€5.5m = 1.364 (2).

Part C 3 marks

How would uncapped participation at the startup change high-state investor distributions, assuming first preference plus 25% of remaining company proceeds?

Fund receives €5m + 25% × €27m = €11.75m (1). Carry is 20% × (€11.75m − €5.5m) = €1.25m (1). Investors receive €10.5m (1). Keep the company preferred and fund carry waterfalls separate.

Case 3 · 10 marks

Closing and servicing a buyout

Part A 4 marks

Reconcile sources, uses and sponsor contribution.

Seller receives €64m − €18m + €4m = €50m (1). Total uses are €50m + €18m + €2m = €70m (1). Sweep €3m existing cash and borrow €30m (1), requiring sponsor equity €37m (1).

Part B 3 marks

Calculate year-one cash interest, extra repayment and closing debt.

Interest is €1.5m (1). Remaining cash after €2m principal is €3m, giving €1.5m sweep (1). Closing principal is €30m − €2m − €1.5m = €26.5m (1).

Part C 3 marks

Test the covenant in the base case and with EBITDA at €6m, holding the debt and eligible cash fixed.

Base net leverage is (€26.5m − €1m)/€7m = 3.643, within 3.8 (1). At €6m it is 4.25, above the limit (1). Permitted net debt becomes €22.8m, a €2.7m excess needing the contractually appropriate remedy (1).

Case 4 · 10 marks

Fund performance and an exit choice

Part A 4 marks

Compute the current public-market equivalent and the break-even value of the unsold holding.

Scaled contributions equal €90 × 180/90 + €60 × 180/120 = €270 (1). The first distribution becomes €24 × 180/144 = €30, giving €280 including €140 and €110 (1). Ratio is 1.037 (1). The position must realize €270 − €30 − €140 = €100 to match the index (1).

Part B 3 marks

Compare the two exit routes and find the two-year break-even amount.

Waiting gives €122/1.12² ≈ €97.26 today (1). Selling for €100 leads by about €2.74 (1). Waiting must produce €100 × 1.12² = €125.44 in two years to match it (1).

Part C 3 marks

What does a realized sale at €100 do to the comparison, and why is even a ratio above one insufficient evidence of skill?

Replacing €110 with €100 produces a ratio of exactly 1.00 (1). Different leverage, sector or illiquidity can explain performance versus the index (1). Selective reporting or differences in vintages can also distort conclusions about the manager (1).
