Going public and choosing a listing route

Separate the company’s need for cash from its owners’ wish to sell.

Theory and terminology

Public financing, shareholder liquidity and the choice of listing route

initial public offering · flotation · IPO strategy · direct listing · SPAC · de-SPAC · going public

What you should be able to explain

  • Separate the company’s need for cash from its owners’ wish to sell.
  • Compare an underwritten IPO, a direct listing, a SPAC combination and a private financing on committed cash and control.

Why the decision changes the company

An initial public offering (IPO) is a company’s first public offering of shares. A listing is admission of securities to a trading venue. A company can obtain a public quotation while existing holders sell their shares. It can also raise private money without listing. Ask whose balance sheet is changing. A new issue puts cash into the company. Selling an existing share moves cash between the buyer and its owner.

An issuer might need permanent equity, a quoted share to use in future acquisitions, or access to a broader investor base. Its owners might want diversification and a way to sell gradually. Going public also requires an organization capable of preparing accounts, disclosing material information, answering investors, and governing conflicts after the deal. Weigh recurrent costs and a possible loss of control alongside the one-off proceeds.

Four routes and the uncertainty each creates

In a marketed, underwritten IPO, the issuer and its banks work on disclosure, market the shares and agree terms of sale. A placing may combine newly created and already-owned shares. Under a firm commitment the banks assume distribution risk once the agreement becomes binding, subject to contractual closing conditions. Terms can change or the deal can be postponed before that point.

In a resale-only direct listing, existing shares become tradable and the issuer receives no sale proceeds. Some venues also permit a primary direct listing. For example, the NYSE permits the issuer to sell new shares in an opening auction under its applicable rules. A SPAC is a listed acquisition vehicle. At its merger with an operating company, shareholder redemptions can reduce cash. A committed private investment in public equity, or PIPE, may add funds. Check redemption exposure, sponsor shares and warrants before comparing ownership.

Translate the board’s priorities into a mandate

Set a minimum net cash amount, a date when the money is actually needed, an acceptable ownership outcome and a financing fallback. Assess each against a base case and a weak market. A private round can avoid some listing work but may introduce preferences, consent rights and a later refinancing need. A board with a near-term payroll commitment gives greater weight to certainty of funding.

Method

Build the result

Closing cash available = primary cash received + other committed funding − redemptions where applicable − cash fees.

Primary cash is paid for new shares. In a SPAC route begin with the trust amount and subtract redemptions once. Secondary sale proceeds go to selling holders. Every amount in the comparison must be at the same closing date and in the same currency.

  1. Start from the receiving company’s cash ledger. Identify each binding source available at closing and the conditions on its release.
  2. Remove shareholder distributions, fees and any minimum cash reserved for another purpose. Keep uncommitted expressions of interest in a separate scenario.
  3. Compare the resulting available amount with required funding. Then compare voting rights and ongoing obligations among the feasible routes.
Worked case

Which routes can fund the plant?

A plant requires €45m net. Route A sells €55m of new shares and €15m of founder shares, with €4m of fees charged to the issuer. Route B is a resale-only direct listing. Route C brings a €60m SPAC trust, 40% redemptions, a €12m binding PIPE and €5m fees. Ignore other cash and claims.

Working

  1. Route A brings €55m to the company before fees. The founder’s €15m is outside the issuer cash ledger.
  2. The issuer retains 55 − 4 = €51m. It has €6m headroom above the plant budget.
  3. Route B raises €0 from issuing shares. The plant shortfall is €45m unless separate private funding closes.
  4. Route C releases 60 × 60% = €36m after redemptions. Add 12 and subtract 5 to get €43m. It falls €2m short.

Decision and interpretation

Route A meets both stated objectives in the modeled case. To choose it, verify underwriting conditions and the feasible issue price. A SPAC label or a listing date provides no assurance about how much cash will arrive.

Quick check
A company needs immediate operating cash. Which item can it count as primary funding?

Only the new-share subscription creates cash available on the company’s own balance sheet.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

Holding the trust, PIPE and fees fixed, what is the highest redemption percentage at which route C still provides €45m? If the PIPE drops to €8m, what changes?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked solution
  1. Writing x as the fraction redeemed, net cash is 60(1 − x) + 12 − 5. Require at least 45, giving 60x ≤ 22.
  2. The maximum redemption rate is 22/60 = 36.67%. At the boundary €38m of trust cash plus €12m less €5m equals €45m.
  3. With an €8m PIPE the maximum is (60 + 8 − 5 − 45)/60 = 30%. The weaker financing commitment reduces the redemption buffer.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A founder wants liquidity and the company needs €45m for a plant. Would getting the shares listed deliver both?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Listing creates a trading venue. I would first find out which shares are being sold and who receives the money. A sale by the founder can create liquidity while raising zero for the company. I would compare net primary proceeds, control, time to closing and a funded fallback for the plant. A popular trading debut cannot pay an invoice unless the issuer actually receives cash.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Would a €10m founder sell-down added to the SPAC deal close the €2m shortfall?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Trace the cash. If a buyer pays the founder, the company still has €43m. The founder could separately subscribe for €2m of new equity or fund a documented loan, but that would be another transaction with its own claims and conditions.
