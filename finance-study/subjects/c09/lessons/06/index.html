IPO underpricing, the winner’s curse and money left on the table

Measure the offer-to-close return and show who bears the apparent price concession.

Theory and terminology

Rock’s winner’s curse and information revelation in IPOs

IPO underpricing · initial return · first-day pop · money left on the table · Rock · winner’s curse · Benveniste and Spindt · leaving money on table

What you should be able to explain

  • Measure the offer-to-close return and show who bears the apparent price concession.
  • Derive the expected break-even offer for a subscriber facing different fill rates.

Measure first, then explain

Let the offer price be the amount paid by allotted investors and the first close be the first day’s closing market price. A positive offer-to-close return is the conventional empirical measure of IPO underpricing. Do not substitute the first trade for the close or a later split-adjusted price for an original offer. An investor who buys after trading starts earns a different return.

A commonly reported opportunity cost multiplies the first-day price difference by shares sold. Break the amount into primary and secondary shares to see whether the issuer or existing sellers bore it. This calculation assumes the whole block could have been sold at that observed price. Demand may have changed at a higher offer. The measure is a descriptive counterfactual and is separate from fees.

Rock’s adverse-selection mechanism

Imagine informed and uninformed subscribers. In a favorable issue informed buyers bid aggressively. A small uninformed order receives only part of its request. In an unfavorable issue the informed buyers avoid the deal, so the same order is filled more heavily. An investor calculating expected value per share actually received must weight states by probability times the number of shares allocated.

Equal probabilities across offerings give equal weights across deals. They do not give equal weights across allotted shares. Even if the average company is worth the offer price, a subscriber’s expected payoff per application can be negative. The required haircut depends on how much the allocation tilts toward weak issues and on other costs of participation.

Other theories make other predictions

In the Benveniste–Spindt explanation, a bank wants investors to reveal useful private information during bookbuilding. Allocation priority or an expected discount can compensate truthful reporting. Part of favorable information may enter the final offer price while part remains in the first-day return. A price revision or a first-day pop by itself is insufficient evidence of this mechanism.

Issuer–bank agency conflicts, issue risk, ownership objectives and investor sentiment offer other explanations. To distinguish them look at investor-level allocations, the book revision, conflicts, issuer objectives and comparable market conditions. Do not infer a universal optimal discount from a historical average.

Method

Build the result

Break-even price = Σ(probability × shares filled × value) / Σ(probability × shares filled). Initial return = (first close − offer) / offer.

Each probability refers to an issue state. Value is the assumed same-date economic value per share in that state. The ratio gives a risk-neutral break-even price with zero costs, no financing charge and shares acquired only when allocated.

  1. Write the profit in each state as shares actually filled times the difference between state value and offer price.
  2. Weight state profits by their probabilities and sum. Solve expected profit equal to zero for the unknown offer price.
  3. Compare this allocated-share price with the unconditional average value. A smaller fill in the valuable state lowers the break-even price.
Worked case

Positive average issue, negative subscriber result

Two states are equally likely. A good issue is worth €12 and a weak issue €8. The investor applies for 100 shares in either issue but receives 25 in the good state and 100 in the weak state. The proposed offer price is €9.50. Ignore fees and financing.

Working

  1. The unweighted mean economic value is €10. An equal-weighted issue comparison implies an average return of 10/9.50 − 1 = 5.26%.
  2. Good-state profit is 25 × (12 − 9.50) = €62.50. Weak-state profit is 100 × (8 − 9.50) = −€150.
  3. Expected profit per application is (62.50 − 150)/2 = −€43.75. Average shares filled are (25 + 100)/2 = 62.5.
  4. Expected value received is (25 × 12 + 100 × 8)/2 = €550. Break-even price is 550/62.5 = €8.80. At €9.50, expected outlay €593.75 yields −7.37% on expected allocated capital.

Decision and interpretation

A headline positive return can coexist with a loss for subscribers facing unfavorable rationing. Different eligibility, allocation rules or information access change the result.

Quick check
Which quantity weights states in a subscriber’s expected break-even calculation?

The investor pays for allocated shares. Weight both state value and outlay by the actual fill.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

A company issues 4m new shares and a founder sells 1m existing shares at €20. The first close is €23. Compute the first-day return and the descriptive amount left on the table for each seller. Can the CFO book that amount as a cash expense?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked solution
  1. Initial return is (23 − 20)/20 = 15%. The difference is €3 on each allotted share.
  2. Primary opportunity-cost measure is 4m × €3 = €12m. The founder’s is 1m × €3 = €3m. Total is €15m.
  3. The €15m is a price-based counterfactual, not a cash payment. The issuer actually receives €80m gross before its fees. Repricing a 5m-share block could have changed demand and first-day trading.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

If two IPOs are equally likely to be good or bad, why might an investor who bids for both lose money at the average company value?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Return depends on the shares the investor actually gets. Informed bidders compete for the good issue, cutting my good-state allotment. They can avoid the bad issue, leaving me a larger allotment precisely when value is lower. In Rock’s model that selection can require a price concession to keep uninformed investors participating. A first-day rise by itself cannot tell us which explanation operated.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If good and weak issues both fill precisely 50 shares, how does Rock’s allocation effect change?

Compare with the follow-up answer

With equal fills, each state gets equal share weight. Expected value per allocated share returns to (12 + 8)/2 = €10. Other risks or motives may still produce a price concession, but this allocation mechanism no longer requires one.
