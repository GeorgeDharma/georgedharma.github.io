IPO overpricing, market timing and investment strategy

Theory and terminology

Offer-to-close returns, relative valuation and matched long-run performance

overpriced IPO · overpricing · overvaluation · first-day loss · IPO investment strategy · offer to close · long-run underperformance · BHAR

What you should be able to explain

  • Test first-day pricing, valuation relative to peers and later investor performance separately.
  • Recommend an issuer decision or a subscription limit using explicit demand, value and risk assumptions.

Three questions hidden inside “overpriced”

A negative offer-to-first-close return calls an offering overpriced relative to that first close. Fair-value overpricing means the actual offer exceeds an estimate of economic value. It requires a valuation model, comparable firms or an explicit cash-flow forecast. Long-run underperformance compares the return earned over a specified holding period with a benchmark. All three can give different answers.

Purnanandam and Swaminathan examine IPO prices relative to matched firms. Their design helps explain why a positive average initial return can sit alongside a high relative valuation. Think about what could invalidate the comparison: stronger prospective growth, different profitability, accounting conventions, capital structure or valuable options. One multiple gives evidence about an assumption, not an observable intrinsic price.

Make an issuer and investor decision before the first trade

An issuer may price lower to improve completion probability, broaden ownership or leave room for future issuance. A higher price can meet a cash target with fewer shares but can also lose price-sensitive bids. Test the lowest price that funds the plan and the highest price supported by executable demand. If they do not overlap, reducing secondary supply, arranging bridge capital, resizing the investment or postponing are decisions with different costs.

An institutional subscriber can write a maximum bid from a valuation range, expected allocation, liquidity and a cash reserve for other commitments. Distinguish limit bidding at the offering from buying at the opening print. For each strategy, estimate payoff from its actual entry and allotment, including the possibility of a zero fill. An average in a historical sample gives no entitlement to the allocation or result.

Measure subsequent performance fairly

Use the same calendar window, currency and dividend convention for the issue and benchmark. A buy-and-hold abnormal return subtracts benchmark terminal wealth from stock terminal wealth per unit invested. A wealth relative divides the two wealth values. A negative market-adjusted result can occur on a rising stock. A stock that falls may outperform an even weaker market.

Long-run comparisons depend on matching, weighting, sample selection, delistings and benchmark rebalancing. A close-to-close study answers how an aftermarket buyer performed. Including the initial price concession answers a different investor question. An analysis that deletes failed firms can overstate returns.

Method

Build the result

Initial return = P₁/P₀ − 1. Relative offer premium = P₀/Vpeer − 1. Buy-and-hold abnormal return = stock terminal wealth − benchmark terminal wealth per €1 invested.

P₀ is the offer price, P₁ is the first close, Vpeer is a model-based per-share value. Terminal wealth includes distributions under the chosen reinvestment convention. Abnormal return here is an arithmetic difference expressed in percentage points.

  1. Fix the investor’s actual entry point and distinguish a dated market observation from estimated fair value.
  2. Construct stock and benchmark wealth indices starting at one at the same time. Include losses and distributions consistently.
  3. Compare wealth differences and ratios separately. Recalculate when a plausible alternative peer set changes the estimated value.
Worked case

Three defensible answers for the same IPO

A share is offered at €20, closes on day one at €22 and at year end at €18. There are no dividends. An operating peer analysis implies €16 per share. The chosen benchmark rises 5% from first close to year end. A separate issuer scenario considers a €19 first close.

Working

  1. Offer-to-first-close return is 22/20 − 1 = +10%. Relative to the €16 peer indication, the offer carries a 20/16 − 1 = 25% premium.
  2. An allotted buy-and-hold investor earns 18/20 − 1 = −10% through year end. A buyer at the first close earns 18/22 − 1 = −18.18%.
  3. From the first close the benchmark earns +5%. The stock’s buy-and-hold abnormal return over that same interval is −18.18% − 5% = −23.18 percentage points.
  4. At a €19 first close the initial return instead is 19/20 − 1 = −5%. For 5m shares sold the signed offer-to-close price difference is −€5m, without any refund from sellers.

Decision and interpretation

Name the comparator when using “overpriced.” A peer premium is sensitive to company quality. An allocation gain says nothing by itself about a trading strategy entering at the close.

Quick check
An offer of €10 falls to €9.50 at its first close, while your fundamental estimate is €8. Which statement follows?

The empirical first-day measure and the valuation comparison address distinct questions. No year-end price has been supplied.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

An IPO prices at €25 and closes at €24. At year end it trades at €26 and has paid €1 cash at the end. A matched index with distributions rises 8% from the first close. Revised peer value is €28. Interpret all three benchmarks.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked solution
  1. The first-day return is 24/25 − 1 = −4%. Relative to the peer indication the offer is at a discount of 1 − 25/28 = 10.71%.
  2. The aftermarket buyer receives €27 including the distribution on €24 invested, a return of 12.5%. The matched-index difference is +4.5 percentage points.
  3. An IPO subscriber earns 27/25 − 1 = 8% over the longer interval from offer. The 8% benchmark given begins at first close. A valid comparison for the subscriber requires the benchmark from the offer date.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

The shares rose 10% on day one. Could the IPO still have been overpriced?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Yes, depending on the benchmark. A €20 offer and €22 first close give positive measured underpricing. If genuinely comparable companies imply a value nearer €16, the offer was expensive against that model. A buyer entering at €22 has a third starting point. I would name the price, date, comparator and investable allocation before giving a verdict.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Suppose the peer’s higher value entirely reflects faster revenue growth. What would you change?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Match prospective growth, margins and reinvestment or model their cash consequences. Recalculate the range. A first-day price observation stays the same; the claim that the issuer was fundamentally cheap or expensive can change with the comparison.
