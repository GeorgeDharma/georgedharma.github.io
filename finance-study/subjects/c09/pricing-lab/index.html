Price an IPO

Marais Instruments needs €48m to build a production line. Its founder also wants to sell. Change the proposed terms to see whether the company, the founder and the order book can all accept the same deal.

The pricing mandate

There are 20m existing ordinary shares. The founder owns 12m. Each share has one vote. The company requires €48m after costs, the founder requires €18m, and new shares may represent at most 20% of the post-issue shares. Each party pays a 4% bank fee on its own proceeds. The company alone pays a further €2m. Orders can be allocated in part.

An investor’s limit is the highest price they will pay. Higher prices remove lower-limit orders from the book. The five orders are independent and have already been checked for duplicates.

Trace the base offer

  1. At €10.50 investors A through D are eligible. Their orders total 2.6 + 2.4 + 1.9 + 1.9 = 8.8m. A 1m withdrawal leaves 7.8m for a 7m offer.
  2. The company sells 5m shares. €10.50 × 5m × 96% − €2m gives €48.40m. The founder sells 2m existing shares and receives €10.50 × 2m × 96% = €20.16m.
  3. The new share count is 25m. The company created 5m, or 20% of that total. The founder retains 10m of 25m, or 40%.
  4. Keeping the share quantities fixed, €10 misses the funding floor by €2m. At €11 the unstressed orders total 6.9m, already short of the 7m base. A higher quoted price only helps if buyers will actually take the shares.

Reverse the decision

Suppose the company keeps 5m new shares and proposes €11. With a 1m withdrawal, how many founder shares can the desk sell? Does that deal meet the founder’s mandate?

Check the decision

The book can place at most 6.9m − 1m = 5.9m. The founder could sell 0.9m and receive 0.9 × €11 × 96% = €9.504m. This falls short of €18m. Reducing the secondary leg cures the book shortfall but fails a separate client constraint.
