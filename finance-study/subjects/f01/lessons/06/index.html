Receivables and estimates

A customer invoice is an asset only to the extent it is collectible. Compare a closing allowance with ageing and subsequent cash receipts.

The starting point

An unpaid invoice represents a right to collect. A business may expect to collect less than the customer owes. An allowance records the estimated shortfall while preserving the gross invoice balance.

One simple estimation method groups invoices by how overdue they are. Older balances may have higher expected loss rates. Use subsequent collections and changes in customers’ circumstances to challenge these rates. The allowance is an estimate, so a change in it can affect the period’s profit.

Theory and method

Probability-weighted expected credit loss

Loss allowance · Discounted cash shortfall

A buyer owes €100,000 in one year. How would a lender estimate a loss when different recovery outcomes are plausible?

A lender is promised one payment, yet the money it actually receives can vary. In some outcomes the borrower pays on time. In others it pays a smaller amount. Put probabilities on the possible shortfalls and translate the future shortfall into its value today. This creates a loss estimate before choosing a single “most likely” outcome. It also explains why a borrower quite likely to repay can still need a material allowance.

How the result is built

Expected loss today = Σₛ pₛ × (contractual cash − expected cash in state s)/(1+i)ᵗ.

The sum runs over states s with probabilities pₛ adding to one. Cash is measured in euros on the same future date, i is the effective one-period discount rate and t is the number of periods.

  1. With an 80% full-payment state and a 20% state paying only €40,000, future shortfalls are €0 and €60,000 respectively.
  2. The probability-weighted shortfall due in a year is 0.8 × €0 + 0.2 × €60,000 = €12,000.
  3. At a 5% annual effective rate its current value is €12,000/1.05 ≈ €11,429. Record the model date, cash assumptions, and how recovery evidence supports them.

When the reasoning holds

  • The illustration has one remaining year and uses an appropriate rate for discounting these shortfalls. Multiple dates require separate discounts for each contractual and recovery payment.
  • The scenario probabilities reflect available evidence and represent mutually exclusive outcomes. Counting a recession loss twice under overlapping scenarios would inflate the result.
Terms in this lesson
Receivable
Money a customer owes for a sale already made.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Expected shortfall
The average loss in a specified worst fraction of outcomes.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
First example

A store has €80,000 of current invoices with a 1% loss estimate and €20,000 overdue with a 10% estimate. Its required allowance is €800 + €2,000 = €2,800. The receivables shown net of the allowance are €97,200.

Estimate losses before cash runs out

A supplier credits customers for thirty days. Some will pay the full invoice, some part, and some none. The gross receivable records the contractual amounts. The loss allowance is a separate balance that reduces the carrying amount to reflect expected shortfalls under the measurement approach used.

Grouping customers by overdue status can be a useful starting model. Customers sharing an age band may still differ in collateral, location or financial condition. Historical collection rates need adjustment when current evidence says tomorrow’s losses may differ. Document assumptions separately from factual balances so a reviewer can see where judgment started.

Separate an expense from an allowance

An allowance is the stock of estimated losses at a date. The impairment expense is a flow during the period. If an amount already allowed for is finally written off, reduce the gross receivable and the allowance together. This consumes the earlier estimate without automatically creating another expense at write-off.

With no recoveries or other movements, closing allowance = opening allowance + impairment expense − write-offs. The expense needed to reach a target therefore equals closing target − opening allowance + write-offs. This reconciliation is especially important when a year has large write-offs. Comparing closing allowance with opening allowance alone understates new loss recognition.

Test the estimate against evidence

Payments received after the balance-sheet date can help assess whether a year-end estimate was reasonable, depending on what they reveal about conditions at that date. Separate a genuinely new event from confirmation of an existing problem. Ask what an analyst could reasonably have known at the time.

Also examine concentration. A large balance due from one struggling buyer can deserve a separate estimate before applying percentage rates to the remaining pool. Counting that customer both individually and in a pool duplicates exposure. Aggressive assumptions about a major client can obscure a deterioration even when average overdue days appear stable.

Method

Rebuild the allowance

  1. Segment

    List gross balances, isolate specific exposures and check that every customer appears exactly once.

  2. Measure

    Multiply each pool by its expected loss rate and add individually assessed losses to obtain the target closing allowance.

  3. Bridge

    Roll forward the opening allowance through write-offs and new expense. Tie net receivables to gross balances less the closing allowance.

Worked case

A garment supplier’s loss roll-forward

Balances after write-offs comprise €90,000 current at 1%, €15,000 overdue at 8%, and a separately assessed €5,000 distressed account expected to pay €2,000. The opening allowance was €4,000 and €1,500 was written off.

Working

  1. Pooled losses are €900 + €1,200 = €2,100. The distressed buyer adds €3,000, so the target is €5,100.
  2. The opening allowance falls to €2,500 after write-offs. A €2,600 impairment expense restores it to €5,100.
  3. Gross balances total €110,000. Closing net receivables are €104,900. Only actual collections would increase cash.

Interpretation

If reliable information raises expected settlement on the distressed account by €1,000, the required allowance and expense both fall €1,000 under the assumptions.

A common mistake

Write-offs are expensed again after the full amount has already been provided.

How to correct it

Check the allowance roll-forward. A write-off against an existing allowance reduces gross receivables and the allowance by equal amounts, leaving net receivables unchanged at that moment.

Quick check
If the existing allowance is €2,000 and there were no write-offs, how much additional expense brings it to €2,800?

The €2,000 opening allowance already covers part of the estimated loss. An extra €800 produces the required closing balance.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A courier closes with €40,000 of normal invoices at an estimated loss of 2% and €12,000 late at 15%. The allowance started at €2,000 and €700 was written off. There are no other movements.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Calculate the closing allowance, impairment expense and net receivables.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    How would a rise in the late-bucket loss rate to 20% change your answer?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

Target €800 + €1,800 = €2,600. Expense €2,600 − €2,000 + €700 = €1,300. Net receivables €52,000 − €2,600 = €49,400.

Part B 3 marks

The late pool adds €12,000 × 5% = €600 of losses. Allowance rises to €3,200, expense to €1,900, and net receivables fall to €48,800 with no immediate cash change.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A buyer owes €100,000 in one year. How would a lender estimate a loss when different recovery outcomes are plausible?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would model plausible collected amounts and their dates, estimate shortfalls relative to what the contract promises and apply scenario probabilities before discounting. Full payment in 80% of cases still leaves a €12,000 expected shortfall at maturity here. At 5% that is about €11,429 today. I would test the distressed recovery and the probability separately so the credit team can challenge each assumption.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if the distressed state still repays €40,000 but a year later than before?

Compare with the follow-up answer

That recovery now has a lower present value. At 5%, delaying €40,000 from year one to year two reduces its present value by about €1,814.06 in the distressed state. Weighting by 20% raises the allowance by roughly €362.81. Even the same nominal recovery can imply more loss when delayed.
