Groups and currencies

Trace a parent’s ownership of a subsidiary, eliminate internal sales and distinguish the currency of operations from the currency used to present group accounts.

The starting point

A group can own several companies that invoice one another. Consolidated accounts describe entities under common control as one economic group. Sales between them disappear from group revenue because an outside customer has yet to buy anything.

A subsidiary can earn and spend in a different currency from the group report. Its functional currency reflects its primary economic environment. The presentation currency is the unit the group uses to report results. Record the exchange-rate convention before translating amounts.

Theory and method

Foreign-currency monetary item remeasurement

Functional currency · Exchange difference

A euro-functional company owes a USD supplier. Why can its euro profit fall before it transfers any dollars?

A French distributor records a dollar invoice when it receives goods. Its promise is to deliver a fixed number of dollars, so the number of euros needed changes when the dollar moves. The stored goods and the supplier obligation can follow different accounting rules. Recalculate the monetary payable at the reporting-date exchange rate and compare it with its recorded euro amount. This isolates the currency exposure while the dollar quantity remains fixed.

How the result is built

Euro payable = USD amount × euros per USD. Exchange loss on a payable = new euro payable − previous euro payable.

USD is the contract currency and EUR the business’s functional currency here. A monetary payable promises a fixed or determinable currency amount. A positive difference is a loss for the payer.

  1. Recognize a $20,000 invoice at €0.90 per dollar as €18,000 inventory and €18,000 payable.
  2. At year-end €0.95 per dollar makes the debt €19,000. Increase the liability by €1,000 and recognize the €1,000 exchange loss under the exercise assumptions.
  3. If the buyer pays at €0.93 next period it spends €18,600 and recognizes a €400 gain against the €19,000 opening payable. Across both periods the net loss is €600.

When the reasoning holds

  • The business’s functional currency is the euro. An entity operating chiefly in dollars would need its functional-currency determination before calculating this profit effect.
  • There is no qualifying hedge or other special accounting treatment, and inventory is measured at historical cost. Those facts keep the payable remeasurement separate from a hedge or fair-value movement.
Terms in this lesson
Revenue
Amount earned by selling goods or services. The customer may pay later.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Receivable
Money a customer owes for a sale already made.
Constant currency
Recalculating results at a common set of exchange rates to examine operating changes across periods.
Margin
In business accounts, profit expressed relative to sales. In derivatives trading, margin is collateral required to support a position.
First example

A parent transfers goods that cost €60 to its wholly owned subsidiary for €90. The subsidiary sells them outside the group for €120. Group revenue is €120, group cost is €60 and group gross profit is €60. The internal €90 is removed.

Which company belongs in the group?

A consolidated group treats a controlling parent and its controlled subsidiaries as one reporting unit. Control in the simple examples means the parent can direct relevant activities and is exposed to economic returns. A shareholding percentage is evidence but contracts and voting arrangements can change the assessment.

With partial ownership, an entity can still control a subsidiary and consolidate its assets and liabilities in full. The share of net assets or earnings attributable to outside owners is then identified as a non-controlling interest. Confusing ownership share with consolidation perimeter can lead an analyst to remove real group assets or include outside companies.

Strip out trading inside the perimeter

When a parent sells inventory to a subsidiary, one member records revenue and another records a purchase. Viewed from the group’s perspective the goods have only changed location. Clear the internal trade and any matching receivable and payable. Earnings on unsold goods still inside the group are unrealized externally.

If only some units have been sold to outsiders, identify which proportion of the internal margin remains in closing inventory. Remove that portion from both inventory carrying value and group profit. The profit on units sold externally is ultimately measured from the original external purchase cost to the outside selling price.

Currencies can change the story

Operational currency and reporting currency need not coincide. A subsidiary may invoice, pay staff and borrow in one main functional currency, while the group presents in another. Translation converts numbers for presentation. A separate issue arises when a company itself owes a fixed amount in a currency different from its functional currency.

For an introductory analytical comparison, convert foreign sales using the exchange-rate basis specified in the exercise. To study growth at constant currency, value both periods at one common rate. Actual consolidated reporting uses account-specific translation conventions. A movement in translated sales alone cannot show whether more products were sold.

Method

Make a simple consolidation bridge

  1. Set the perimeter

    Identify the group members, the outside customer and which balances are internal before translating or adding amounts.

  2. Follow the units

    Determine the seller’s external cost and internal margin. Trace which units exited the group and which remain in closing inventory.

  3. Translate consistently

    Define which currency one unit of the other buys. Separate a change in local sales from the conversion effect when discussing growth.

Worked case

An internal coat sale

A parent sends 50 coats costing €20 each to its wholly owned subsidiary at €30 each. The subsidiary sells 30 to outside customers for €45 each and retains 20. Ignore tax and other costs.

Working

  1. Standalone parent margin is €500. The subsidiary records outside revenue of €1,350 and cost of €900, making €450. Their standalone gross margins sum to €950.
  2. The 20 unsold coats include €10 internal margin each, or €200 unrealized. Remove this from group inventory and profit.
  3. Group external sales €1,350 less original cost of 30 × €20 = €600 gives €750 profit. Ending inventory is 20 × €20 = €400.

Interpretation

The €750 equals aggregate standalone margins of €950 less €200 unrealized. The €1,500 internal sale itself never appears in consolidated external revenue.

A common mistake

Someone removes the full internal markup even after part of the shipment has reached independent customers.

How to correct it

Use quantities or a defensible margin rate to isolate only unrealized profit in goods still inside the group at period-end.

Quick check
The subsidiary still holds the goods at period-end. At what amount does the group carry them, assuming no write-down?

The €30 markup was earned only inside the group. Removing that unrealized profit leaves the original external cost of €60 in consolidated inventory.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A parent sells goods costing €480 to its wholly owned distributor for €600. One quarter remains unsold. The rest sells for €690 outside the group. Separately, the group reports a foreign unit’s 100 local-currency sales at €0.80 per unit this year versus €0.90 last year on identical sales.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Calculate group profit and closing inventory on the goods.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    How much of the change in euro sales for the foreign unit reflects local activity?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

Outside revenue €690 less 75% × €480 = €360 external cost gives €330 group profit. Inventory is €120. Remove 25% × (€600 − €480) = €30 of unrealized markup.

Part B 3 marks

Sales convert to €80 versus €90, a €10 decline. With identical local sales and the same common exchange rate, the difference would be zero. In this example all the euro decline is translation.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A euro-functional company owes a USD supplier. Why can its euro profit fall before it transfers any dollars?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

The obligation is fixed in dollars, while this company reports its own transactions in euros. At year-end its €18,000 payable has grown to €19,000 because buying the same dollars would now cost more. That €1,000 is a currency loss even without a cash payment. I would keep it distinct from the inventory purchase and reconcile the later settlement to the remeasured closing balance.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Would the direction of the year-end profit effect reverse if the fixed $20,000 were owed by a customer to the distributor?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Yes. A dollar receivable becomes worth €19,000 instead of €18,000 when the dollar strengthens. The distributor holding that monetary asset has a €1,000 gain under these assumptions. If settlement later occurs at €0.93, the follow-on change is a €400 loss.
