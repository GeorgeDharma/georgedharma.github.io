Transactions and double entry

Each event changes at least two accounts. Borrowing increases cash and debt together, so it creates no immediate profit.

The starting point

A baker puts €2,000 of savings into a new shop. Two things become true at once. The shop holds more cash, and the owner has a larger claim on it. Double entry records both sides of that one event from the shop’s point of view.

An account collects changes in one item, such as cash or a bank loan. Each entry has equal debits and credits. These are the names of the two sides of the ledger. Whether a debit increases an account depends on the type of account.

Theory and method

Double-entry bookkeeping

Journal entry · Trial balance

A €900 supplier instalment was posted as an expense. The trial balance agrees to the cent. What correcting entry would you propose?

A hotel already recorded a linen delivery and owes the supplier €900. Paying later exchanges two things already on its books. Cash leaves and the promise to the supplier disappears. Recording a second expense treats the same delivery as if it had happened twice. A balanced journal detects unequal debit and credit totals, yet each of these competing stories could be written with equal totals. The invoice and the original posting tell us which story fits.

How the result is built

For every journal entry, total debits = total credits.

Debit and credit mean the left and right ledger columns in euros. Trade payables are supplier obligations. Linen expense is the cost of linen already consumed.

  1. Trace the original invoice. On delivery the hotel debited linen expense €900 and credited trade payables €900.
  2. On payment the posted journal debited expense €900 and credited cash €900. The cash side is already correct.
  3. Debit trade payables €900 and credit expense €900 to remove the duplicate expense and clear the obligation. The correction needs no further cash entry.

When the reasoning holds

  • The first invoice entry already exists and relates to the same delivery. Without it, the payment could represent the first recognition of expense.
  • The hotel received and consumed the linen. An unused advance to a supplier could instead represent an asset.

Terms in this lesson
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Revenue
Amount earned by selling goods or services. The customer may pay later.
Receivable
Money a customer owes for a sale already made.
Liability
Something owed to another party, such as a loan or an unpaid supplier bill.
First example

The shop borrows another €500. Debit cash €500 and credit the bank loan €500. Cash is now €2,500, financed by €500 of debt and €2,000 of owner capital. The loan created no sales.

Whose books are open?

A founder pays €600 personally for a machine used by a newly formed business. One payment creates different records for the founder and for the business. The business controls a machine and either owes its founder €600 or has received an owner contribution. The agreement determines which. The founder’s private bank account never enters the business ledger.

The accounting entity is the boundary around the transactions being measured. Ask who controls the resource, who owes the obligation, and whether cash moved inside that boundary. An invoice, bank statement and contribution agreement answer different parts of those questions. A cash receipt on its own seldom tells you whether its source is a sale, loan or investment.

How debits reach the statements

Debit and credit mean left and right in a two-sided account. Assets generally increase on the debit side. Liabilities and contributed capital generally increase on the credit side. Revenue increases owners’ residual earnings and normally has a credit balance. Expenses reduce those earnings and normally have a debit balance.

A journal is the dated record of both sides. Posting carries those sides to the individual ledger accounts. At period-end a trial balance lists their totals. Equal debits and credits tell you the arithmetic fits. They cannot detect an entire missing transaction, a duplicated balanced entry or an amount posted to the wrong expense.

Sales can require two entries

Consider a shop that hands over completed goods and gives the customer thirty days to pay. One entry records the customer’s promise and the revenue. A second removes goods from inventory and records their cost. The resulting profit belongs to the period of sale even if no cash comes in that day.

Collection later increases cash and reduces the receivable. On the other hand an unpaid supplier balance is a liability. Paying that supplier uses cash and reduces what the shop owes. Re-reading each event from the shop’s viewpoint keeps customers’ promises and the shop’s own promises on different sides.

Method

Build a small ledger

  1. Classify

    Name each affected account and identify whether it is an asset, liability, owner contribution, revenue or expense.

  2. Post

    Record the increases and decreases using equal debits and credits, allowing one transaction to affect more than two accounts.

  3. Reconcile

    Sum assets, obligations and equity. Then trace a receipt or invoice to show why at least one important balance belongs there.

Worked case

A weekend bicycle stall

Mira contributes €900 cash, buys goods for €400 cash, sells half the goods for €300 on account and collects €120 before closing.

Working

  1. Contribution debits cash €900 and credits capital €900. Purchase debits inventory €400 and credits cash €400.
  2. Delivery debits receivables €300 and credits revenue €300. Debit cost of sales €200 and credit inventory €200.
  3. Collection debits cash €120 and credits receivables €120. Ending cash €620, inventory €200 and receivables €180 sum to €1,000.

Interpretation

With no liabilities, equity is €900 of capital plus €100 of profit. Recording collection as a second sale would count revenue twice.

At the board

Post the transactions and prove the balance

You start a shop with €3,000 in cash from its owner and borrow €2,000 in cash. Then you buy €900 of stock for cash and sell goods costing €360 for €600 in cash. Ignore VAT, taxes and other costs. In the table, equity includes the owner’s contribution and the profit kept by the shop.

After this transactionCashInventoryBank debtEquity
Owner pays in capital€3,000€0€0€3,000
Bank advances the loan€5,000€0€2,000€3,000
Shop pays for stock€4,100€900€2,000€3,000
Customer pays and takes goods€4,700€540€2,000€3,240

The sale requires two pairs of entries. Debit cash €600 and credit revenue €600. Debit the cost of goods sold €360 and credit stock €360. Profit is €240. Closing assets of €5,240 equal €2,000 debt plus €3,240 equity. A cash sale can never tell you its own profit until you know the cost of what left the shop.

A common mistake

A balanced trial balance is treated as proof that all expenses were captured.

How to correct it

Trace invoices from an independent source into the ledger to look for missing expenses. Also trace ledger entries back to supporting documents to challenge whether they occurred.

Quick check
If the shop immediately repays €100 of the loan principal, what changes?

Repayment uses €100 of cash to discharge €100 of the obligation. With no interest or fee in this example, profit is unaffected.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A repairer starts with €1,500 of owner cash, purchases parts for €450 on supplier credit, then uses €150 of parts on a completed repair charged at €500. No bills have been paid.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Identify the debit and credit entries for the purchase and the repair.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Reconcile assets to claims and explain what happens if the customer pays €200 next week.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Debit inventory €450 and credit suppliers €450. Debit receivables €500 and credit revenue €500. Debit cost of sales €150 and credit inventory €150.

Part B 4 marks

Cash €1,500, receivables €500 and parts €300 total €2,300. Liabilities €450 plus equity €1,500 + €350 profit also total €2,300. Collection moves €200 from receivables to cash without changing profit.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A €900 supplier instalment was posted as an expense. The trial balance agrees to the cent. What correcting entry would you propose?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would verify the invoice and its ledger reference, then debit the supplier account and credit expense for €900. That leaves the cash payment intact, restores profit by €900 against the erroneous position, and removes a liability already settled. Agreement of the trial balance only shows equal posting totals. Both the mistake and the correction balance.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Suppose the hotel paid only €700 and charged that €700 to expense. What changes?

Compare with the follow-up answer

I would debit payables and credit expense for €700. The €900 delivery remains the sole expense, the €700 payment reduces cash and the unpaid €200 stays in trade payables. Correcting the full invoice would wipe out a genuine remaining obligation.
