Revenue and expenses

Recognize a sale when the relevant performance occurs. Match the cost of the goods sold to the sale and separate that event from payment.

The starting point

An online shop can receive money before it earns a sale, or deliver goods weeks before it collects payment. Accrual accounting assigns revenue to the period when the shop fulfils its promise to the customer.

For an ordinary sale of goods, assume control transfers on delivery. At delivery the shop recognizes revenue and the cost of the items sold. A customer advance collected earlier creates an obligation to deliver, and an unpaid sale creates a receivable.

Theory and method

Relative standalone selling price allocation

Performance obligation · Contract liability

A customer pays €1,080 for a delivered device and a year of support. How much revenue belongs to the first month?

A device buyer pays once but gets two different services from the seller. She can use the device at delivery, while a technician remains on call over the next twelve months. A single receipt cannot establish when each promise was fulfilled. One way to allocate a bundle price is to compare what the two promises sell for separately. This makes the discount apply in proportion to their individual prices before timing the recognition of each part.

How the result is built

Allocated priceᵢ = contract price × standalone priceᵢ / sum of standalone prices.

Index i identifies the distinct device or support promise. Standalone price is its observable separate selling price in euros. A contract liability is the amount received for performance still owed.

  1. The device sells separately for €900 and annual support for €300. Their total €1,200 exceeds the €1,080 bundle price, implying a common 10% discount.
  2. Allocate €810 to the device and €270 to the support. Delivery transfers control of the device; one month of evenly supplied support earns €270/12 = €22.50.
  3. By the end of month one total revenue is €832.50 and the remaining support liability is €247.50. Both amounts sum to the cash collected.

When the reasoning holds

  • The promises are distinct and the discount is allocated proportionally in this exercise. Evidence that a discount relates entirely to one item could change the allocation.
  • The customer receives support evenly through the year and the device transfers on day one. Material installation obligations could change the satisfaction date.

Terms in this lesson
Revenue
Amount earned by selling goods or services. The customer may pay later.
Receivable
Money a customer owes for a sale already made.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Liability
Something owed to another party, such as a loan or an unpaid supplier bill.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
First example

On 28 February, a customer pays €180 for a desk delivered on 4 March. The desk cost the shop €110. February ends with cash and a €180 customer advance. March records €180 of revenue and €110 of cost, producing €70 of gross profit.

Find the promised goods or service

A club sells a twelve-month membership for one payment. The member is buying access throughout the year. A shop selling a sealed camera usually promises something different, a good whose control passes at a particular point. Dates of order, invoicing, delivery and cash transfer can therefore give four different answers to when revenue arises.

Identify the customer, what has been promised, the amount earned and evidence of fulfilment. For simple services delivered evenly, elapsed time can measure performance. A large deposit alone gives no evidence of service already provided. If the service pattern is uneven, counting months mechanically may misstate how much work has been supplied.

Separate the bill from the work

Cash before performance creates a customer advance, often called deferred revenue or a contract liability. Performance before unconditional collection can give rise to an asset. In a simple completed credit sale where only the passage of time remains before payment, the asset is a receivable. Different contract conditions require further analysis.

In the next period the liability decreases as the seller delivers. Collection of an existing receivable simply changes which asset the seller holds. Compare movements in advances and receivables with reported revenue to understand whether growth reflects delivery, advance sales, or a buildup of customer credit.

What becomes an expense

The cost of an item sold leaves inventory when that sale is recognized. Costs of services consumed in the period can become expenses even before a supplier invoices the business. If a payment buys a right to future service, an unused portion can remain a prepaid asset.

A useful test asks what resource remains at period-end and whether the business still controls it. A paid insurance policy with six unused months has a future benefit. An advert that has already run generally does not give the firm an identifiable remaining service, even if managers hope it raises future sales.

Method

Construct a timing bridge

  1. Mark each date

    Write down the contract start, cash movement, fulfillment and reporting date independently.

  2. Allocate the performance

    Measure how much of the agreed service has been supplied by that date. Show your basis if the contract spans periods.

  3. Classify the remainder

    Distinguish cash, customer promises, services still owed to customers, and unconsumed services bought from suppliers.

Worked case

A prepaid maintenance agreement

On 1 October a workshop collects €9,600 for twelve months of evenly delivered maintenance. It also prepays €2,400 for twelve months of workshop insurance beginning that day. Its year ends on 31 December.

Working

  1. Three months of access have been supplied. Revenue is 3/12 × €9,600 = €2,400. The remaining €7,200 is a customer obligation.
  2. Insurance consumed is 3/12 × €2,400 = €600. The prepayment asset at the reporting date is €1,800.
  3. Ignoring other activity, profit is €2,400 − €600 = €1,800. Net cash received was €7,200, a different measure.

Interpretation

The unused insurance asset and unearned membership liability explain why timing differences can affect both sides of the balance sheet.

A common mistake

The invoice date is accepted as conclusive proof of delivery.

How to correct it

Check dispatch documents, client acceptance, contract terms and activity either side of year-end. A December invoice for January work can overstate December revenue.

Quick check
If the same desk is delivered in March but paid for in April, when is the €70 gross profit recognized?

Delivery in March satisfies the shop’s promise and releases the cost from inventory. April collection simply swaps a receivable for cash.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A seller collects €1,200 on 1 November for six months of evenly supplied monitoring and, separately, delivers goods costing €190 on 28 December for €300 on credit. Its year ends 31 December.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate total recognized revenue, the advance remaining, and year-end receivables.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    There are no service costs or other transactions. Reconcile profit with the customer cash received.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Monitoring earned 2/6 × €1,200 = €400 and goods earned €300, giving €700 total revenue. €800 remains deferred and the goods sale leaves €300 receivable.

Part B 4 marks

Profit €700 − €190 = €510. Cash collected is €1,200. The €800 unearned portion increases cash above revenue and €300 uncollected revenue reduces it. The €190 inventory cost affects profit separately.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A customer pays €1,080 for a delivered device and a year of support. How much revenue belongs to the first month?

Compare with a spoken answer

I would first show that hardware and support are distinct promises. Using the separate prices gives €810 for the device and €270 for support. Assuming delivery on the first day and an even support service, first-month revenue is €832.50. The remaining €247.50 is an obligation to serve the customer in later months. I would verify delivery evidence and support dates before closing the ledger.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Suppose the device is delivered on the first day but support starts only next month. Does collecting all the cash accelerate support revenue?

Compare with the follow-up answer

For the current month I would recognize the €810 device allocation and retain €270 as a liability. The customer has paid, but the technician’s promised period of availability has yet to begin. Recognition of the €22.50 monthly support amounts starts when service starts.
