The balance sheet

Assets show resources controlled at a date. Liabilities are obligations. Equity is the residual owners would claim at the reported carrying values.

The starting point

Suppose you could photograph a business’s finances at closing time. Its balance sheet would show the resources it controls, the obligations it owes, and the remainder attributable to owners. This is a snapshot at a particular date.

The balance sheet equation is assets = liabilities + equity. Equity is the residual measured under the accounting policies used. A machine’s carrying amount may differ from its resale price, so reported equity need not equal the price someone would pay for the business.

Theory and method

Current ratio and quick ratio

Liquidity ratios · Acid-test ratio

Would you accept a manager’s claim that selling slow stock at cost will strengthen both of these liquidity ratios?

A wholesaler needs money for next month’s bills. Its current ratio counts stock alongside receivables and cash, while the quick ratio focuses on assets more readily usable for payment. A cash sale at carrying cost simply replaces one current asset with another. Yet its economic importance can be considerable if the stock was hard to sell. Compare the two balance-sheet measures before and after the transaction, then ask when suppliers actually demand cash.

How the result is built

Current ratio = CA/CL; quick ratio = (cash + readily collectible receivables + qualifying liquid investments)/CL.

CA is current assets and CL is current liabilities on the same date, both measured in euros. The ratios are multiples. The exercise has no liquid investments or prepaid items.

  1. Start with €12 cash, €18 collectible receivables, €30 inventory and €40 current liabilities. The current ratio is 60/40 = 1.50 and quick ratio 30/40 = 0.75.
  2. Sell €20 of that stock for €20 cash. Current assets remain €60. Quick assets become €50 and their ratio becomes 1.25.
  3. A subsequent €10 supplier payment reduces cash and liabilities together. The new current ratio is 50/30 ≈ 1.67 and the quick ratio is 40/30 ≈ 1.33.

When the reasoning holds

  • The stock sells for cash at its carrying amount. Credit terms delay the improvement in available cash, and a discount reduces total assets.
  • The denominator contains all current obligations due under the assumed classification. An omitted tax or loan instalment makes both indicators optimistic.

Terms in this lesson
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Working capital
Money tied up in day-to-day operations. For a deal, agree which receivables, stock and payables count.
Receivable
Money a customer owes for a sale already made.
First example

A café lists €12,000 cash, €3,000 inventory, and equipment carried at €15,000. Its assets total €30,000. With suppliers owed €4,000 and a bank loan of €16,000, equity is €30,000 − €20,000 = €10,000.

A dated statement and a period of change

Imagine signing a contract on 31 December and receiving the bank proceeds on 2 January. A balance sheet is tied to the stated date. The underlying rights and obligations as they exist on that date govern recognition. A list of what happened during January cannot simply be added to the December balances.

A statement of changes or a cash flow statement explains movements between two snapshots. An asset ending €5,000 higher might reflect purchases of €20,000 and sales of €15,000. The net change alone cannot reveal either gross flow. Ask for opening balances and transaction detail when the question concerns activity.

What capital can absorb

Equity is the difference between recorded assets and liabilities. An unexpected asset write-down reduces this cushion before all creditors necessarily suffer a loss. A firm with positive equity can nevertheless fail to pay on time if most of its assets consist of long-lived equipment that cannot be sold promptly for their book amount.

Liquidity concerns access to funds when payments come due. Solvency concerns the capacity of the asset base and future business to support obligations overall. Compare genuinely available liquid resources with a maturity schedule. Cash pledged to guarantee another contract may not be available to pay tomorrow’s supplier.

Working capital as a first screen

A common indicator is current assets minus current liabilities. Items expected to turn into cash or be settled in the near term enter their respective groups under the applicable classification rules. This subtraction can reveal a financing gap, but two firms with the same number can face quite different timing.

Read individual lines. A fresh customer invoice due before payday has a different liquidity profile from old inventory with no buyer. Longer supplier terms can help fund inventory today but create a concentration of bills next month. A business receiving cash up front can operate for long periods with negative working capital.

Method

Read a maturity mismatch

  1. Fix the date

    Which resources and commitments are present at the reporting date, and could any recorded cash have restrictions?

  2. Measure the buffer

    Calculate equity and net current assets separately. A large equity balance does not say how much is immediately available.

  3. Overlay the calendar

    List receipts likely before each payment and test whether collection delays would exhaust the cash balance.

Worked case

A clinic with valuable equipment

A clinic has €14,000 cash, €26,000 receivables and €80,000 equipment. Suppliers are owed €32,000 and long-term bank debt is €55,000. Suppliers want €25,000 within thirty days.

Working

  1. Assets total €120,000. Total liabilities of €87,000 leave €33,000 equity, so the accounting residual is positive.
  2. Current assets €40,000 less €32,000 of current liabilities give €8,000 net current assets. Assume no current bank instalment.
  3. If only €6,000 of receivables arrive in the next month, the clinic has €20,000 against €25,000 due, a €5,000 shortfall.

Interpretation

The clinic could seek earlier collections or committed funding. Cutting the carrying value of equipment changes equity yet produces no immediate cash.

At the board

Post the transactions and prove the balance

You start a shop with €3,000 in cash from its owner and borrow €2,000 in cash. Then you buy €900 of stock for cash and sell goods costing €360 for €600 in cash. Ignore VAT, taxes and other costs. In the table, equity includes the owner’s contribution and the profit kept by the shop.

After this transactionCashInventoryBank debtEquity
Owner pays in capital€3,000€0€0€3,000
Bank advances the loan€5,000€0€2,000€3,000
Shop pays for stock€4,100€900€2,000€3,000
Customer pays and takes goods€4,700€540€2,000€3,240

The sale requires two pairs of entries. Debit cash €600 and credit revenue €600. Debit the cost of goods sold €360 and credit stock €360. Profit is €240. Closing assets of €5,240 equal €2,000 debt plus €3,240 equity. A cash sale can never tell you its own profit until you know the cost of what left the shop.

A common mistake

The entire amount of receivables is counted as cash available today.

How to correct it

Use due dates, actual collection experience and disputed balances to build a dated receipt schedule. Keep a conservative scenario alongside the contractual one.

Quick check
The café pays a €1,000 supplier balance from cash. What is equity immediately afterwards?

Cash and supplier obligations both fall €1,000. The new residual is €29,000 of assets less €19,000 of liabilities, still €10,000.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A distributor has €9,000 unrestricted cash, €18,000 inventory, €13,000 receivables and €40,000 equipment. It owes €26,000 within a year and €30,000 later.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Find equity and net current assets.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Only €4,000 will be collected before a €16,000 payment, with no sales proceeds or credit line available. Diagnose the immediate problem and quantify it.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Assets are €80,000 and liabilities €56,000, leaving €24,000 equity. Current assets are €40,000, so net current assets are €14,000.

Part B 4 marks

€9,000 cash + €4,000 collections = €13,000. The funding gap is €3,000. Positive equity and net current assets do not guarantee funds by the date required.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Would you accept a manager’s claim that selling slow stock at cost will strengthen both of these liquidity ratios?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Only the quick ratio rises on the sale itself. Cash replaces inventory inside current assets, so the current ratio stays 1.50. Quick assets rise to €50 and the quick ratio reaches 1.25. I would still compare cash and dated collections with next month’s bills. A presentable reporting-date ratio cannot show a Tuesday funding gap.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if stock carried at €20 could sell for only €14 cash?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Current assets fall €6 to €54 and the current ratio falls to 1.35. Quick assets rise from €30 to €44, giving 1.10. The business realizes a €6 loss while improving access to cash, which explains why a single liquidity statistic gives an incomplete answer.
