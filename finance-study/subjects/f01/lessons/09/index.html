Financial ratios

Decompose return on equity into profit margin, asset turnover and financial leverage. Improving return on equity can reflect higher debt as well as better trading.

The starting point

If two firms report the same return to owners, the underlying businesses can still be very different. One may earn more on each sale while another finances more of its assets with borrowing. Ratios help separate those explanations.

The DuPont identity multiplies profit margin, asset turnover and financial leverage. Write the components as net income/sales, sales/average assets and average assets/average equity. Cancelling repeated amounts leaves net income/average equity, the return on equity for that period.

Theory and method

Five-factor DuPont analysis

Tax burden · Interest burden · Operating margin

Two firms have the same operating margin. What separates the effects of financing and tax when their return on equity differs?

A company can sell the same goods with the same operating profit as a rival yet deliver less of that profit to owners. Interest takes a portion before tax, taxes take another portion, and the business uses a particular mix of assets and equity. Write the intermediate profit claims in sequence so each adjacent numerator cancels the next denominator. This exposes which link changed without assuming any single link caused the final result.

How the result is built

ROE = (NI/EBT) × (EBT/EBIT) × (EBIT/S) × (S/A) × (A/E).

NI is net income, EBT profit before tax, EBIT profit before interest and tax, S sales, A average assets and E average equity. All use a common currency, period and ownership scope. ROE is return on equity.

  1. For sales €200, EBIT €40, interest €10 and tax €6, EBT is €30 and NI €24. Tax retention is 24/30 = 0.80 and interest retention 30/40 = 0.75.
  2. Operating margin is 40/200 = 20%. At €100 average assets and €60 average equity, turnover is 2 and the equity multiplier about 1.667.
  3. Multiplying 0.80 × 0.75 × 0.20 × 2 × (100/60) gives 0.40, which agrees with €24/€60. Factors reveal distinct paths to the same ratio.

When the reasoning holds

  • EBT, EBIT and average equity must be nonzero for this decomposition. Very small or negative denominators make interpretation unstable.
  • Profit and claim definitions must align across companies. Different treatment of minority owners or one-off tax benefits can distort the apparent factor comparison.
Terms in this lesson
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Margin
In business accounts, profit expressed relative to sales. In derivatives trading, margin is collateral required to support a position.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
First example

A firm earns €6 on €100 of sales, uses average assets of €50 and has average equity of €25. Its return on equity is 6% × 2 × 2 = 24%. The same figure comes from €6 divided by €25.

What question does a ratio answer?

A buyer comparing retailers asks whether one earns more from each sale, generates more sales from its assets, or uses more borrowing. Each question uses a different denominator. Net margin divides net income by sales, turnover divides sales by average assets, and financial leverage divides average assets by average equity.

Use an average balance when comparing a full-period flow with a stock that can change during the year. The simple mean of opening and closing balances is an approximation. If assets were acquired on the final day, that mean can overstate the capital actually in use throughout the year.

Keep the numerator and denominator aligned

Return on equity is best interpreted for the same claimants on both sides. In a simple single-owner business, net income divided by average equity fits. In a group with outside minority owners, mixing parent-attributable income and the whole group’s equity produces a ratio that does not correspond clearly to either group of investors.

The DuPont decomposition is an identity, an arithmetic relationship when the same definitions are used throughout. It is not a causal model. More leverage can increase return when operating returns exceed funding costs, but new interest expense or falling margins can reverse that result. Tiny or negative equity makes the ratio especially hard to interpret.

Evaluate growth in context

Ratios compare proportions and can hide changes in scale. A firm can grow euro profit while its margin falls if it sells sufficiently more. Sales can also rise temporarily when unusually generous credit terms pull future business into the current period. Ask how quickly customers pay and whether inventory rises alongside the growth.

Cross-check a ratio with an adjacent measure. A rising return on equity accompanied by flat margins, weakening cash conversion and a shrinking equity base suggests a different explanation from one driven by stronger products. A useful answer distinguishes what the ratios prove arithmetically from what requires evidence in the financial statements.

Method

Decompose and interpret

  1. Build the denominators

    Average the balances over the relevant period, note abnormal opening or closing dates, and align income with owners.

  2. Multiply the components

    Compute each ratio separately and verify that their product equals the direct return on equity.

  3. Investigate the largest movement

    Identify a specific operating or funding change that could explain it, then request the evidence needed to distinguish explanations.

Worked case

Two firms earning the same profit

Bayside and Ridge each have annual sales €240m and net income €12m. Bayside has €120m average assets and €60m average equity. Ridge has €160m average assets and €40m average equity.

Working

  1. Both have a 5% margin. Bayside turns assets twice and has leverage of 2, giving return on equity 5% × 2 × 2 = 20%.
  2. Ridge turns assets 1.5 times and has leverage of 4, giving 5% × 1.5 × 4 = 30%. Directly, €12m/€40m gives the same 30%.
  3. Ridge earns the higher percentage on a smaller equity base despite generating fewer sales per euro of assets. It did not earn greater euro profit.

Interpretation

Examine Ridge’s financing costs, debt maturities and whether its assets were acquired late in the year before concluding that its shareholders face a better bargain.

A common mistake

An increase in return on equity is called evidence that sales operations improved.

How to correct it

Break out margin, turnover and leverage, and compare the period’s absolute earnings. A shrinking denominator can improve a ratio even with unchanged operations.

Quick check
Holding margin and asset turnover fixed, what raises return on equity in this identity?

Greater assets per euro of equity magnify the calculated return for the stated fixed operating ratios. In real borrowing decisions, interest charges may also change the margin.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A company reports €180m sales and €9m net income. Assets increase from €90m to €110m and equity from €40m to €50m. Assume the simple average is representative.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Calculate all three components and return on equity.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    At identical sales and balances, profit falls to €6.3m due to interest. Which component changes and what is the new return?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

Average assets €100m and equity €45m. Margin 5%, turnover 1.8, leverage 100/45 ≈ 2.22. Product is 20%, equal to €9m/€45m.

Part B 3 marks

Margin falls to 3.5%. Turnover and leverage are unchanged on the given balances. Return becomes €6.3m/€45m = 14%, a six-percentage-point decline.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Two firms have the same operating margin. What separates the effects of financing and tax when their return on equity differs?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would split net margin into operating margin, the fraction surviving financing costs, and the fraction surviving tax. In this company 20 cents of every sales euro becomes operating profit, 75% of that survives interest and 80% survives tax. I then combine those figures with asset turnover and leverage. That gives 40% and shows exactly which period inputs produced it.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if financing costs rise by €5, the tax rate stays 20%, and sales, operating profit and average balances do not change?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Pretax profit falls from €30 to €25, tax becomes €5 and net income falls to €20. Return on €60 equity is now 33.33%. Operating margin and turnover are unchanged by assumption. The interest-retention term falls from 75% to 62.5%, with tax retention still 80%.
