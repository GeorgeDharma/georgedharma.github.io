Debt, leases and equity

Separate finance charges from repayment of principal. Compare financing obligations using both the balance sheet and the accompanying notes.

The starting point

A loan payment often combines two different things. Interest pays the lender for the use of money. Principal repayment returns part of the money borrowed. Equity is the owners’ residual claim after obligations are covered.

Leases can also commit a business to future payments. In cases with a recognized lease liability, its balance, interest and maturity schedule help you compare the commitment with borrowing. Notes about due dates also reveal whether near-term cash pressure differs between companies with similar total debt.

Theory and method

Effective interest method

Amortized cost · Discount accretion

A lender advances €900 and receives €990 in a year. Which portion becomes interest income even if the note calls its cash coupon zero?

A note can promise repayment only at maturity and still compensate its lender for time. The lender starts with a €900 investment and will receive €990 a year later. The difference appears over the holding period as interest under an effective-rate measure. Reading the word “coupon” alone would miss that financing income. Track how the carrying amount grows toward the contractual cash amount even when the borrower sends nothing during the year.

How the result is built

Closing carrying amount = opening carrying amount × (1 + effective period rate) − contractual cash collected.

Carrying amount is the loan asset in euros. The effective rate is the rate matching the original advance to the contractual future cash under the simplified facts. Cash collected includes coupons or principal paid at period-end.

  1. Solve €900 × (1+i) = €990 to obtain the effective annual rate i = 10%. The €90 discount compensates the lender over one year.
  2. For a two-year zero-coupon example with a €900 advance at the same annual rate, year-one interest is €90 and closing carrying amount €990.
  3. Year-two interest is €99, taking the asset to €1,089. A €1,089 redemption then removes the asset and brings in cash.

When the reasoning holds

  • All payments occur as promised and there are no extra fees, prepayments or impairments. Credit loss would require its own measurement.
  • The note is measured in this example on an amortized-cost basis. A fair-value measurement could change the carrying amount when market rates change.
Terms in this lesson
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Principal
The amount borrowed or invested before interest is added.
Liability
Something owed to another party, such as a loan or an unpaid supplier bill.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
How to read the notation
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.

All notation examples

First example

A borrower starts the year owing €10,000 at 6%. A €2,600 payment at year-end includes €600 interest and €2,000 principal. Debt falls to €8,000. The full €2,600 leaves cash, while only €600 is this year’s interest expense.

Read the funding promise

A shareholder can receive dividends if the business distributes money. A lender normally has contractual rights to interest and principal. Some instruments mix features, so begin with the actual payment terms rather than their marketing names. A long maturity does not remove an obligation to pay, while an ordinary share need not require future redemption.

Separate three exam questions. What amount is recorded at the reporting date? How much finance cost arose during the period? What cash must be found by each future date? A single total cannot answer all three. Principal instalments, maturity dates and fixed or floating terms determine whether accounting expense corresponds closely to the near-term payment.

Why present value appears in a lease

A lessee may gain the right to use equipment for several periods while committing to future rentals. In a simple recognized lease, the initial liability is the present value of unpaid contractual payments, discounted using the rate specified for the exercise. With no other adjustments, the right-of-use asset can start at the same amount.

Each period, interest builds on the opening liability and the cash payment reduces it. The right-of-use asset is depreciated under its own schedule. Thus the amount reducing the liability is different from the expense charged for using the equipment. Actual recognition rules depend on the applicable standard and the facts.

Compare a loan with a rental commitment

A business using leased vans can appear different from one using identical vans bought with borrowed money. Include recognized lease liabilities consistently when constructing financing measures. Check maturity schedules for both forms of funding and identify contract payments omitted from a simplified estimate.

Equity can be substantial yet offer few funds with which to pay the next instalment. A creditor evaluating rollover risk, the risk of being unable to refinance maturing debt, examines committed cash and borrowing lines along with likely future funding costs. A company with a large bullet repayment has concentrated that risk in one date.

Method

Unpack a financing schedule

  1. Read the opening principal

    Use the balance just before interest accrues and a rate for that exact period. Separate fees or fresh advances if given.

  2. Allocate the payment

    Interest increases the liability until paid. The part of the scheduled cash payment above interest reduces principal.

  3. Link to the statements

    Compare the closing liability, finance expense and cash payment. For leased equipment calculate depreciation separately from liability amortization.

Worked case

Two rentals on a small press

A print shop promises €1,100 at the end of year one and €1,210 at the end of year two. Use 10% annually and ignore fees, taxes and residual value. The lease term equals the two-year service period.

Working

  1. The opening liability is €1,100/1.10 + €1,210/1.10² = €2,000. Assume the right-of-use asset also starts at €2,000.
  2. Year one interest is €200. Pay €1,100, reducing principal by €900 to €1,100. Year two interest €110 and payment €1,210 then clear it.
  3. Straight-line depreciation is €1,000 per year. Year one’s combined depreciation and interest are €1,200 versus €1,100 of rental cash.

Interpretation

The year-one payment does not measure the expense or principal reduction alone. State which figure your analysis needs.

A common mistake

A debt maturity note is added in full to the reported liability.

How to correct it

Check whether the note lists undiscounted future contractual payments, including interest on the same obligation. Use it for timing. Avoid treating this disclosure as another, separate borrowing.

Quick check
What should an analyst add to the debt total to judge whether the borrower can meet its payments next quarter?

An obligation due in three years creates a different immediate funding need from one due tomorrow. The timing of payments must be compared with cash the borrower can access.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A term loan opens at €18,000 and accrues 5% annual interest. The borrower pays €4,900 after one year and €4,700 one year later, with no new advances or fees.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Prepare two years of interest, principal paid and closing balances.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    A second bank quotes an identical opening balance and rate with all principal due at maturity. Explain the difference a lender should investigate.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

Year one interest €900, principal paid €4,000 and close €14,000. Year two interest €700, principal paid €4,000 and close €10,000.

Part B 3 marks

Equal opening balances need not imply equal near-term cash requirements. Gradual repayments bring forward funding needs but reduce later exposure and future interest. The bullet loan concentrates principal and refinancing risk at its maturity.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A lender advances €900 and receives €990 in a year. Which portion becomes interest income even if the note calls its cash coupon zero?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

The one-year note earns €90 of interest on the €900 lent, an effective rate of 10%. I would accrue that return into the carrying amount before redemption. The coupon only describes separately scheduled periodic cash. The price advanced and the total redemption determine the lending return here. On a longer note the same effective percentage would apply to the updated opening balance each period.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If a two-year borrower pays €50 after year one on the €900 advance and still has an agreed 10% effective rate, what final payment is consistent?

Compare with the follow-up answer

After year-one interest of €90 and €50 received, the loan asset is €940. Year two adds €94 of interest. A final €1,034 receipt clears the asset. Keeping the old €1,089 redemption as well would imply a different effective rate.
