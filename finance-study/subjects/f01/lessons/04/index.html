Cash flow and reconciliation

Start with profit, reverse noncash items and adjust for amounts unpaid or uncollected. Explain how a profitable firm can use cash.

The starting point

A profitable wholesaler can still struggle to pay staff if customers pay late. The cash flow statement explains how much cash actually moved. Its operating section often starts from profit and reconciles differences in timing.

Depreciation reduces profit without spending cash in the current period, so add it back. Rising receivables and inventory generally absorb operating cash. Rising supplier balances generally conserve it. Keep investing and financing flows separate from this operating calculation.

Theory and method

Indirect cash flow reconciliation with a disposal gain

Cash flow from operations · Investing cash flow

A business sold an old machine for €18,000 and reported a €5,000 profit on the sale. Which number goes where in its cash flow statement?

A workshop selling equipment receives the full amount from the buyer. Its income statement compares that amount with the machine’s remaining book value, so only the difference enters profit. If the operating cash calculation starts from profit, it inherits the smaller gain. Without correcting it, a report would count part of an investing transaction inside operations as well. Separate the cash event from the accounting removal of the carrying amount.

How the result is built

Gain on disposal = proceeds − carrying amount. Indirect operating cash = profit + noncash charges − disposal gain + operating balance adjustments.

Proceeds mean actual cash collected for the machine. Carrying amount is its recorded cost less accumulated depreciation. The exercise measures each figure in thousands of euros.

  1. The machine carried at €13k sells for €18k cash. Debit cash €18k, remove the €13k carrying amount and recognize a €5k gain.
  2. Net income is €32k including that €5k gain. Add €7k depreciation and subtract the €5k gain, giving €34k before working-capital adjustments.
  3. Trade receivables rose €4k, leaving operating cash of €30k. The full €18k is an investing receipt. Together those two sections bring in €48k.

When the reasoning holds

  • The sale was collected in cash during the period. A buyer’s note receivable would defer the cash component until collected.
  • The machine was used in production. A dealer selling machines held as routine trading inventory would ordinarily treat those customer receipts differently.

Terms in this lesson
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Receivable
Money a customer owes for a sale already made.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Payable
Money a business owes a supplier for goods or services already received.
First example

A company earns €40,000, including €5,000 depreciation. Uncollected customer balances rise €12,000, inventory rises €8,000 and unpaid supplier balances rise €3,000. With no other adjustments, operating cash is €40,000 + €5,000 − €12,000 − €8,000 + €3,000 = €28,000.

Two routes to operating cash

A business can calculate cash from operations by summing customer receipts and cash paid to suppliers and staff. This direct route asks what actually passed through the bank for ordinary trading. The indirect route starts from accrual profit and removes timing and noncash effects to reach the same operating amount.

If the routes disagree, inspect the period covered, classification of cash flows and whether gross or net balances were used. Gains on asset sales may appear in profit although the sale proceeds belong in investing cash flows. Remove the gain on the indirect bridge, then show actual proceeds once in investing.

Turn balance movements back into cash

If every sale is on credit, a simplified receivable account starts with the opening amount, adds sales, subtracts collections and closes at the ending balance. Rearranging yields collections = sales + opening receivables − closing receivables. Write-offs, foreign currency translation and acquired receivables can also change the balance in real accounts.

Inventory introduces a second bridge. Purchases = cost of goods sold + closing inventory − opening inventory, under simple cost assumptions. Supplier cash paid equals purchases + opening trade payables − closing trade payables if everything is bought on credit. Work each account separately before combining the signs.

Read weak cash conversion carefully

One year of low operating cash can reflect valuable growth if new orders require inventory and short-term credit for customers. It can also reflect customers taking longer to pay or a business accumulating unsold goods. The same arithmetic supports different explanations, so compare it with sales growth, ageing schedules and subsequent collections.

Cash from operations also moves when payment dates shift across a reporting boundary. Delaying suppliers can improve current cash while making next month harder. When comparing years, normalize the trading calendar and see whether the underlying cash cycle is improving or the firm merely borrowed time from suppliers.

Method

Recover the direct cash number

  1. Receipts

    Reconcile sales to collections with opening and closing customer balances. Adjust separately for any noncash changes.

  2. Payments

    Reconstruct purchases from inventory movements, then adjust them for movements in unpaid supplier bills.

  3. Cross-check

    Reconcile operating profit using the same working-capital movements and any noncash charges. A balanced bridge explains each difference.

Worked case

A bookseller’s trading year

A bookseller records sales of €120,000 and cost of goods sold of €72,000. Receivables rise €9,000, inventory rises €6,000 and supplier balances rise €4,000. Cash salaries are €20,000 and depreciation is €3,000. All sales and purchases are on credit.

Working

  1. Collections are €120,000 − €9,000 = €111,000. Purchases are €72,000 + €6,000 = €78,000.
  2. Cash paid to suppliers is €78,000 − €4,000 = €74,000. Direct operating cash is €111,000 − €74,000 − €20,000 = €17,000.
  3. Profit is €25,000. Adding depreciation €3,000, subtracting €9,000 and €6,000, then adding €4,000 also produces €17,000.

Interpretation

A purchase of new store equipment would be an investing outflow separately. Its price does not belong in the customer and supplier bridge above.

A common mistake

Depreciation is described as cash generated by equipment.

How to correct it

It is added back because it was deducted in profit without a current cash payment. The operating cash itself came from the net trading receipts.

Quick check
If the company collects €4,000 more from customers before year-end with profit unchanged, operating cash becomes what?

Extra collections reduce the increase in receivables by €4,000. Cash from operations rises by that amount even though the sales were already recognized.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A wholesaler earns €56,000 before depreciation of €8,000, with no tax or financing costs. Receivables fall €5,000, inventory rises €14,000 and payables fall €4,000. Equipment costing €11,000 is acquired for cash.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate operating cash from the reported profit after depreciation.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Calculate the combined operating and investing cash movement and explain the payable sign.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Profit is €48,000. Operating cash is €48,000 + €8,000 + €5,000 − €14,000 − €4,000 = €43,000.

Part B 4 marks

Deduct the €11,000 equipment purchase to reach €32,000 net cash inflow across these sections. Falling payables mean cash paid exceeds this period’s credit purchases by €4,000.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A business sold an old machine for €18,000 and reported a €5,000 profit on the sale. Which number goes where in its cash flow statement?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would remove the €5,000 disposal gain from the indirect operating bridge and put the full €18,000 cash receipt in investing. The gain measures proceeds above the €13,000 carrying amount and explains the profit effect. It does not measure the cash collected. Using the given operating adjustments gives €30,000 operating cash, before adding the separate investing proceeds.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If the machine were sold for €10,000 rather than €18,000 with the same €13,000 carrying amount, how would the profit-to-cash bridge respond?

Compare with the follow-up answer

A €3,000 disposal loss would reduce profit, so I would add it back in the operating bridge and show €10,000 under investing. Operating cash from the business would be unchanged if all trading activity were identical. The cash loss of €8,000 versus the first offer appears through lower investing proceeds.
