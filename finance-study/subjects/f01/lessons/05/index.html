Inventory and fixed assets

Buying stock delays an expense until sale. Buying equipment usually spreads its cost over its useful life through depreciation.

The starting point

A delivery shop buys both bicycles for resale and a van for its drivers. Their purpose determines how their costs reach profit. Bicycles held for resale start as inventory. The van supplies services over several years and normally starts as equipment.

When a bicycle is sold, its recorded cost becomes cost of goods sold. Depreciation allocates the van’s depreciable amount over its estimated useful life. Straight-line depreciation allocates equal amounts if the expected use supports that pattern.

Theory and method

Lower of cost and net realizable value

Inventory write-down · Net realizable value

A retailer expects to sell damaged chairs for more than their purchase cost. Could the inventory still require a write-down?

A furniture shop holds a scratched chair and has found a buyer who will pay €120 after it is repaired and delivered. The offer looks attractive beside its €110 recorded cost. Yet the shop must still pay for repairs and selling expenses to complete that sale. Compare what the item can recover after these unavoidable steps with the amount already carried. A high headline price can conceal a loss waiting in inventory.

How the result is built

NRV = estimated selling price − remaining completion costs − costs necessary to make the sale. Closing carrying amount = min(cost, NRV).

NRV means net realizable value per item in euros. Cost is the recorded acquisition or production cost. The minimum chooses the lower recoverable carrying amount in the example.

  1. A chair costs €110. Expected sale €120 less €12 finishing and €8 selling costs gives €100 net, so reduce carrying value by €10.
  2. For 30 identical chairs, reduce inventory and period profit by €300. No customer cash has yet arrived.
  3. If price falls further to €115 with the same €20 necessary costs, net value becomes €95. The additional reduction is €5 per chair beyond the first write-down.

When the reasoning holds

  • The sale estimate and necessary costs are grounded in reasonable current evidence. An unverified list price can overstate recovery.
  • The chairs with similar condition are the units assessed. Averaging their losses with profits on unrelated healthy stock could conceal a write-down.

Terms in this lesson
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Margin
In business accounts, profit expressed relative to sales. In derivatives trading, margin is collateral required to support a position.
First example

Ten bicycles at €200 cost €2,000. Selling three leaves €1,400 in inventory and moves €600 to cost of goods sold. A €12,000 van with a €2,000 estimated residual value and a five-year useful life has annual straight-line depreciation of €2,000 for a full year.

Which units left the shelf?

A grocer buys identical bottles at several prices. Selling one bottle requires assigning a cost to the sale and a cost to what remains. A first-in, first-out cost formula, commonly called FIFO, assigns the earliest purchases to cost of goods sold. A weighted-average formula shares the cost across units available under the chosen averaging period.

Both allocations can describe the same sales proceeds and physical quantities. When purchase prices rise over time, FIFO generally assigns older, lower unit costs to the sale and larger amounts to ending inventory than an average formula. That difference affects both gross margin and the value shown on the balance sheet.

Cost recovery and selling value

Inventory that is damaged or obsolete may no longer recover its recorded cost. In a lower-of-cost-and-net-realizable-value example, estimate the selling price less the costs still needed to finish and sell the item. Compare that amount with its recorded cost to determine whether a write-down is needed.

Equipment creates a different pattern. Its capitalized cost includes amounts necessary to prepare it for its intended use in this example. Spread depreciable cost, or cost less estimated residual value, over useful life once available for use. Routine maintenance consumed in ordinary running generally creates a current expense.

Changing an estimate

A truck may wear out faster than expected. Starting from its carrying amount at the date of reassessment, allocate the revised remaining depreciable amount over the revised remaining useful life. A revised estimate changes the pattern going forward in this example. It does not require erasing the original depreciation merely because new evidence arrived.

Examine use records, maintenance plans, expected resale proceeds and management’s prior estimates. A firm can increase current earnings by extending lives in a model. An analyst should compare the new assumption with physical use and peers before accepting the interpretation as improved operating performance.

Method

Reconcile quantities and carrying amounts

  1. Count

    Reconcile beginning units plus purchases less units sold to ending units before pricing anything.

  2. Allocate

    Apply one stated cost-flow method consistently. Compare inventory carrying cost with recoverable selling proceeds if goods are damaged.

  3. Depreciate

    For equipment, identify its in-use date, residual value and remaining life. Recalculate if the evidence changes those estimates.

Worked case

Stock bought at two prices

A shop begins with no stock, buys 10 lamps at €30 and later 10 at €50. It sells 12 lamps for €70 each. There are no other costs or taxes.

Working

  1. Available cost is €800 for 20 lamps. FIFO assigns €300 + €100 = €400 to the sale and €400 to eight lamps remaining.
  2. Periodic weighted-average cost is €40 per lamp. Cost of sales is €480 and ending inventory €320.
  3. The same €840 sale produces gross profit of €440 under FIFO or €360 using the average. Each result reconciles total available cost.

Interpretation

If each of the eight unsold lamps can now realize only €35 net, compare €280 total net proceeds with the relevant carrying amount. FIFO would require a €120 write-down, the average method €40.

A common mistake

A new useful-life estimate is applied to the equipment’s original cost as if all past depreciation disappeared.

How to correct it

Start at the carrying amount on the reassessment date and subtract the revised residual. Allocate that remaining amount over the remaining service period.

Quick check
After one full year, what is the van’s carrying amount assuming no impairment?

The vehicle started at €12,000. Deduct one year’s €2,000 allocation. Its €2,000 residual value was used in the annual charge calculation.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A printing machine costs €26,000 installed, with €2,000 residual value and six years of useful life. After two complete years its remaining life is estimated at three years and its residual at €1,000.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Find carrying amount before the revision and the new annual straight-line expense.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Explain two facts that could support this revision and identify its immediate cash effect.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

Initial expense is (€26,000 − €2,000)/6 = €4,000 a year. Carrying amount after two years is €18,000. New expense is (€18,000 − €1,000)/3 = €5,666.67 a year.

Part B 3 marks

Higher measured usage or a changed replacement schedule may shorten life. Lower demonstrable resale prices can reduce the residual. Revising estimates itself causes no immediate cash flow.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A retailer expects to sell damaged chairs for more than their purchase cost. Could the inventory still require a write-down?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Yes. The expected proceeds after finishing and selling costs determine recoverability. Here €120 less €20 gives €100 against €110 cost. I would record the €10 gap for each affected chair, document the repair quote and evidence for the expected selling price, and keep the cash budget separate because the accounting write-down itself spends no money.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Suppose the shop already paid the repair bill last week and it is included in the €110 recorded cost. Should the model subtract €12 again?

Compare with the follow-up answer

If repair is complete and already included in recorded cost, there is no remaining €12 completion payment. With only €8 selling costs left, the €120 sale gives €112 of net realizable value. That exceeds €110, so the stated inputs no longer call for a write-down.
