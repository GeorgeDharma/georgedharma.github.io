Case 1 of 4 · 10 marks

Opening the ceramics studio

A new studio receives €8,000 owner capital and €4,000 bank borrowing in cash. It buys €3,000 of materials on credit, uses €1,200 on delivered orders sold for €3,900 on credit, collects €1,000 of those sales and pays its supplier €700. Ignore interest and tax.

Case 2 of 4 · 10 marks

From a wholesaler’s profit to its bank balance

All sales and purchases are on credit. Revenue is €160,000, cost of sales €95,000, cash staff costs €30,000 and depreciation €5,000. Receivables rise €7,000, inventory falls €3,000 and suppliers fall €4,000. Ignore write-offs and other items for the cash bridge.

Case 3 of 4 · 10 marks

Growing with equipment finance

A firm begins the year with equipment carried at €48,000, four years of remaining use and an €8,000 residual value. A bank loan starts at €30,000 with 8% annual interest. An instalment of €8,400 is paid at year-end. Revenue is €75,000, operating costs before depreciation are €40,000, and average equity is €50,000. Ignore tax and other activity.

Case 4 of 4 · 10 marks

Consolidating an overseas distributor

A wholly owned distributor buys 100 items from its parent at €12 each. The parent originally paid €8 each. The distributor sells 75 to independent customers at €20 each. A peer reports 57% gross margin and takes a €40 plant-closure expense below gross profit. Ignore taxes and exchange movements for this trading comparison.

Case 1 · 10 marks

Opening the ceramics studio

Part A 4 marks

Post the financing and purchase entries, and the two sides of the sale.

Debit cash €8,000 credit owner capital €8,000. Debit cash €4,000 credit debt €4,000. Debit inventory €3,000 credit suppliers €3,000. Debit receivables €3,900 credit sales €3,900. Debit cost €1,200 credit inventory €1,200. Award one mark for each group of financing, purchase, revenue and cost entries.

Part B 4 marks

Reconcile closing assets, liabilities and equity.

Cash €12,300, receivables €2,900 and remaining inventory €1,800 total €17,000. Borrowing €4,000 plus suppliers €2,300 gives €6,300 obligations. Owner capital €8,000 plus €2,700 profit gives €10,700 equity. Both sides total €17,000. Award for the asset, liability and equity calculations and their check.

Part C 2 marks

Which amount measures profit, and why did receiving the bank advance not change it?

Profit is €3,900 less €1,200 = €2,700. Borrowing creates an obligation equal to the cash received, with no revenue earned.

Case 2 · 10 marks

From a wholesaler’s profit to its bank balance

Part A 4 marks

Use the direct method to calculate cash from operations.

Cash receipts €153,000. Purchases €95,000 − €3,000 = €92,000. Paying suppliers €96,000 reflects the €4,000 reduction in outstanding bills. Direct operating cash = €153,000 − €96,000 − €30,000 = €27,000. Award one mark for each result.

Part B 3 marks

Independently reconcile profit to that cash figure.

Profit is €160,000 − €95,000 − €30,000 − €5,000 = €30,000. Add €5,000 depreciation, subtract €7,000 receivables, add €3,000 inventory reduction and subtract €4,000 payables reduction to reach €27,000. Give credit for profit and both directions of working-capital adjustments.

Part C 3 marks

Separately, closing gross invoices of €60,000 are now assessed at an average 3% loss rate, with €1,100 already allowed and no write-offs. Calculate the additional expense and cash effect.

Target allowance €1,800. Expense to increase it is €700. Updating the estimate alone has no cash effect. Net receivables from those invoices would be €58,200.

Case 3 · 10 marks

Growing with equipment finance

Part A 3 marks

Calculate annual depreciation, operating profit and end-of-year equipment carrying amount.

Depreciable balance €48,000 − €8,000 = €40,000, giving €10,000 a year. Operating profit is €75,000 − €40,000 − €10,000 = €25,000. Carrying amount is €38,000. Award one for each figure.

Part B 4 marks

Separate loan interest from principal and compute net income and return on equity.

Interest is €2,400. Principal paid is €8,400 − €2,400 = €6,000 and closing debt is €24,000. Net income €25,000 − €2,400 = €22,600. Return on average equity is €22,600/€50,000 = 45.2%. Award for interest, debt schedule, net income and ratio.

Part C 3 marks

Next year the remaining equipment life is reassessed to two years with a €6,000 residual. Find the new expense and distinguish it from the scheduled cash payment.

New annual depreciation is (€38,000 − €6,000)/2 = €16,000. Reassessment changes future expense allocation, while it creates no immediate payment and does not alter the bank’s contractual instalments.

Case 4 · 10 marks

Consolidating an overseas distributor

Part A 4 marks

Calculate the separate gross profits and the consolidated gross profit.

Parent records 100 × (€12 − €8) = €400. Distributor records 75 × (€20 − €12) = €600. Combined separate totals are €1,000, but 25 unsold items contain €100 internal profit. Consolidated gross profit €900, also €1,500 external sales less €600 outside cost. Award for both standalone amounts, elimination and total.

Part B 3 marks

Find consolidated closing inventory and margin. Should the peer’s closure cost be added to gross profit?

Closing inventory = 25 × €8 = €200. Gross margin = €900/€1,500 = 60%. A closure expense below gross profit cannot have lowered the reported 57% gross margin, so an add-back to that figure would be inconsistent.

Part C 3 marks

A separate unit earns 200 foreign units of sales at €0.60 per unit versus 180 at €0.70 last year. Quantify reported euro growth and growth at last year’s rate.

Euro sales fall from €126 to €120, a −€6/€126 = −4.76% change. At the old rate sales rise from €126 to €140, or 11.11%. Local growth was positive while changing conversion rates lowered reported euros.

  1. F01.11 · Why tax expense can exceed the current tax bill

    A retailer accrues a £24,000 warranty charge. Tax relief arises only on payment. Its accounting profit after the charge is £96,000. Tax is 20% and sufficient future taxable profits are probable. Find both tax components now and explain next year's settlement.

  2. F01.12 · When a warranty or lawsuit belongs on the balance sheet

    At its reporting date a builder has a probable present legal obligation estimated reliably at £80,000. A second, unrelated claim is merely possible, with £35,000 at stake. Management also plans £12,000 of voluntary staff retraining next year. Classify all three.

  3. F01.13 · Is a factory worth less than its book value?

    A different unit carries £12m of goodwill and £68m of eligible equipment. Use value is £69m and net sale value is £65m. Find the loss and closing balances. A year later recovery rises to £84m. Can goodwill be restored?

  4. F01.14 · Follow one loan through all three IFRS 9 impairment stages

    A lender has a £40,000 fixed-rate loan with two years remaining and a £400 opening allowance. Reliable new evidence establishes a significant increase in lifetime default risk, with no credit impairment yet. Normal/downturn weights are 75%/25%. Within each scenario, first defaults in the next year or the year after have respective probabilities 2%/4% and 6%/10%. Conditional cash shortfalls at maturity are £10,816 normal and £16,224 downturn. The original effective rate is 4%. Determine stage, allowance and entry. One year later the borrower is credit-impaired, gross is still £40,000, and the only expected final collection is £29,120 a year later.
