Incremental cash flows

Identify cash that changes because a project is accepted. Include opportunities forgone and displaced sales, and exclude sunk costs.

The starting point

Imagine running the same company twice, once accepting a project and once declining it. Incremental cash flow is the difference between those two futures on each date. A past consulting fee is sunk because it is already paid in both futures.

An opportunity cost is what the company gives up by using an existing asset. A new product can also displace profitable sales of an old product. Count the lost contribution after avoidable costs and count each effect only once.

Theory and method

Incremental cash-flow principle

Opportunity cost · Cannibalization · Sunk cost

A factory director says an empty building is free and a completed survey cost €30,000. Which amount belongs in the project decision?

An unused building can still have a buyer. Imagine running the company twice, accepting the project in one future and selling the building in the other. The sale proceeds disappear when the project occupies it. A completed, nonrefundable survey appears in the past of both futures, so accepting the proposal changes neither future survey bill. This thought experiment also catches benefits such as avoiding a contractual cancellation payment.

How the result is built

ΔCFₜ = CFₜ(accept) − CFₜ(decline)

CFₜ is the entire company's cash flow at date t in euros. ΔCFₜ is the change caused by acceptance. Both alternatives use the same date and tax assumptions.

  1. List cash receipts and payments in the world where the project is declined, including genuinely available alternate uses.
  2. Repeat for acceptance and take the difference date by date. Losing €7 of sales with €3 of avoidable costs removes €4 of contribution.
  3. Discount the dated differences at the appropriate risk-matched rate. Put the lost sale price at the date the alternate buyer would have paid.

When the reasoning holds

  • The alternative must be feasible. An advertised sale price from a buyer unable to close gives an unreliable opportunity cost.
  • Compare after-tax cash on consistent terms. Disposal tax can make forgone net proceeds smaller than the asset's headline price.

Terms in this lesson
Net present value (NPV)
Today’s value of incoming cash minus today’s value of outgoing cash.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Working capital
Money tied up in day-to-day operations. For a deal, agree which receivables, stock and payables count.
Discount rate
Rate used to express a future payment in today’s money, reflecting time and the relevant risk.
First example

A proposed product earns €80,000 before shared resources. It uses a room the firm could rent out for €12,000 and displaces €7,000 of other contribution. Its incremental annual cash benefit is €61,000. A €5,000 feasibility study paid last year never enters that comparison.

Compare two futures at each date

An incremental project forecast compares the company's cash if it accepts with its cash if it declines. Draw both futures when the distinction is unclear. If the warehouse will be sold when a proposal is rejected, its forgone sale proceeds belong in the proposal even if its book value is very small.

A sunk cost was incurred before the decision and cannot be recovered by declining. A future cancellation penalty is different. If it is paid only on rejection, acceptance avoids that future payment. The difference between the two futures identifies the correct sign.

Follow the cash consequences of a sale

A new product can divert customers from the old product. The loss is the old contribution after costs genuinely saved on units no longer sold. If the old fixed rent continues, it has not been saved. Similarly, allocating existing head-office salaries to a project changes reported profit without necessarily changing company cash.

Building inventory or allowing customers time to pay ties up cash before all reported profit is received. Initial operating working capital is an outflow. Its eventual release is a separate inflow only to the extent collection or sale is realistic.

Keep cash flows and financing on matching bases

For an operating project discounted at a rate for all sources of finance, use operating cash flows before debt interest. Otherwise the borrowing cost can be charged in the cash flows and again through the discount rate. Model incremental tax on operations where the case specifies tax.

At project closure, identify salvage proceeds, disposal taxes, shutdown costs and working-capital recovery at their actual dates. A profitable year near the end is not the same as receiving the assets' sale proceeds today.

Method

Assemble the incremental schedule

  1. Day zero

    Include new asset spending, incremental operating working capital and any cash forgone by committing an existing asset. Exclude unrecoverable past costs.

  2. Operations

    Use new contribution less displaced contribution and genuinely incremental overhead. Add any specified incremental tax effects.

  3. Closure

    Recover usable working capital, add resale proceeds and deduct removal costs. Preserve separate time columns before discounting.

Worked case

A bakery tests a one-season line

All amounts are thousands. New equipment costs 30 today, working capital requires 4 today, and a room could otherwise earn rental income of 5 at year end. A survey costing 3 was paid last month. One year later the new product generates cash contribution 48, displaces 8 of old contribution, and equipment sells for 6. Working capital is collected in full. Ignore tax.

Working

  1. The initial incremental outflow is 30 + 4 = 34. The survey is common to accepting and declining because it cannot be refunded.
  2. Year-end incremental operating cash is 48 − 8 − 5 = 35. Closure adds 6 + 4, bringing the year-end cash flow to 45.
  3. At a 10% required return NPV is −34 + 45/1.10 = 6.91. An allocated share of existing office rent should change this result only if the company pays a different amount under acceptance.

Interpretation

Including the lost rent and excluding the survey follow the same rule. One cost changes across futures, while the other has already been incurred under either future.

A common mistake

Deducting lost old sales revenue when the displaced units also save production costs.

How to correct it

Calculate lost contribution. Establish which variable expenses disappear and which leases and salaries continue after the shift.

Quick check
If the room would otherwise stand empty and has no other use, what is the revised annual incremental benefit?

The room now has zero opportunity cost. Subtract €7,000 of displaced contribution from €80,000. The study was paid for under either decision.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A project requires 45 of equipment and 5 of working capital today. Next year new sales generate 70 before additional cash costs of 10. Old contribution of 7 is lost. Equipment sells for 3 and 5 of working capital is recovered. A prior report cost 4. Ignore tax.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate the two incremental cash flows.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Find NPV at 10%.

  3. Part C · 3 marks

    A currently unused shed is required and could instead be sold today for 8. Reassess.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Today pay 45 + 5 = 50. Next year receive 70 − 10 − 7 + 3 + 5 = 61. The nonrefundable report does not change either future.

Part B 2 marks

NPV is −50 + 61/1.10 = 5.45. Each amount is dated before comparison.

Part C 3 marks

Forgoing the sale adds an opportunity cost of 8 today if there are no later offsetting proceeds from that shed. Revised NPV is about −2.55. The relevant amount is its available sale value, not its recorded cost.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A factory director says an empty building is free and a completed survey cost €30,000. Which amount belongs in the project decision?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would show the firm-wide difference. An executable €80,000 sale makes that building costly to occupy even if its accounting carrying amount is zero. The survey is already paid and cannot be recovered. I would also check whether accepting the proposal avoids costs or displaces other sales, and put each incremental amount in the year when cash actually changes.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if the survey contract contains a refundable €12,000 deposit recovered only when the project is abandoned?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Abandoning produces €12,000 that disappears under acceptance. That foregone refund becomes a project outflow at its expected refund date. The remainder already paid remains sunk if no decision can recover it.
