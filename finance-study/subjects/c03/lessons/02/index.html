Capital budgeting

Compare mutually exclusive projects by present value. Check the scale and timing when rankings by internal rate of return differ.

The starting point

Net present value (NPV) adds the present values of future incremental cash flows and subtracts the cash invested today. The discount rate represents what comparable risk could earn elsewhere. For mutually exclusive projects under the same capital availability, the larger positive NPV adds more value.

The internal rate of return (IRR) is the rate at which a project’s NPV becomes zero. It expresses a percentage and can rank a small, high-percentage investment above one that adds more euros. Different timing can also reverse rankings when the discount rate changes.

Theory and method

Net present value and capital rationing

Fisher separation theorem · Profitability index · Mutually exclusive investments

A board has €100m to invest in indivisible projects. Is sorting projects by NPV per euro enough to select the best program?

A factory can accept a profitable machine and still lose a chance to accept two better machines together. In perfect capital markets, separating investment from owners' desired consumption motivates accepting investments that enlarge present wealth. A hard internal budget changes the feasible set. For indivisible purchases, rank complete affordable combinations and compare their total value. Spending every available euro is useful only when the added projects increase value.

How the result is built

Choose the feasible portfolio with the largest Σ xⱼ NPVⱼ, subject to Σ xⱼ Iⱼ ≤ B and xⱼ ∈ {0,1}

j identifies a project. Iⱼ is initial outlay, NPVⱼ is its present value added, B is the budget, and xⱼ is one for acceptance or zero for rejection. All monetary inputs are in millions today.

  1. Compute each project's incremental NPV after opportunity costs and dependencies.
  2. Enumerate combinations or use an integer optimization when the list is large. Exclude portfolios above the initial budget.
  3. Compare total project NPV across feasible packages. The ratio ranking can miss high-value combinations when a remaining budget is too small for the next project.

When the reasoning holds

  • Add individual NPVs only when projects are independent. Shared equipment, competing demand or bundled discounts require joint cash flows.
  • The single-budget test assumes only today's cash is constrained. Later bottlenecks require additional date-specific constraints.
Terms in this lesson
Net present value (NPV)
Today’s value of incoming cash minus today’s value of outgoing cash.
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Discount rate
Rate used to express a future payment in today’s money, reflecting time and the relevant risk.
First example

Project A costs 10 and returns 14 after a year. Project B costs 100 and returns 125. At 10%, NPVs are 14/1.10 − 10 = 2.73 and 125/1.10 − 100 = 13.64. A has the higher IRR (40% versus 25%), but B creates more value if either can be funded.

Build the investment rule from a financing alternative

If a risk-matched investment earns 10%, one unit today can become 1.10 next year. Reversing that trade means one unit due next year is worth 1/1.10 today. Net present value adds all dated cash flows after putting them into today's units.

For independent projects with adequate resources, each positive-NPV project adds estimated value. For mutually exclusive alternatives the company can take only one, so compare total NPVs on the same assumptions. A separate budget constraint can require choosing a feasible combination of projects.

Interpret the internal rate carefully

The internal rate of return sets a project's NPV to zero. For a conventional outflow followed solely by positive inflows, a higher discount rate lowers NPV. In this setting an IRR above the required return supports acceptance when the project is considered on its own.

Mutually exclusive projects can differ in scale or timing. A small project can generate a high percentage on little capital. A large project can add more currency value at a lower percentage. When signs change repeatedly the NPV equation may even have multiple roots, making a single IRR rule unreliable.

Find a crossover through incremental cash

Subtract one project's cash-flow schedule from the other's to measure precisely what the larger commitment buys. Discount the difference at the required return. A positive incremental NPV means selecting the second project adds value compared with selecting the first.

The crossover rate makes those incremental flows worth zero. It describes a ranking boundary, not necessarily either project's own IRR. A meaningful comparison also requires consistent project lives or an explicit assumption about replacement if equipment will be renewed.

Method

Choose between mutually exclusive designs

  1. List the alternatives

    Include doing nothing where feasible. Date all cash flows and use the risk-appropriate return for each compatible stream.

  2. Measure value

    Calculate each NPV and the NPV of the incremental choice. State which schedule is being subtracted from which.

  3. Challenge the ranking

    Compare scale, timing, financing limits and lives. If a crossover is relevant, solve the incremental zero-NPV equation.

Worked case

Two bottling lines

A small line costs 50 and pays 66 in one year. A large line costs 100 and pays 123 in one year. The company can fund either, has one location and requires 8%. There are no later flows.

Working

  1. The small line's NPV is −50 + 66/1.08 = 11.11 and its IRR is 32%. The large line's NPV is −100 + 123/1.08 = 13.89 and its IRR is 23%.
  2. Switching to the large line costs 50 more today and pays 57 more next year. Incremental NPV is −50 + 57/1.08 = 2.78.
  3. The incremental return is 57/50 − 1 = 14%. Below 14% the extra capacity is worth paying for. Above it, the small line ranks higher, assuming its own NPV remains positive.

Interpretation

The result depends on the ability to invest the additional 50 and on projected use of the larger line. A demand stress that reduces its incremental inflow can reverse the choice.

A common mistake

Ranking projects by the number of years until initial spending is recovered.

How to correct it

Payback omits flows after its cut-off and often ignores discounting. Show whether later receipts change the economic ranking.

Quick check
At a 20% discount rate, what NPV does project B now offer?

125/1.20 − 100 = 4.17. Subtracting the discount rate from a one-year cash return without first discounting does not give present value.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

Project X costs 40 and pays 54 next year. Project Y costs 90 and pays 111 next year. They serve the same demand and cannot both be accepted. Required return is 10%.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate each NPV and select a project.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Calculate the crossover rate and interpret it.

  3. Part C · 2 marks

    What if there is a hard budget of 60 and the projects cannot be scaled?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

X gives −40 + 54/1.10 = 9.09. Y gives −90 + 111/1.10 = 10.91. Select Y if the firm can fund it and these cash-flow forecasts hold.

Part B 3 marks

Switching from X to Y requires 50 now and returns 57 next year. Crossover is 57/50 − 1 = 14%. At 14% their NPVs coincide.

Part C 2 marks

Y is infeasible. X is feasible and has positive NPV at 10%. The constrained decision can differ from the unconstrained ranking.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A board has €100m to invest in indivisible projects. Is sorting projects by NPV per euro enough to select the best program?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would compare the packages. Project A looks best per euro, but it prevents either remaining project from fitting into the budget. Two smaller independent projects add €28m together against €18m from A. The board should also investigate whether the hard budget is genuine. If outside finance is available on the same risk-adjusted terms, positive-NPV projects might all be worth funding.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if B and C share customers and together lose €12m of present value?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The combination adds €28m − €12m = €16m. A alone adds €18m, now the largest feasible package of the stated projects. Any proposal to fund another pairing must identify room in the budget.
