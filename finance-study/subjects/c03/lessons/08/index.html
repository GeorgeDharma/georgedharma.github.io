Payout policy

Compare dividends, repurchases and retained cash. Ask how taxes, investment opportunities, signals and management incentives alter a frictionless payout result.

The starting point

A dividend distributes cash to all shareholders. A repurchase buys shares from those who sell and reduces the number outstanding. Both reduce the cash left in the company. A larger earnings-per-share figure after repurchase does not by itself create extra operating earnings.

In a frictionless market with unchanged investment decisions, fair pricing and no taxes or fees, payout form alone does not change total shareholder wealth. Taxes, information, mispricing or a need to preserve cash for valuable projects can change the practical decision.

Theory and method

Miller–Modigliani dividend irrelevance

Homemade dividends · Dividend versus repurchase · Payout substitution

How can a shareholder fund spending when the firm keeps all cash, and when does that equivalence fail?

A shareholder planning a €1,000 purchase can take a corporate dividend or sell part of a holding. In a frictionless market with unchanged investment and fair pricing, both choices convert the same wealth into cash. If a dividend forces the firm to issue underpriced securities or abandon an investment, the counterfactual has changed. Likewise, selling personal shares may incur taxes, fees or ownership restrictions that alter how much usable cash the investor receives.

How the result is built

W = nPₑₓ + nd ; Pₑₓ = P₀ − d in the frictionless benchmark

W is wealth after a dividend, n shares owned before the distribution, P₀ the pre-dividend price, d the dividend per share and Pₑₓ the ex-dividend price. All prices and cash are measured per share at equivalent dates.

  1. A firm with 100 shares worth €20 owns €2,000 of total equity. Distributing €2 per share sends €200 out of the firm.
  2. With no change in investment or information, post-dividend equity is €1,800 or €18 per share. Ten shares carry €180 in equity and €20 cash.
  3. If the firm keeps cash, the holder can sell one of ten shares at €20 and retain nine worth €180. Both packages provide the same spending cash and residual wealth.

When the reasoning holds

  • Investment policy and fair security prices are held fixed. Foregoing a positive-NPV project changes total shareholder wealth through operating decisions.
  • Identical tax treatment, divisible shares and low transaction costs make homemade dividends feasible. Taxes or an inability to sell can make payout matter to particular holders.
Terms in this lesson
Net present value (NPV)
Today’s value of incoming cash minus today’s value of outgoing cash.
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
Benchmark
A reference chosen in advance for comparison, such as a market index or an equal-weight portfolio.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
How to read the notation
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.

All notation examples

First example

A company is worth €100m including excess cash and has 10m shares at €10. It uses €20m to buy 2m shares at €10. The remaining €80m divided by 8m shares is still €10 a share. A €2 dividend instead leaves a share worth €8 plus €2 cash.

Follow the cash through a distribution

Before a dividend a share includes a claim on the cash about to leave. After distribution the company owns less cash and the shareholder holds a separate cash payment. In a frictionless benchmark, all else equal, the ex-dividend share price falls by the dividend per share.

A share repurchase leaves fewer shares outstanding. At the fair pre-buyback price, the total value and share count fall proportionally, so the remaining price per share is unchanged under the same assumptions. Investors who sell receive cash in exchange for surrendering a claim.

Compare payout with keeping cash

A shareholder wanting cash can sell a few shares in a frictionless market, and one wanting to reinvest dividends can buy shares. This supports an irrelevance benchmark when investment policy stays fixed, markets work without costs and payout forms are taxed alike.

If retaining cash finances a positive-NPV project which would otherwise be lost, the investment decision changes the comparison. Conversely, managers could spend excess cash on low-value projects. Assess the realistic alternative use of cash, likely external financing costs and liquidity needs.

Understand transfer and signalling effects

Buying shares above intrinsic value can transfer wealth from remaining holders to sellers, even if the company reports higher earnings per share. Buying below intrinsic value can favour continuing holders. Such statements require an intrinsic-value assumption that may be hard to observe.

A regular dividend can create expectations about future payments. A repurchase program can offer more flexibility, though its signal depends on actual execution and context. Tax rules also vary across investors and jurisdictions. State the specific tax assumptions when comparing net cash.

Method

Reconcile value per original owner

  1. Before payout

    Find equity value including excess cash, share count and value per share. State which operating earnings and cash returns might change.

  2. Dividend or buyback

    For a dividend subtract the cash and calculate the new price. For a buyback calculate shares actually retired at the price paid.

  3. Total wealth

    For a particular ownership group, add the cash received and the value of remaining holdings. A per-share metric by itself omits sellers.

Worked case

A premium buyback

A firm has equity intrinsically worth 100, including 20 of excess cash, and 10 shares outstanding. It spends 20 buying shares at 12.50 each, although the assumed fair pre-buyback value is 10. Ignore taxes, fees and changes to operations.

Working

  1. The firm buys 20/12.50 = 1.6 shares. It retains equity worth 80 spread over 8.4 shares.
  2. Each continuing share is now worth 80/8.4 = 9.5238. Selling holders receive 20 for claims initially worth 1.6 × 10 = 16.
  3. The transfer to sellers is 4. Continuing holders' aggregate value falls from 8.4 × 10 = 84 to 80, matching that gain. Underlying operating value is unchanged.

Interpretation

The premium assumption concerns intrinsic value. A company announcement alone does not prove its stock is expensive or inexpensive.

A common mistake

Using a rising earnings-per-share figure as the entire argument that a repurchase created wealth.

How to correct it

Track absolute equity value, the repurchase price and shares retired. Also account for interest income given up on the distributed cash.

Quick check
If the same firm distributes €10m as a dividend on its 10m shares, what is the theoretical ex-dividend share price under these assumptions?

Each share receives €1. With operations and investment plans unchanged, its value falls from €10 to €9 when the cash leaves the company.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

A company is worth 240 including 60 excess cash and has 20 shares. It distributes 48. Assume fair pricing, no taxes and unchanged investment and earnings.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Compare a cash dividend with a market-price buyback.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    An investor owns two shares and sells none in the buyback. Compare total wealth after each action.

  3. Part C · 2 marks

    If investment policy changes because management abandons a valuable project, does this comparison suffice?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

A dividend of 48/20 = 2.40 per share leaves a price of (240 − 48)/20 = 9.60. A fair buyback at 12 retires 4 shares, leaving 192/16 = 12 per share.

Part B 2 marks

Dividend gives 2 × 9.60 + 2 × 2.40 = 24. Keeping two post-buyback shares gives 2 × 12 = 24. Ownership percentage rises in the buyback but the firm holds less cash.

Part C 2 marks

No. Incorporate the lost project's NPV and feasible alternative funding. The fixed-investment benchmark then no longer describes the choice.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How can a shareholder fund spending when the firm keeps all cash, and when does that equivalence fail?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would compare cash plus the remaining investment. With ten €20 shares, I can receive a €2 dividend per share and retain ten €18 shares, or sell one €20 share and retain nine. Each leaves €180 invested and €20 to spend. I would check tax, dealing costs and whether the payout changes investment or sends new information before applying that result in practice.

Interviewer’s follow-up

A founder must keep at least ten shares to retain a voting right. How does that change the comparison?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Selling one of ten shares sacrifices control rights with a potentially material value. The market-price wealth arithmetic still holds for a passive holder, but it omits the founder's private voting benefit. A cash distribution could meet the spending need while preserving that right.
