Financing irrelevance

In the Modigliani–Miller benchmark, investors can reproduce leverage on their own. Financing can redistribute risk even when it leaves total firm value unchanged.

The starting point

Imagine selling claims on a business by giving a lender first claim and shareholders the remaining cash. In the Modigliani–Miller benchmark, changing these claims cannot change the value produced by the underlying assets. The assumptions include no taxes, distress costs or financing frictions and equal borrowing opportunities.

Investors can recreate leverage by borrowing personally to buy part of an unlevered business. As the proportion financed by debt rises, a given fluctuation in business value has a larger percentage effect on the remaining equity. A higher expected equity return can simply compensate for greater risk.

Theory and method

Modigliani–Miller propositions I and II

Capital structure irrelevance · Homemade leverage · MM with corporate taxes

State both Modigliani–Miller propositions, explain why borrowing more can raise equity return without creating value, and identify the tax exception.

Two warehouses collect the same rent in every state. One divides its rent between bondholders and shareholders. The other gives all of it to shareholders. Buying all claims on either warehouse gives the same business cash. If the packages cost different amounts, investors able to trade on equal terms can buy one and sell the equivalent expensive one. Shareholders in the indebted warehouse have a smaller residual base, so the same operating shock moves their percentage returns more.

How the result is built

Vᴸ = Vᵁ ; kᴱ = kᵁ + (D/E)(kᵁ−kᴰ) ; with permanent eligible debt, Vᴸ = Vᵁ + TᴄD

Vᴸ and Vᵁ are levered and unlevered asset values. kᴱ, kᵁ and kᴰ are required equity, operating and debt returns. D and E are market debt and equity. Tᴄ is the corporate tax rate. The first two expressions use the tax-free benchmark.

  1. Proposition I follows by replicating the entire operating payoff. Varying the financing split does not change the sum of asset cash available to investors in the benchmark.
  2. Write the operating return as the market-value-weighted return on debt and equity. Rearrangement yields Proposition II. At kᵁ = 10%, kᴰ = 4% and D/E = 1, shareholders require 16%.
  3. Under corporate taxes, permanent fixed debt can create an annual shield Tᴄ × kᴰ × D. Capitalizing at kᴰ gives TᴄD under the specific perpetuity assumptions. In that textbook tax case the equity premium term is multiplied by (1−Tᴄ).

When the reasoning holds

  • The tax-free propositions assume identical operating decisions, frictionless trading, similar borrowing access and no corporate tax or distress costs. If debt changes operations, the replicated cash differs.
  • TᴄD requires permanent fixed nominal debt, a fully usable deduction, constant tax rate and the stated discount treatment. Amortization, tax losses or uncertain leverage require valuing dated shields.

Terms in this lesson
Benchmark
A reference chosen in advance for comparison, such as a market index or an equal-weight portfolio.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
First example

Assume identical operating assets worth €100m. An all-equity firm gives shareholders €100m of value. If a twin owes debt worth €40m, its equity is worth €60m in the benchmark. A €10m fall in asset value cuts the twin’s equity to €50m if debt value stays fixed.

Start with the assets before dividing the claims

Imagine identical operating companies generating the same cash in every possible state. One issues only shares. The other promises part of the cash to debt investors first and leaves the remainder for shareholders. The Modigliani–Miller benchmark asks whether this rearrangement alone can change the total market value of the operating assets.

Assume no taxes, bankruptcy or transaction costs, identical information, access to comparable borrowing, and operating decisions that do not change with financing. Buying all the debt and equity in the levered firm then reproduces the unlevered firm's whole operating cash flow. Equal payoffs imply equal total value.

Show homemade leverage explicitly

An individual can mimic a fraction of levered equity by buying the same fraction of unlevered assets and borrowing the corresponding fraction of corporate debt. Subtract the individual's promised repayment in every state. With the model's common borrowing terms this reproduces the company's equity payoff.

If the two packages have different market costs despite identical payoffs, the cheaper one can be bought against the more expensive one under the model's trading assumptions. This replication argument explains irrelevance. It does not claim that financing never matters in an actual company.

Explain why equity becomes riskier

Debt has a prior claim. When the same change in operating value passes to a smaller starting equity base, its percentage gain or loss grows. In the simple tax-free benchmark, required equity return equals the unlevered required return plus debt-to-equity times the difference between operating and debt required returns.

Using more lower-cost debt while holding the equity cost fixed misses the increase in residual risk. The opportunity cost of the operating assets remains the weighted average under the benchmark. Distress costs, interest deductions, borrowing constraints or changed project selection can break the equivalence.

Method

Replicate a fractional equity position

  1. Match assets

    Start with the desired fraction of the unlevered operating firm. Check its payoffs in at least two business states.

  2. Match prior debt

    Borrow the same fraction of the levered company's debt at the same terms. Subtract its repayment from the asset payoffs.

  3. Compare prices

    Compare net initial capital with the price of the corresponding equity fraction. Explain what real-world friction might prevent the trades.

Worked case

An investor copies a levered shareholding

An unlevered business is priced at 120. Its assets pay 150 in a good state or 108 in a bad state next year. An identical business owes safe debt worth 40 today that repays 42 next year. Its total value is 120 in the benchmark.

Working

  1. Ten percent of the levered company's equity costs 0.10 × (120 − 40) = 8. It pays 0.10 × (150 − 42) = 10.8 or 0.10 × (108 − 42) = 6.6.
  2. Alternatively buy 10% of the unlevered business for 12 and borrow 4 personally. The initial equity investment is 8, and the debt payment is 4.2.
  3. The position pays 15 − 4.2 = 10.8 or 10.8 − 4.2 = 6.6. Matching both states explains why a price gap between the two equity positions would invite substitution.

Interpretation

If the individual could borrow only at a much higher rate, the homemade position would no longer deliver identical proceeds.

A common mistake

Interpreting a higher expected return on levered equity as free value creation.

How to correct it

Compare the state exposure of the residual shareholding and the total value of all claimants. Additional equity return can compensate for greater risk.

Quick check
Under those assumptions, what percentage of the twin’s original equity value does that €10m loss represent?

Divide the €10m asset loss passed to shareholders by their initial €60m equity. Debt changes how the business risk is shared.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

Identical operating assets are worth 200. An unlevered firm requires 9% on those assets. Its twin has market debt of 80 costing 4%. Apply the tax-free benchmark.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find the twin's equity value and required equity return.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Show that the overall required return is unchanged.

  3. Part C · 2 marks

    Name an assumption that could fail for an owner of a small private firm and explain the effect.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Equity is 200 − 80 = 120. Required equity return is 9% + (80/120) × (9% − 4%) = 12.33%.

Part B 2 marks

Using market weights, 0.60 × 12.333% + 0.40 × 4% = 9%. The low debt cost and higher equity cost jointly preserve the operating return.

Part C 2 marks

A household may face personal borrowing limits or a higher borrowing rate than the firm. It could then struggle to recreate corporate leverage with identical cost and state payoffs.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

State both Modigliani–Miller propositions, explain why borrowing more can raise equity return without creating value, and identify the tax exception.

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Proposition I says two otherwise identical businesses have the same total value under the frictionless, tax-free assumptions. Proposition II says the increased debt claim makes the remaining equity riskier, so its required return rises with debt relative to equity. A tax deduction can change total cash left for investors. Under permanent eligible debt of €40m and a 25% corporate tax rate, the special-case shield value is €10m.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What happens to the €10m shield calculation if €40m is repaid next year, interest is 5% and the single deduction is certain?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Interest is €2m and the one-time tax saving is €0.5m. At a 5% shield discount rate its present value is €0.476m. The perpetual-debt assumption that produced €10m is absent.
