Finding the economic age for replacing equipment

Select a repeatable replacement age using incremental maintenance and age-dependent resale values.

Theory and terminology

Equivalent annual cost and the replacement chain

EAC · optimal replacement interval

What you should be able to explain

  • Select a repeatable replacement age using incremental maintenance and age-dependent resale values.
  • Decide whether to keep an installed machine another year using its foregone sale price and marginal cost.

The option you are buying is a cycle

A business can replace an identical machine every year, every second year, or at another chosen age. Each policy delivers the same recurring service but requires purchases at different dates. A three-year cycle can have a higher total present cost simply because it delivers more years. The company needs a common service period or equivalent annual cost.

For each candidate age, start a fresh cycle with a new purchase. Include all maintenance through sale and the amount received at that chosen age. Annualize using that cycle's own duration. When identical cycles can repeat indefinitely under unchanged real or nominal assumptions, the lowest equivalent annual cost gives the lowest long-run cost.

The machine already installed has a market price

The original price paid for the existing machine is sunk. The value it could fetch today is available to the replacement decision. Keeping it means forgoing that sale, bearing the cost of capital on the money tied up, paying next year's repairs, and receiving whatever sale value remains next year.

For one additional year, express all terms at its end. Grow today's possible sale price by one year of interest. Add next year's maintenance and subtract next year's possible sale proceeds. Compare this one-year cost with the optimized replacement policy's annual cost at the same date.

Check what can actually repeat

A fleet that must operate continuously can often replace identical assets many times. If production ends in three years, environmental rules change the next model's economics, or the replacement will offer more capacity, use the actual horizon and the incremental services. A constant annual benchmark would conceal those differences.

A single marginal comparison supports an immediate one-year decision in the exercise, where later age-specific costs rise. If a costly overhaul creates several unusually cheap years, compare all feasible remaining holding intervals to the switching policy. A particularly expensive next year alone need not justify retirement.

Method

Build the result

PVₙ = P + Σₜ Mₜ/(1+r)ᵗ − Sₙ/(1+r)ⁿ; EACₙ = PVₙ / Σₜ 1/(1+r)ᵗ.

P is the new price now, Mₜ is maintenance at the end of year t, Sₙ is resale at chosen age n, and r is the per-year discount rate. Both sums run from one through n. EAC is an equivalent cost per service year in the same money.

  1. For each chosen age, discount costs and disposal proceeds to the purchase date.
  2. Divide that cycle's present cost by its own annuity factor to obtain an equivalent year-end cost. Select the lowest feasible cycle.
  3. At an existing age a, compare a further year's marginal cost Mₐ₊₁ + (1+r)Sₐ − Sₐ₊₁ with the lower-cost challenger service. Investigate longer hold periods if the future marginal costs are irregular.
Worked case

When to sell a delivery sorter

All money is € thousands. A new sorter costs 100. Year-end maintenance at ages one through four is 5, 12, 24 and 45. Respective sale values are 72, 55, 36 and 20. Replacement sorters repeat this pattern, services are identical, the annual discount rate is 10% and taxes are ignored. An installed unit is exactly two years old.

Working

  1. A one-year policy costs 100 + (5 − 72)/1.10 = 39.09 today. Divide by 0.90909 to get EAC of 43.00.
  2. Two-year PV is 100 + 5/1.10 + (12 − 55)/1.10² = 69.01. Dividing by the two-year annuity factor 1.73554 gives 39.76 per year.
  3. For three years the PV is 105.45 and the factor 2.48685, giving 42.40. For four years PV is 149.57 and the factor 3.16987, giving 47.18. The two-year policy is cheapest.
  4. Keeping the installed two-year-old for year three costs 24 + 1.10 × 55 − 36 = 48.50 at year end. This exceeds the challenger's 39.76. The following year would cost 45 + 1.10 × 36 − 20 = 64.60, so extending both years does not reverse the decision.

Decision and interpretation

Sell at age two and restart the two-year cycle under the stated conditions. Before issuing a purchase order, check whether an actual bid supports the €55k sale price and whether a replacement is available without lost output.

Quick check
A machine could sell for €20k today or €14k in a year. Next year's repairs cost €3k and the annual rate is 10%. What is the end-of-year marginal cost of keeping it?

The foregone €20k would grow to €22k. Add €3k of repairs and deduct €14k of resale, giving €11k.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

A pump costs €60k new. Maintenance by year is €4k, €10k and €23k. Sale prices at ages one, two and three are €42k, €31k and €20k. Identical pumps can be replaced forever at 10%, with equal output and no taxes. Choose among the three replacement ages and decide what to do with a one-year-old pump.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked solution
  1. The one-year present cost is 60 + (4 − 42)/1.10 = €25.45k, equivalent to €28.00k per year.
  2. Two-year PV is 60 + 4/1.10 + (10 − 31)/1.10² = €46.28k, equivalent to €26.67k a year. Three-year PV is €74.15k, equivalent to €29.82k a year.
  3. Choose the two-year cycle. Keeping the one-year-old for year two costs 10 + 1.10 × 42 − 31 = €25.20k, below €26.67k, so keep it for that year.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A machine still works. When is retiring it earlier cheaper than operating it for another year?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would compare replacement ages on the cost of delivering one year of service. When identical cycles can repeat, I discount the purchase, maintenance and sale proceeds of each cycle and annualize its cost. For an installed machine I use what we could sell it for today, the next repairs and the resale value after another year. The original purchase price is sunk.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if a sale of the business will end demand for the pump exactly one year from now?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Price the feasible one-year options directly. Keeping costs €25.20k at the year's end, while buying new for a one-year service interval costs €28.00k on that date. Keep the existing pump. There is no continuing service need to support an indefinite replacement cycle.
