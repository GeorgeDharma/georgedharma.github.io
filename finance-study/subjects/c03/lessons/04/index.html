Weighted capital costs

Weight debt and equity costs by market values and match the discount rate to the cash flow. A company-wide rate can misprice an unusually risky project.

The starting point

The return required by all funders depends on how much of a business is financed by lenders and how much by shareholders. Use current market values for the weights. When interest deductions are usable, include the tax effect in the debt component of the weighted average cost of capital (WACC).

Use WACC to discount cash from operations available to all funders before debt payments. A company-wide WACC fits projects with comparable risk and target financing. A riskier new business should be valued using assumptions for that business.

Theory and method

Weighted average cost of capital

Market-value weights · Target leverage · Free cash flow to the firm

Why would using a conglomerate's WACC systematically favour risky projects and reject attractive safe ones?

A transport group operates a contracted city route and a speculative tourism venture. Pricing both with one blended hurdle discounts each cash flow as though it shared the group's average risk. The average rate can overvalue the high-risk venture and undervalue the contracted route. A rate suited to all funders of the project has to reflect its operating risk, the market values of its financing sources and the actual value of interest deductions.

How the result is built

WACC = [E/(D+E)]kᴱ + [D/(D+E)]kᴰ(1−T)

D and E are market values of debt and equity, kᴰ and kᴱ their annual marginal required returns, and T the usable marginal corporate tax rate. WACC is the annual return for appropriately matched operating cash.

  1. Find market or credible target financing weights. A €60m equity claim and €40m debt imply weights of 60% and 40%.
  2. Apply a 25% usable deduction to 5% debt, giving 3.75%. With 10% equity the blended rate is 7.5%.
  3. Compare the project's risk with the rate. A certain-equivalent discount or comparable operating division can reveal misranking that a group average obscures.

When the reasoning holds

  • The standard constant WACC assumes an approximately stable target capital structure. A predetermined rapidly shrinking debt balance is often easier to value through APV.
  • Forecast cash must be after operating taxes and before payments to debt holders. Excluding interest in the numerator avoids charging financing twice.

Terms in this lesson
Weighted average cost of capital (WACC)
A blend of the required returns on debt and equity, weighted by how much each supplies. It can be used to discount cash flows available to both.
Net present value (NPV)
Today’s value of incoming cash minus today’s value of outgoing cash.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Discount rate
Rate used to express a future payment in today’s money, reflecting time and the relevant risk.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
First example

Suppose the market values of equity and debt are 60 and 40. Equity requires 10%, debt 5%, and usable interest deductions save 25% tax. WACC = 0.60 × 10% + 0.40 × 5% × 0.75 = 7.5%.

Identify the cash flow being discounted

Free cash flow to the firm represents operating cash after reinvestment but before interest or principal paid to creditors. Both shareholders and lenders have claims on it. A weighted required return reflects the cost of financing that same operating activity across those claimants.

Equity cash flow comes after net borrowing and debt service, so the shareholders' return is the relevant starting rate. Charging interest in a firm cash-flow forecast and then discounting at a rate that already includes the debt contribution mixes financing methods.

Why the weights use value today

Market-value weights represent what capital suppliers currently have invested at today's prices. Book values measure historical accounting amounts and can be far from current market values. A long-run target financing mix may be more appropriate than a temporary balance-sheet position if the model assumes it will be maintained.

The familiar after-tax formula is equity weight times cost of equity plus debt weight times pretax borrowing cost times one minus the marginal tax rate. It assumes interest deductions will be usable within the valuation framework. Tax losses or debt limits may change that assumption.

Recognize project and policy boundaries

WACC from an established retail business can misprice a risky mining project. A project should reflect its own operating exposure, currency and financing policy. Comparable pure-play firms can help estimate a more suitable rate, with adjustment for financing differences.

If leverage falls sharply after a buyout, a single target WACC may poorly describe future years. A changing discount-rate schedule or adjusted present value can make the debt path explicit. Recompute the cost of equity as well as the weights when leverage changes materially.

Method

Calculate and challenge WACC

  1. Weights

    Value debt and equity at their stated market values, add them, and divide each by the total. Verify they add to one.

  2. Costs

    Use current marginal inputs and the applicable tax-shield assumption. Keep costs and weights in the same currency and period.

  3. Suitability

    Identify the claimant on the forecast and compare project risk and target financing with the WACC assumptions.

Worked case

An established division values expansion

The division's market equity is 180 and debt is 120. Equity costs 11%, debt costs 6% and the tax rate is 25%, with deductions usable. An expansion costs 95 and pays a single after-tax operating cash flow of 104 in one year. Its risk and target financing match the division.

Working

  1. Total capital is 300, so equity and debt weights are 60% and 40%. After-tax debt cost is 6% × 0.75 = 4.5%.
  2. WACC is 0.60 × 11% + 0.40 × 4.5% = 8.4%. The project operating cash flow is discounted before any separate interest deduction in the cash forecast.
  3. NPV is 104/1.084 − 95 = 0.94. At a genuinely project-specific 10% required return NPV would be 104/1.10 − 95 = −0.45, reversing the decision.

Interpretation

The thin margin makes the risk match consequential. A board paper should show what changes in operations might justify the higher rate or lower the expected cash flow.

A common mistake

Using book equity when the problem provides both a traded share price and share count.

How to correct it

Compute market equity as price times shares outstanding. Use book value only with an explicit justification as a proxy.

Quick check
If interest deductions have zero value here and all other inputs stay unchanged, what would WACC be?

The after-tax debt cost returns to the full 5%. Weighting 10% by 60% and 5% by 40% gives 8%.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

A company has 10m shares priced at 12 and debt with market value 80m. Equity requires 12%, pretax debt costs 5%, and usable deductions save 20% tax. Cash flows represent operating cash available to all capital providers.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate WACC.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    If no interest deduction can ever be used, what rate follows with other inputs fixed?

  3. Part C · 2 marks

    Can the 8.8% automatically be used for a new equity-only venture in a different industry?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Equity value is 120m, so weights are 120/200 = 60% and 40%. WACC equals 0.6 × 12% + 0.4 × 5% × 0.8 = 8.8%.

Part B 2 marks

The debt input becomes 5%. WACC under this sensitivity is 0.6 × 12% + 0.4 × 5% = 9.2%.

Part C 2 marks

No. Its operating risk and financing policy differ. Estimate an appropriate project cost based on comparable business risk and the venture's intended financing before discounting.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why would using a conglomerate's WACC systematically favour risky projects and reject attractive safe ones?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would compare the operating exposure of each project with the assets used to estimate the group WACC. Here a 7.5% hurdle would decline a contracted project worth €1.90m and accept a tourism project that destroys €1.80m at its own hurdle. I would estimate a pure-play rate from comparable operations and document the target financing policy before sending either project to the investment committee.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Do you cut WACC immediately because the company borrowed after the balance-sheet date?

Compare with the follow-up answer

A transitory debt increase need not change target leverage. I would examine how the debt will be repaid, its effect on equity risk and whether the project shares that policy. Simply increasing the cheap-debt weight while freezing equity return is inconsistent.
