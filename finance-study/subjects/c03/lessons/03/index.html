Cost of equity and debt

Investors require compensation for time and risk. Estimate the cost of new debt and distinguish it from an old loan’s stated coupon.

The starting point

A company’s financing cost is what investors require for providing funds on current terms. A bond’s coupon determines its contracted interest payment. Its price adjusts as market rates and perceived credit risk change, so the coupon rate on old debt can differ from the cost of issuing new debt.

For equity, the capital asset pricing model connects the required return to the risk-free rate plus beta times the market risk premium. Beta measures sensitivity to broad market returns. Treat estimates as assumptions and check whether comparison firms carry similar operating and financing risks.

Theory and method

Capital asset pricing model and debt yield

Sharpe–Lintner CAPM · Yield to maturity · Credit spread

Can a firm use its 7% bond coupon as its project hurdle rate when its equity beta is 1.3?

A lender has a prior contractual claim while a shareholder waits for the residual. A bond coupon fixes a promised interest amount on face value. Investors buying it at a different price earn a different promised yield. A shareholder can diversify many company-specific surprises, but remains exposed to broad market movements. In the capital asset pricing model that market sensitivity determines the expected equity premium. A project can have different risk from either financing claim.

How the result is built

kᴱ = r𝑓 + βᴱ[E(Rₘ) − r𝑓] ; Pᴰ = Σₜ CFᴰₜ/(1+y)ᵗ

kᴱ is required equity return, r𝑓 the risk-free rate, βᴱ equity market beta, E(Rₘ) expected market return. Pᴰ is bond price, CFᴰₜ promised debt cash at date t, y the periodic yield and Σₜ the sum over dates.

  1. Estimate the market premium relative to the matching risk-free rate. With 3% risk-free and 8% expected market return the premium is 5%.
  2. At beta 1.3, compensation for equity's market exposure is 6.5 percentage points, giving 9.5% required equity return.
  3. Price promised debt receipts at current market terms. A one-year payment of 107 on a price of 99 implies 8.08% promised yield. A risky bond's expected realized return also depends on recovery.

When the reasoning holds

  • CAPM uses an equilibrium benchmark with diversified investors. An estimate from a few volatile returns may give an unreliable beta.
  • Bond yield assumes the contractual payments in the calculation. Default probability and illiquidity can make a quoted spread a poor estimate of pure market-risk compensation.

Terms in this lesson
Capital asset pricing model (CAPM)
A model that relates an asset’s expected return above the risk-free rate to its exposure to the wider market. That exposure is called beta.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Coupon
The interest payment specified in a bond’s terms.
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Asset pricing
Studying how risk, time and market conditions relate to the prices and expected returns of investments.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
First example

An old loan pays 3%, but lenders now quote 6% for a comparable new loan. Use 6% as the current pretax borrowing input under these terms. If the risk-free rate is 3%, beta 1.2 and the market premium 5%, the model cost of equity is 3% + 1.2 × 5% = 9%.

Separate coupon, promised yield and expected return

A coupon is the contract payment calculated on debt principal. A market yield uses the price investors pay today for the promised payments. A borrower with an old 4% coupon can therefore face a 6% market cost when it needs new finance.

If repayment is uncertain, promised yield can exceed the expected return lenders receive after defaults and recoveries. A quoted marginal borrowing rate is a practical input under stated conditions. In a high-default setting be clear whether your model discounts expected cash flows or contractual promises.

Estimate how shareholders price market exposure

The capital asset pricing model links required equity return to the risk-free rate plus beta times the expected market risk premium. Beta measures how the security's returns move with the broad market. A beta of two describes systematic sensitivity, not a promise to earn twice the market return each year.

The market premium is already the market return above the risk-free rate. If a question gives the expected market return itself, subtract the risk-free rate before multiplying by beta. Use a risk-free input consistent with the currency and maturity convention of the analysis.

Choose comparison companies thoughtfully

Historical equity beta reflects both business risk and financing risk. A highly indebted peer can have a larger equity beta even if its operations closely match the project. Sector peers, leverage differences and the noise in historical estimates deserve attention when selecting an input.

Use marginal debt terms for similar borrowers and maturity. A subsidized old bank facility or a short floating loan might poorly represent a long-term corporate investment. Where the case permits a range, show how the plausible inputs affect the cost of finance.

Method

Estimate current required returns

  1. Debt schedule

    Write the current price or net proceeds and future contractual payments. For one year divide the payment by the price and subtract one.

  2. Equity estimate

    Check whether the market input is a premium or a total expected return. Multiply the premium by equity beta and add the risk-free rate.

  3. Qualify

    Describe credit loss, leverage, currency and term assumptions. State whether taxes have already been included in the input.

Worked case

A bond trades below face value

A one-year bond with face value 100 and coupon 4 trades at 98. Its principal and coupon are assumed certain for the calculation. The risk-free rate is 3%, the market's expected return 8% and the firm's equity beta 1.4.

Working

  1. The bond pays 104 at maturity. Its promised one-year yield is 104/98 − 1 = 6.12%, despite the printed coupon of 4%.
  2. The market risk premium is 8% − 3% = 5%. CAPM cost of equity is 3% + 1.4 × 5% = 10%.
  3. If a new otherwise comparable loan is available at 6.2%, that current quote is more informative about new borrowing than the historical coupon. The two outputs concern debt and equity with different claims.

Interpretation

A shift in the firm's financing policy could change both its market borrowing quote and levered equity beta. Treating either as a permanent company constant would require justification.

A common mistake

Multiplying beta by a market return that already includes the risk-free rate.

How to correct it

Label the market input. If it is a total return, subtract the same risk-free rate used in the final addition.

Quick check
If beta falls to 0.8 with the same risk-free rate and market premium, what cost of equity does this model give?

The market-risk component is 0.8 × 5% = 4%. Add the 3% risk-free rate to reach 7%.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

A one-year note pays 108 on a price of 102. A default scenario with 10% probability would instead pay 70. Equity beta is 0.9, the risk-free rate 2% and market premium 6%.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate the promised and expected one-year returns on the note.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Compute the cost of equity under CAPM.

  3. Part C · 2 marks

    Why would simply using the 8-unit coupon rate for current finance be questionable?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Promised return is 108/102 − 1 = 5.88%. Expected payment is 0.9 × 108 + 0.1 × 70 = 104.2, giving expected return 104.2/102 − 1 = 2.16%.

Part B 2 marks

Required equity return is 2% + 0.9 × 6% = 7.4%. This is an expected required return, not a contractual amount.

Part C 2 marks

The buyer pays 102, may suffer default, and receives principal as well as coupon. Current new borrowing also depends on comparable term and credit conditions.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Can a firm use its 7% bond coupon as its project hurdle rate when its equity beta is 1.3?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would reject 7% as a general corporate hurdle. It is a contractual payment on old debt. I would estimate current borrowing terms from comparably risky debt and the equity return from the market exposure of shareholders. With a 3% baseline, a 5% premium and beta 1.3, the equity component is 9.5%. I would next test which operating risk and financing policy match the project.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If half the 7% coupon were subsidized by a government guarantee, would the business itself become safer?

Compare with the follow-up answer

A guarantee can reduce financing costs or transfer loss to the guarantor. It does not automatically reduce the risk of the factory's sales or costs. I would value a contractual subsidy separately when its benefit is specific to this financing.
