Portfolio and country risk

Defaults can cluster by industry, currency or region. Two loans with the same standalone loss estimate can contribute different amounts of portfolio risk.

The starting point

A lender can know the expected loss of each loan and still underestimate the chance of a severe portfolio loss. Customers may depend on the same export market, commodity price or exchange rate. In a shared shock, defaults occur together. Correlation matters especially for the distribution of losses around the average.

Country risk can include exchange restrictions or transfer difficulties as well as deteriorating business conditions. A company can earn enough local currency yet struggle to obtain the foreign currency its debt requires. Trace customers, suppliers, debt currency and repayment routes before calling two exposures diversified.

Theory and method

Expected shortfall and tail contribution

Conditional value at risk · Concentration risk

Which borrower contributes most to a portfolio’s worst 2.5% of losses if the largest country event occurs only 2% of the time?

A portfolio manager wants to know which relationship consumes capacity in deep stress. Expected loss averages across all years. Expected shortfall averages just the worst fraction of outcomes at a chosen level. A whole scenario can be more frequent than the final slice required for the tail, so only the relevant fraction of its probability belongs in that average. Loan contributions can then be measured in exactly the same tail states as the combined portfolio.

How the result is built

ES₉₇.₅ = Σ wⱼ(LAⱼ + LBⱼ)/0.025

ES₉₇.₅ is expected portfolio loss in its worst 2.5% probability mass. The index j labels scenarios, wⱼ is probability mass from scenario j included in that tail, and LAⱼ and LBⱼ are monetary losses on loans A and B in thousands of euros.

  1. In normal years, probability 95%, both loans lose zero. A sector event with 3% probability costs A 25 and B zero. A broad event with 2% probability costs A 40 and B 20.
  2. Rank portfolio losses 60, 25, zero. Take all 2% from the broad event and only 0.5% from the sector event to fill the 2.5% tail.
  3. Expected shortfall is (0.02 × 60 + 0.005 × 25)/0.025 = 53. A contributes 37 and B contributes 16, using the portfolio’s tail weights.

When the reasoning holds

  • Losses are measured at one common horizon and the stated discrete scenarios exhaust the distribution. An omitted severe event can dominate the result.
  • Proportional allocation within the cutoff state assumes all outcomes in that state have the same specified losses. Conditional averaging above a strict quantile alone can omit needed probability mass.
Terms in this lesson
Correlation
A measure of how closely two series move together in a linear pattern.
Principal
The amount borrowed or invested before interest is added.
Diversification
Combining exposures that do not always move together to reduce dependence on a single outcome.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
Counterparty
The other party to a financial contract or trade.
How to read the notation
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.

All notation examples

First example

Two €100 loans each have a 5% chance of losing the full principal. Their total expected loss is €10. Under independence, the chance both default is 0.05 × 0.05 = 0.25%. If both fail in the same 5%-probability shock, expected loss is still €10, but the chance of losing €200 is 5%.

Calculate the portfolio distribution

Expected losses add across loans because expectation is linear. Independence is unnecessary for that sum. Dependence becomes important when asking how often losses exceed a level, how much capital a severe event might consume, or how much diversification a new loan contributes.

A portfolio of small loans can behave like one large risk if every customer depends on a single employer or export buyer. Nominal loan count and borrower legal names can mask this link. Map ultimate sources of cash, common collateral, guarantees and shared funding markets.

Move from domestic cash to foreign debt service

A borrower can be solvent in local currency but unable to obtain or transfer the currency its foreign lender requires. Separate commercial default, adverse exchange-rate movement, convertibility restrictions and transfer restrictions. Each may harm collections through a different route.

A currency hedge can have an amount or maturity mismatch, collateral requirements, and an exposed counterparty. Export receipts in the debt currency provide a natural hedge only if they actually arrive in the correct entity by the payment date. A domestic sales invoice labelled in dollars is less useful if buyers cannot obtain them.

Use scenario limits intelligently

A country limit can restrict gross exposure, net exposure after permitted guarantees, or stressed loss. State the chosen unit and avoid counting a foreign guarantee twice. A facility may concentrate country exposure despite a globally diversified parent if local restrictions trap the operating cash.

Construct a common scenario and propagate its consequences loan by loan. Recalculate debt burden, cash transfer dates, default and recovery. Then compare aggregate loss with the stated tolerance. An average loss estimate is a different quantity from the amount lost if the scenario actually occurs.

Method

Map the shared shock

  1. Exposure

    Identify underlying customers, countries, currencies, maturities and guarantees for each legal borrower.

  2. Transmission

    Write the path from the external event to each borrower’s cash generation, contractual payment and possible recovery.

  3. Aggregation

    Apply portfolio limits to the required measure. Show both state probabilities and conditional loss amounts, and identify which addition most increases the severe outcome.

Worked case

Two suppliers and one port closure

A bank lends €200,000 to each of two suppliers. If a port closes, both lose 50% of their outstanding principal. Closure probability is 4%. Each performs otherwise. A third, unrelated domestic customer has the same individual loss distribution but its event is independent of the port.

Working

  1. Each supplier’s expected loss is 0.04 × €100,000 = €4,000. Together the expected total is €8,000, and there is a 4% chance of losing €200,000 at once.
  2. Replace one supplier with the domestic customer. The expected total is still €8,000. Joint loss of €200,000 now occurs with probability 0.04 × 0.04 = 0.16%.
  3. The chance of any loss becomes 1 − 0.96² = 7.84%, up from 4%. Diversification reduces the frequency of the largest loss even while increasing the chance of a smaller loss somewhere.

Interpretation

This distinction is central when interpreting a portfolio limit. An objective to cap disaster losses produces a different question from an objective to eliminate every small loss.

A common mistake

Summing individual default probabilities and calling the result the probability of losing both loans.

How to correct it

Write the joint scenarios and their dependence. Sum expected monetary losses freely. Use the joint distribution to infer a tail probability.

Quick check
Which loan offers less diversification alongside an existing loan to a copper exporter, all else equal?

Sharing the same buyer can make both borrowers lose revenue at the same time. Similar individual loss estimates can conceal this joint exposure.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A borrower owes US$1m tomorrow and has local-currency cash worth US$1.2m at the old exchange rate. Its local currency falls by 25% in dollar value, and a transfer limit permits at most US$0.8m of remittances.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find its dollar resources and actual maximum transfer.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Which stress persists if the currency immediately recovers but the cap remains?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

After depreciation the cash buys US$0.9m. The transfer cap binds at US$0.8m, leaving US$0.2m unpaid by tomorrow under these terms.

Part B 2 marks

The transfer restriction still prevents sending more than US$0.8m despite adequate local purchasing power. Offshore dollars available to the borrower, or a reliable in-currency guarantee, could change collection feasibility.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Which borrower contributes most to a portfolio’s worst 2.5% of losses if the largest country event occurs only 2% of the time?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Loan A contributes €37,000 of the €53,000 tail average. I take the entire 2% broad shock and enough of the 3% sector shock to fill the residual 0.5%. Using the identical portfolio tail for each borrower preserves additivity. I would review country concentration, sector dependence, and recovery assumptions before deciding whether to reduce A or add independent exposure.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If the sector event causes loan B an additional €10,000 loss, while the broad state remains the worst, what changes?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The sector portfolio loss becomes 35. Expected shortfall rises to (0.02 × 60 + 0.005 × 35)/0.025 = 55. A’s contribution stays 37 and B’s rises from 16 to 18, because only one fifth of the selected 2.5% tail comes from that sector state.
