Residential mortgages and household affordability

Calculate the payment ceiling and maximum residential loan under income and valuation limits.

Theory and terminology

Mortgage affordability and collateral limits

debt-to-income · DTI · loan-to-value · LTV

What you should be able to explain

  • Calculate the payment ceiling and maximum residential loan under income and valuation limits.
  • Reconcile savings, closing costs and post-closing reserves, including a reset-rate stress.

Rebuild the household’s monthly budget

Start with gross monthly income accepted by the lender. Distinguish recurring verified earnings from a recent discretionary bonus. List other recurring debt payments separately from the new housing payment. Include mortgage principal and interest, taxes, insurance and required association dues under the policy in the case.

A debt-to-income ratio divides monthly contractual obligations by qualifying monthly income. Two families with the same ratio may have different disposable cash, tax liabilities and childcare costs. Review residual cash after essential spending as well as the ratio. The thresholds below belong to a hypothetical lender.

Convert payment capacity into principal

An amortizing mortgage requires equal end-of-month payments that cover interest and reduce principal. At a higher interest rate, the same payment supports a smaller loan. State whether the product fixes the rate for its full life or can reset, and which rate the credit policy requires for qualification.

Collateral provides a separate constraint. For this purchase the lender uses the smaller of the independently appraised value and contract price. A low appraisal can force the buyer to contribute additional cash even when the monthly payment is affordable.

Verify cash at the closing table

Document acceptable sources for the purchase contribution and closing costs. Cash required to remain after closing cannot also finance the purchase. A household financing its down payment may create another monthly obligation, which changes the affordability calculation.

Record the binding limit, the funding gap, and what documentation would change the decision. A promise of future savings has a different evidential weight from money already verified in the bank.

Method

Build the result

M = dY − O − H. Lpay = M[1 − (1 + i)^(−n)]/i. Lmax = min(Lpay, v × V).

Y is monthly qualifying income, d the allowed total debt ratio, O other monthly debt, H monthly housing costs excluding principal and interest, and M the monthly principal-and-interest allowance. i is the monthly qualifying rate, n the number of payments, v the allowed loan-to-value fraction and V the eligible property value. Both limits are in currency units.

  1. Multiply income by the allowed ratio to obtain total permissible monthly debt. Deduct existing debt and the nonloan housing costs once.
  2. Discount the affordable level payments at the qualifying rate to find their present principal equivalent. At a zero rate, the equivalent is M × n.
  3. Compare this principal with the valuation limit. Test whether available savings can fund the remaining purchase price and closing costs while preserving reserves.
Worked case

An adjustable-rate purchase with a low appraisal

A buyer offers $330,000. The appraisal is $325,000. Monthly income is $8,000, other debt $700 and housing costs excluding principal and interest $550. A 30-year loan starts at 6% and must qualify at 7.2%. Use monthly rates equal to annual rates divided by 12. The lender allows 36% total debt and 80% loan-to-value. Savings are $92,000, closing costs $7,000 and required reserves $10,000.

Working

  1. Monthly principal-and-interest capacity is 0.36 × $8,000 − $700 − $550 = $1,630.
  2. At 6% a $260,000 loan costs $1,558.83 monthly. At 7.2% it costs $1,764.85. The higher payment takes total debt to $3,014.85, or 37.69% of income.
  3. The stressed payment limit supports about $240,134. The valuation limit is 0.80 × $325,000 = $260,000. Use the smaller figure.
  4. The buyer can apply $92,000 − $7,000 − $10,000 = $75,000 toward the price. Minimum necessary borrowing is $255,000, about $14,866 above stressed capacity.

Decision and interpretation

Defer this structure. A documented additional contribution or a lower purchase price could close the gap. A different product requires its own qualifying payment and costs.

Quick check
Which new documentation most directly closes the worked buyer’s binding gap, assuming the lender accepts it?

The extra contribution reduces the required loan to $240,000, within the stressed income limit. More property value leaves that limit unchanged.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

A $280,000 purchase appraises at $270,000. Monthly income is $6,500. Total debt may reach 40%, other debt is $450 and additional housing costs are $450. Each dollar borrowed costs $0.0068 per month at the qualifying rate. The loan-to-value limit is 85%. Savings equal $60,000, costs $7,000 and required reserves $5,000. Can the buyer close?

Reveal the worked solution
  1. The loan payment ceiling is $2,600 − $450 − $450 = $1,700, supporting $1,700/0.0068 = $250,000.
  2. The property limit is 0.85 × $270,000 = $229,500. Available purchase cash is $60,000 − $7,000 − $5,000 = $48,000.
  3. The buyer needs $232,000, leaving a $2,500 gap. Seek documented funds or renegotiate the price and recalculate both limits.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Can this household fund a purchase and service the mortgage if its initial rate resets?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would qualify the household at the required reset rate, verify the appraisal and reconcile its expected closing cash. The ratio permits a $1,630 monthly loan payment. At 7.2% that supports about $240,134. The buyer needs $255,000 after documented costs and reserves, so the present application has a cash shortfall of about $14,866.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Would retiring an installment debt necessarily solve this case?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Lower debt raises the income-based limit, which already exceeds the property ceiling. Paying it from the buyer’s savings reduces available purchase cash. The revision requires a new cash-to-close reconciliation.
