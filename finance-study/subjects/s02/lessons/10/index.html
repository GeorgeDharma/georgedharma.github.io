Credit scoring and judgment

Distinguish ranking borrowers from calibrating their actual default probabilities. Check overrides, missing observations, fairness and performance across time and borrower groups.

The starting point

A credit score can perform two jobs. Ranking orders applicants from lower to higher risk. Calibration assigns the actual likelihood of default over a stated time. A model can rank borrowers sensibly while consistently underestimating their default probabilities, for example after an economic downturn.

Evaluate the model on later data that were unavailable at development. Compare forecast and realized default rates within bands, examine discrimination, and review missing data and human overrides. Rejected applicants can be difficult to evaluate because the lender never observes how its own proposed loans would have performed.

Theory and method

Bayes’ theorem in credit screening

Positive predictive value · Base-rate effect

A fraud or credit alert catches 80% of eventual defaults. Why might most flagged borrowers still perform?

Consider 1,000 otherwise comparable applications. If only 2% would default, the alert has just twenty defaulters available to catch, while even a modest false-positive rate operates on the other 980 applicants. A screening rule can detect most defaulters and still produce many performing customers among alerts. The lending decision requires the probability of default given an alert, whereas measured sensitivity starts from a population already known to have defaulted.

How the result is built

P(D | A) = sπ/[sπ + f(1 − π)]

D means default within one specified year, A is a positive alert, π is portfolio default prevalence, s is alert sensitivity among defaulters, and f is its false-positive rate among performing borrowers. P(D | A) is the fraction of alerts that default.

  1. With 2% prevalence, 80% sensitivity and 10% false-positive rate, expect 16 true alerts among 20 defaulting customers and 98 false alerts among 980 performing customers.
  2. Total expected alerts are 114 per 1,000. Sixteen divided by 114 gives about 14.04% default among alerted cases.
  3. At 5% prevalence the same characteristics yield 40 true and 95 false alerts. Conditional default rises to 40/135 = 29.63% without changing the rule itself.

When the reasoning holds

  • Sensitivity and false-positive rates are assumed transferable to the new cohort. Policy changes or drift in applicant characteristics may alter both.
  • Outcomes must correspond to the same observation horizon and target population. A sample containing only approvals can bias default prevalence and alert accuracy.
Terms in this lesson
Calibration
Choosing model parameters so that the model reproduces specified market observations as closely as possible.
First example

A low-risk group with predicted one-year default of 1% realizes 2%. A higher-risk group with prediction 5% realizes 8%. The grouping orders outcomes correctly in this sample, but the observed rates exceed both forecasts. Sample size and uncertainty matter before changing the model.

Separate ranking from probability accuracy

Two applicants can be ordered from higher to lower risk without knowing either true default probability. Discrimination measures how often a score assigns greater risk to customers who later default than to those who perform. A ranking metric can stay unchanged after multiplying all predicted probabilities by two.

Calibration asks whether, among customers assigned a particular probability, the observed event frequency supports that estimate over the matching horizon. A model can discriminate well but dramatically underprice loans by understating every probability. Measure both properties on observations unavailable when the model was fitted.

Approval policy changes what is observable

Credit outcomes are usually observed on loans the institution funded. A policy rejecting applicants below a cutoff changes the observed sample. A score that performs well inside the approved range may behave differently outside it. Treat claims about declined customers as assumptions unless separate outcome evidence exists.

Avoid leakage from collections or later account deterioration into features purportedly available when underwriting. Divide tests by origination time, customer identity where relevant, and business-cycle period. Missing values can signal genuine differences, inconsistent data capture or treatment that changes across branches.

Set a cutoff with economic and fairness evidence

The cost of declining a viable customer differs from the loss on approving a defaulting one. A cutoff can be based on expected contribution using spread, exposure, loss severity and the calibrated probability. A lender should separately evaluate outcomes across relevant groups and investigate whether variables proxy for inappropriate factors under applicable rules.

Judgment may add information absent from standardized inputs. Record both upward and downward overrides, the reason, who approved them and subsequent performance. Comparing overridden cases directly with all others can be misleading because they may have entered review for a reason.

Method

Validate an underwriting score

  1. Time split

    Freeze the training period and model. Test later originations using only information documented as available on each application date.

  2. Two checks

    Compute exposure-appropriate observed and expected defaults by band, then check whether ordering survives across cohorts. Include uncertainty for small groups.

  3. Decision

    Compare the decision threshold with expected unit economics. Investigate errors, overrides and group disparities before changing the credit rule.

Worked case

A cutoff that looked sensible in training

A retail lender observes 2,000 low-band customers with forecast 1% and 500 high-band customers with forecast 4%. In a later year, 40 and 30 default. Treat counts as unweighted customers and forecasts as one-year probabilities.

Working

  1. Expected defaults are 20 and 20. Observed rates are 2% and 6%. Both cohorts perform worse than forecast. Overall observed 70 versus expected 40 gives an observed-to-expected ratio of 1.75.
  2. The high band still has a higher realized default rate. This two-band check supports ordering while revealing underprediction. It does not establish a precise ranking within either band.
  3. If a performing loan contributes €400 and a defaulting loan loses €6,000, break-even probability solves (1 − p) × €400 − p × €6,000 = 0, or p = 6.25%.

Interpretation

The high band’s point estimate of 6% is close to break-even. Sample variation, a changed economy and concentration in particular applicant groups deserve examination before extending approval.

At the board

Read a credit-screening result in both directions

In a fictional historical cohort, 40 of 1,000 borrowers defaulted. A warning system flagged 30 of those 40 and flagged 96 of the 960 other borrowers. A flag sends a case to manual review.

Prior observationFlaggedUnflaggedAll borrowers
Later defaulted301040
Later repaid96864960
Total1268741,000

The system detected 30 / 40 = 75% of the defaults. A reviewer with a flagged case wants the reverse probability, 30 / 126 = 23.8%. Among unflagged cases, 10 / 874 = 1.14% defaulted. All three answers are correct for different populations. Threshold changes alter missed defaults and review workload. Do not carry this percentage into a new population without checking its base default rate.

A common mistake

Calling a model accurate because it correctly classified 98% of loans in a portfolio with a 2% default rate.

How to correct it

Compare it with the rule that predicts no defaults at all, measure ranking and calibration, and connect classification errors to the losses and foregone contributions they create.

Quick check
Which observation most directly tests calibration of a 3% one-year prediction?

Calibration asks whether reported probabilities correspond to observed frequencies over the same horizon. Validation also checks sample sizes and shifts in borrower conditions.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A score forecasts 2% default for 400 borrowers and 8% for 100 others. Eight and twelve borrowers default respectively during the following year.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Evaluate calibration and ordering at the band level.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    What additional analysis would distinguish a temporary poor year from a persistent model problem?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

The lower band matches its 2% forecast. The higher band realizes 12% versus 8% forecast. The broad ordering persists, but higher-risk probabilities appear understated in this sample.

Part B 2 marks

Check several untouched origination cohorts with consistent horizons and applicant mix, quantify uncertainty, and compare performance by underwriting policy and economic conditions. A single 100-customer band supplies limited precision.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A fraud or credit alert catches 80% of eventual defaults. Why might most flagged borrowers still perform?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would ask the question in the direction the underwriter actually faces. Starting from an alert, only 16 of 114 flagged customers in the example default, about 14%. That may justify more investigation or pricing, depending on loss and margin. Automatic rejection would also turn away 98 performers. I would validate prevalence and classification by cohort before using the rule on a different customer population.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The bank raises screening specificity from 90% to 95% while maintaining 80% sensitivity. What happens at 2% prevalence?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Moving specificity from 90% to 95% halves the false-positive rate from 10% to 5%. This gives 49 false alerts and 16 true ones. The default share rises to 16/65 ≈ 24.62%. Fewer performing applicants lose access under a policy triggered by the alert.
