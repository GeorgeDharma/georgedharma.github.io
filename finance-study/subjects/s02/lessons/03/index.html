The operating and cash cycles

Trace purchase, stock, sale, collection and supplier payment. Seasonal working capital can support a revolver. Long-lived equipment usually needs a different maturity.

The starting point

A business can make profitable sales and still borrow to meet payroll while it waits for customers to pay. The operating cycle runs from acquiring inventory through collecting the sale. Supplier credit finances part of that time. The cash conversion cycle is inventory days plus collection days minus supplier-payment days.

A revolving line lets the firm draw and repay repeatedly as seasonal needs change. Test whether the balance actually falls when collections arrive. Permanent growth or deteriorating collections can produce persistent borrowing that behaves differently from a short seasonal advance.

Theory and method

Cash conversion cycle and the cost of trade credit

Working-capital financing · Early-payment discount

Your customer can pay €98 today or €100 in 20 days. How do you decide whether faster supplier payment makes sense for its credit line?

A retailer receives 2% off an invoice for paying on day ten rather than day thirty. Delaying payment preserves €98 for another twenty days but costs €2. Supplier credit therefore has an implicit price. Faster payment increases the retailer’s cash conversion cycle because supplier financing ends earlier. It can still make economic sense if committed borrowing covers the gap more cheaply and the retailer can use the line without breaching collateral or liquidity terms.

How the result is built

Period cost = (F − E)/E; simple annual equivalent = [(F − E)/E] × 365/d

F is the full invoice in euros, E is the discounted early payment in euros, and d is the additional days allowed. The annual rate uses a 365-day simple convention solely for comparison.

  1. Compare €100 later with €98 now. The effective amount borrowed from the supplier for twenty days is €98, producing a period cost of €2/€98 = 2.0408%.
  2. Annualizing simply gives 2.0408% × 365/20 = 37.24%. An effective compound annual rate would assume the same opportunities can be repeated throughout the year.
  3. A 10% annual simple bank rate costs €98 × 0.10 × 20/365 ≈ €0.54 for those days. Capturing the €2 discount leaves about €1.46 before fees.

When the reasoning holds

  • Payment on day ten must actually qualify for the discount, and the line must be legally and operationally available then.
  • Use the marginal cost of funding. Commitment fees already sunk and a fresh transaction fee have different effects on this particular choice.
Terms in this lesson
Working capital
Money tied up in day-to-day operations. For a deal, agree which receivables, stock and payables count.
Receivable
Money a customer owes for a sale already made.
Payable
Money a business owes a supplier for goods or services already received.
First example

A clothing distributor holds goods for 50 days, collects 35 days after sale and pays suppliers after 25 days. Its cash cycle is 50 + 35 − 25 = 60 days. With a simplified steady outlay of €2,000 daily, the gap is roughly €120,000.

Relate physical movement to financial statements

Inventory days estimate how long goods sit before sale. Divide average inventory by cost of goods sold and multiply by the days in the period. Collection days use trade receivables and credit sales. Payment days ideally use trade payables and credit purchases. These denominators differ because invoices to customers include a selling margin.

A report using closing balances from a holiday peak may systematically overstate typical days. Conversely, a quiet year-end can hide the peak funding requirement. Weekly or monthly averages, invoice aging and a purchasing calendar improve the interpretation.

Two different clocks

The operating cycle follows purchase, storage, sale and customer payment. The cash cycle begins when suppliers receive funds. Supplier credit shortens the interval during which the business needs its own or the bank’s money. A firm collecting customer deposits in advance can even have a negative cash cycle.

A days calculation measures time. Turning it into euros requires the relevant daily flow. Use cost or purchases for stock and payables, and selling-price credit sales for receivables. Directly forecast cash by date when large deliveries, duties or advance payments make daily averages unrealistic.

Seasonal and permanent needs

A healthy seasonal revolver may build during the buying season and run down after collections. If it stays drawn after peak sales, examine whether stock is obsolete, customer payment is late or the business has grown permanently. Extending the line without understanding the change can fund hidden losses.

A borrower may improve reported payment days by withholding overdue suppliers. This can ease short-term cash while threatening future deliveries. Ask whether terms were negotiated or simply breached and estimate how much funding would be needed if suppliers demanded prompt payment.

Method

Reconstruct the requirement

  1. Measure

    Use comparable periods and units for balances and daily flows. Note the choice of credit purchases or a stated cost proxy.

  2. Stress

    Translate extra storage or collection days using their own daily cost or sales rates. Include supplier terms that might tighten simultaneously.

  3. Verify

    Build a dated cash schedule around the peak and compare it with committed usable credit after collateral restrictions.

Worked case

Trading faster while needing more cash

An importer sells €12,000 on credit and buys €8,000 of goods each day. It holds 25 days of stock, takes 30 days to collect and pays suppliers after 20 days. Assume steady volume and no other working-capital items.

Working

  1. Inventory is €200,000, receivables €360,000 and payables €160,000. Operating working capital is €400,000. The cash cycle is 25 + 30 − 20 = 35 days.
  2. A supplier grants ten more payment days, releasing €80,000. A new customer mix slows collections by ten days, tying up €120,000. The two changes leave the cycle at 35 days.
  3. Cash tied up nevertheless grows to €440,000 because sales and purchases have different daily values. A bank evaluating a requested €40,000 increase can now ask whether the underlying changes are sustainable.

Interpretation

Checking cash from the individual balances prevents a day-based shortcut from concealing growth in the borrowing requirement.

A common mistake

Multiplying every change in days by revenue.

How to correct it

Apply a balance’s own flow. A day of stock is carried at cost, while a day of invoices generally includes the seller’s margin.

Quick check
If customers begin paying ten days later with unchanged daily outlays, how much additional cash is tied up?

Ten extra days × €2,000 per day = €20,000. The case assumes steady activity. Seasonal peaks call for a dated cash forecast.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A company buys €5,000 of stock daily, sells €7,000 daily on credit, and negotiates three fewer inventory days. Customer payment slows by five days while suppliers give two additional days.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate the combined change in operating working capital.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Which operational records would help determine whether the increase is temporary?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Inventory falls €15,000, receivables rise €35,000, and payables rise €10,000. Net working capital rises by −€15,000 + €35,000 − €10,000 = €10,000.

Part B 2 marks

Review customer invoice ages and promised payment dates, verify supplier terms, and compare stock movements and upcoming orders with the seasonal purchasing plan. These reveal whether the changes reverse near the normal collection peak.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Your customer can pay €98 today or €100 in 20 days. How do you decide whether faster supplier payment makes sense for its credit line?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would price the cash days released by the supplier. The borrower effectively pays €2 to keep €98 for twenty days, whereas the bank funding costs about €0.54 at 10%. I would support taking the discount if a dated cash forecast and borrowing-base certificate show capacity. If the line is tight, the €1.46 saving must be weighed against a missed payroll or another lost discount.

Interviewer’s follow-up

A €1.75 transaction fee applies every time the bank funds one invoice. What happens to your conclusion?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Bank interest plus the fee is about €2.29 on this invoice, above the €2 discount. At these amounts deferring payment is cheaper by about €0.29. Batch financing could change the marginal fee, so I would verify whether the charge applies per invoice or per draw.
