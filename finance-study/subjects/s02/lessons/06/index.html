Default, exposure and severity

Separate probability of default, exposure at default and loss given default. A borrower can draw an unused line or lose collateral value just when it becomes distressed.

The starting point

A loss estimate asks three different questions. Probability of default (PD) measures the chance of default during the chosen horizon. Exposure at default (EAD) is the amount owed when default happens. Loss given default (LGD) is the fraction of that exposure ultimately lost after recoveries on the chosen valuation basis.

A simple expected-loss calculation is PD × EAD × LGD using appropriate conditional inputs. A customer may draw more of a credit line just before trouble, while collateral values may fall during the same downturn. Stress tests change exposure and severity as well as the chance of default.

Theory and method

Conditional default hazard and marginal expected loss

Survival curve · PD–EAD–LGD decomposition

Your model gives annual conditional default rates of 2%, 3% and 5%. Why can a three-year expected-loss model not charge all three probabilities to the original balance?

A borrower can default for the first time only once. The second-year 3% rate applies to borrowers that survived year one. Meanwhile principal may amortize and recoveries can deteriorate in a recession. A model for a multi-year facility therefore tracks the surviving share entering each year, the amount exposed on that year’s default, and the net loss conditional on it. A conditional rate from an annual rating table requires this translation before aggregation.

How the result is built

S₀ = 1; Sₜ = Sₜ₋₁(1 − hₜ); EL = Σ Sₜ₋₁hₜEₜLₜ

Sₜ is the probability of surviving through year t, hₜ is that year’s default probability conditional on entering it, Eₜ is exposure in euros at default, Lₜ is fractional loss severity, and EL is total expected undiscounted euro loss over the three years.

  1. With annual hazards 2%, 3% and 5%, survival after each year is 0.98, 0.9506 and 0.90307. The probability of default by year three is 9.693%.
  2. The probabilities of first default in years one through three are 0.02, 0.0294 and 0.04753. They sum to 0.09693.
  3. For exposures €100, €80 and €60 with 50% severity, expected losses are €1.00, €1.176 and €1.4259. Total €3.6019, before any discounting.

When the reasoning holds

  • Loss is recorded once on first default. Cures, redefaults and competing prepayments require an expanded state model.
  • Here exposure and severity are specified per year. If larger draws occur in precisely the cases with worse recoveries, estimate their joint default-state loss.

Terms in this lesson
Probability of default (PD)
The chance that the borrower meets the specified default definition over the stated horizon.
Exposure at default (EAD)
Amount at risk when a borrower defaults. It can include draws on a previously unused facility.
Loss given default (LGD)
The fraction of exposure lost if a borrower defaults, after the recoveries included in the chosen definition.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
Principal
The amount borrowed or invested before interest is added.
First example

A firm has drawn €400,000 on a €1m line. Assume it draws half of the €600,000 unused amount before default. EAD is €700,000. With 2% one-year PD and 40% LGD, expected loss is 0.02 × €700,000 × 0.40 = €5,600.

Loss probability and loss amount

Default means failure under the definition used for this model, such as an unremedied missed payment. State the observation horizon, whether default is measured for the customer or facility, and the population being measured. A one-year probability cannot simply be substituted for a lifetime probability in a multi-year loan.

Exposure at default represents what the lender is owed when the default happens. A credit card or revolver can be drawn further before failure. A conversion factor approximates the share of previously unused credit expected to become drawn. Repayments and contractual caps also affect exposure.

Severity conditional on default

Loss given default compares the economic shortfall with exposure once default has occurred. Include realistic proceeds, enforcement expenses and the time recoveries take. If collateral proceeds arrive two years after default, discounting can make the economic loss larger than a comparison of nominal principal and sale proceeds suggests.

A guarantee can move part of recovery to a second obligor. The guarantee’s value depends on the joint event of borrower distress, guarantor capacity and legal enforceability. Adding both the full collateral value and a guarantee covering the same shortfall can overstate proceeds.

Where the product formula comes from

A performing loan contributes zero credit loss in this simple binary model. With probability p it defaults and loses some amount L. Average loss therefore equals p times the average loss conditional on default. EAD times LGD is a useful way to express that conditional loss when appropriately exposure-weighted or fixed.

When exposure and severity move together within default scenarios, compute each scenario’s loss before averaging. Multiplying a simple average exposure by a simple average severity can conceal their dependence. Separate this expected loss from the tail loss that may determine available capital.

Method

Construct the loss estimate

  1. Specify

    Choose the horizon, default event and currency. Draw the performing and default branches first.

  2. Map cash

    Measure conditional exposure and present recovery branch by branch, after permitted expenses, and specify any unused commitment.

  3. Aggregate

    Multiply branch probability by monetary loss, sum across states and stress the assumptions likely to worsen together.

Worked case

A revolver with delayed collections

A €1m line has €500,000 drawn and a 60% conversion factor on unused commitments. On default the lender expects €440,000 net recovery one year later. Use 10% to discount that recovery to the default date. The one-year PD is 3%. Treat losses as measured at the default date.

Working

  1. Conditional exposure is €500,000 + 0.60 × €500,000 = €800,000. Recovery at default-date value is €440,000/1.10 = €400,000.
  2. Monetary loss on default is €400,000 and severity is 50%. Expected one-year loss on the stated timing basis is 0.03 × €400,000 = €12,000.
  3. If the borrower draws the full line before default with identical recovery, severity rises to 60% and expected loss to €18,000. This is a drawdown effect even with unchanged PD.

Interpretation

A separate end-to-today discount may be required under a different valuation convention. Stating the measurement date makes the arithmetic auditable.

A common mistake

Multiplying the default rate by the current balance and calling it the loss.

How to correct it

Estimate exposure at default and subtract conditional recoveries. State whose probability, what horizon and which severity the resulting amount uses.

Quick check
Keeping the same EAD and PD, what happens if recoveries fall to 40% of exposure?

A recovery of 40% means a loss severity of 60%. €700,000 × 0.02 × 0.60 = €8,400.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A €250,000 loan has 4% probability of default. Half of defaults produce net recovery €180,000 and half €80,000, all measured at default.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Compute mean loss conditional on default and unconditional expected loss.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Would averaging the two recovery rates give the same answer if exposures differed? Explain.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Conditional losses are €70,000 and €170,000, averaging €120,000. The unconditional expected loss is €120,000 × 0.04 = €4,800.

Part B 2 marks

Equal exposures make simple averages work here. With unequal exposures, calculate exposure minus recovery separately in each state, or use properly exposure-weighted severity. Giving equal weight to percentages alone can distort euro loss.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Your model gives annual conditional default rates of 2%, 3% and 5%. Why can a three-year expected-loss model not charge all three probabilities to the original balance?

Compare with a spoken answer

I would convert annual hazards into first-default probabilities before applying exposure. On the stated curve year three contributes 4.753% of the original cohort, then I multiply by the €60 balance and 50% loss severity. The lifetime total is about €3.60, before discounting. Adding 2%, 3% and 5% and charging that sum to the original €100 misses both survival and amortization.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What happens to the year-three contribution if half the surviving borrowers prepay in full at the start of that year, independently of default risk?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Only half of the 95.06% survivors then have an exposure. Year-three expected loss falls from €1.4259 to €0.71295 per original loan. If prepayment disproportionately removes the safest borrowers, I would also re-estimate the conditional hazard of the remaining pool.
