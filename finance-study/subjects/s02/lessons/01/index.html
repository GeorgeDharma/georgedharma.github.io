Credit markets and rationing

Study why a bank may refuse a higher rate. If safe borrowers leave or borrowers shift to risky projects, the lender’s expected return can fall.

The starting point

When a loan pays its lender a fixed amount on success and little on failure, raising the quoted rate has two effects. It increases the amount collected when a borrower pays. It can also change who applies. Safer firms with modest expected gains may leave while firms pursuing high-risk opportunities remain. This is adverse selection.

Borrowers already funded might also change the projects they choose because greater upside belongs to them while lenders share some losses. That is an incentive problem called moral hazard. If either effect lowers expected collections, a bank may prefer to ration credit at its existing rate despite unmet demand.

Theory and method

Stiglitz–Weiss credit rationing

Adverse selection · Incentive compatibility · Risk shifting

Can a higher repayment promise make an existing borrower choose a worse project even if the bank knows the borrower’s type?

A manufacturer can install a dependable machine yielding €155 for sure, or a speculative one yielding €186 with probability one half and zero otherwise. An owner with limited liability keeps only the part above the promised repayment. Raising that promise takes a euro from the dependable project in every state, while it takes a euro from the speculative project only in its success state. A lender can therefore change which machine is attractive even while financing exactly the same owner.

How the result is built

Uₛ = 155 − D, Uᵣ = 0.5(186 − D), Uᵣ ≥ Uₛ when D ≥ 124

D is the contractual euro repayment between zero and €155. Uₛ and Uᵣ are expected euros left to the owner under the safe and risky machines. The owner earns zero in the risky failure state.

  1. At a repayment of €100 the owner keeps €55 safely or expects half of €86, which is €43, from speculation.
  2. Set 155 − D equal to 93 − 0.5D. Moving terms across gives 62 = 0.5D, so the owner is indifferent at €124.
  3. At €130 the risky residual is €28 versus €25 safely. The bank then receives an expected €65 instead of the €130 it would collect if the safe action were enforceable.

When the reasoning holds

  • Limited liability truncates the owner’s loss at zero. Full personal liability backed by collectable wealth would alter the risky failure payoff.
  • The project choice is hidden or unenforceable. If the bank can verify the machine before disbursing directly to its seller, it can contract around this particular incentive.

Terms in this lesson
Adverse selection
A contract can attract a different mix of applicants when people know more about their own quality or risk than the other side knows.
Moral hazard
A contract can change behaviour after it is signed when the other party cannot fully observe or control those actions.
Interest rate
The price of borrowing money or the return for lending it, quoted as a percentage over a stated period.
Liability
Something owed to another party, such as a loan or an unpaid supplier bill.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
How to read the notation
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.

All notation examples

First example

For a €100 one-year loan with zero recovery, assume a 5% offer attracts borrowers with a 98% repayment chance. Expected receipts are 0.98 × €105 = €102.90. If a 12% offer attracts a pool with only an 85% repayment chance, expected receipts fall to €95.20.

Selecting a customer pool

A credit officer sees applications while entrepreneurs see their own projects. Imagine two business owners who both want €100. One expects a modest, almost certain sale. The other has a small chance of winning a large contract. Above a particular interest rate the first owner earns too little to apply. The second may still find the gamble attractive.

This difference is present before the loan is signed. A bank that knows the population average but cannot identify each type must price the applicants who actually accept. Raising a quotation changes the population entering the contract. Historical defaults from last year’s customers may describe a different group.

Why the return can bend down

A limited-liability borrower keeps project proceeds above the promised repayment. Below it, the lender may take only what the business can deliver. This makes equity’s payoff steeper on the upside. A higher repayment can induce a funded borrower to switch to a more volatile project, even with a lower expected total payoff.

The selection channel changes which people borrow. The incentive channel changes what an existing borrower does. Either can make a larger promise less valuable to the bank. When funds available at the bank’s best rate are below demand, some observationally similar applicants can go without credit despite offering to pay more.

Finding evidence in a credit file

To examine selection, compare applicants around rate changes, including people who refuse an offer. To examine incentives, observe project choices or risk-taking after disbursement. Both comparisons are vulnerable to a recession that simultaneously raises quoted rates and damages borrowers.

Collateral or equity contributions can sometimes reveal commitment or affect incentives. Their usefulness depends on lenders being able to verify value and borrowers having wealth to pledge. A sound low-wealth applicant can be excluded by a collateral-heavy rule.

Method

Evaluate a posted rate

  1. Payoff

    Write the cash collected for each outcome, including recoveries and whether interest is paid in default.

  2. Composition

    Calculate a repayment probability for the applicants accepting each offer. State which type may exit or switch projects.

  3. Decision

    Compare expected collections with the full funding cost and the supply of money available. Predict rationing only when demand at a profitable rate exceeds supply.

Worked case

Two quotations for equipment finance

A €1,000 one-year loan costs the lender €1,025 at maturity. At a 6% offer, four fifths of takers repay for sure and one fifth repay with probability 0.75. At 16% only the second type accepts. Recovery is €300 whenever a borrower defaults.

Working

  1. The first offer has repayment probability 0.8 + 0.2 × 0.75 = 0.95. Expected receipts equal 0.95 × €1,060 + 0.05 × €300 = €1,022.
  2. The second offer yields 0.75 × €1,160 + 0.25 × €300 = €945. The promised payment is larger while the bank collects €77 less in expectation.
  3. Neither quote covers the €1,025 target. If an enforceable guarantee raises recovery at the first quote to €400, receipts become €1,027. Check guarantee cost and guarantor capacity before approval.

Interpretation

The numbers explain a downward segment of the return schedule. They do not establish how many applications the bank receives or prove that rationing actually occurs at either price.

A common mistake

Treating rejected applications as proof of rationing.

How to correct it

Establish that observationally similar customers remain willing to borrow at the prevailing price and that raising the price would fail to improve the lender’s return.

Quick check
If exactly the same 98%-repayment pool would accept 12% and its behaviour stayed unchanged, what receipts would the bank expect?

With composition and behaviour held fixed, expected receipts are 0.98 × €112 = €109.76. The earlier fall depended on a change in borrowers or incentives.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A €200 loan promises €220. Low-risk takers repay with probability 0.99 and high-risk takers with probability 0.70. Each type makes up half the pool. Recoveries are zero.

  1. Part A · 2 marks

    Compute expected receipts.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    At a promised €250 only high-risk customers accept. Find receipts and name evidence for the proposed mechanism.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 2 marks

Repayment probability is (0.99 + 0.70)/2 = 0.845. Expected receipts are €220 × 0.845 = €185.90.

Part B 3 marks

€250 × 0.70 = €175. Ask which applicants refused the higher quote and compare their pre-offer characteristics. A shift in those observed applying supports selection. Changes after disbursement would instead bear on incentives.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Can a higher repayment promise make an existing borrower choose a worse project even if the bank knows the borrower’s type?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would separate two channels. Here the same applicant changes investment because the lender bears part of the downside. That is a post-contract incentive problem. Separately, higher rates can change which customers apply. In the Stiglitz–Weiss argument either channel can bend expected bank returns downward. To infer credit rationing I would also need excess demand at the bank’s preferred rate and evidence that a higher quote would fail to improve collections.

Interviewer’s follow-up

A guarantee makes the owner personally pay €20 in the risky failure state. Would the choice still switch at €130?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Risky expected residual falls from €28 to €18, while the safe residual stays €25. The safe project wins at €130. The new indifference point, within the stated payoff region, solves 155 − D = 83 − 0.5D, giving €144. The guarantee must be enforceable against independent wealth.
