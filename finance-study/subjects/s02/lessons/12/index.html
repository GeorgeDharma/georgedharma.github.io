Early warning and workout

Track arrears, covenant breaches and deteriorating collateral. Compare a waiver, restructuring and recovery under the case’s enforceable priority and timing assumptions.

The starting point

Late payments, slower customer collections, shrinking covenant headroom and unreliable financial reporting can all prompt a credit review. Separate a temporary cash delay from a business that cannot earn enough to service its debt. Trace the cash forecast and the priority of claims under each realistic option.

A waiver can give time after a breach. A restructuring might extend maturity, change payments or exchange part of a claim. Compare expected recoveries after costs and delay with prompt enforcement. Ask what must improve and which evidence would show progress. Book value alone does not determine the best recovery.

Theory and method

Discounted workout recovery and the continuation threshold

Going-concern versus liquidation analysis · Break-even turnaround probability

A banker prefers restructuring because the best outcome exceeds liquidation proceeds. What probability would make that choice economically defensible?

A lender can sell collateral now, or provide emergency cash and wait for a turnaround. Waiting buys a chance of preserving a valuable business but exposes the bank to additional loss if the rescue fails. The decision depends on the lender’s net share of proceeds in each branch, the new money it must put at risk, delay and the probability of success. A large headline enterprise value can coexist with a poor distribution to a particular lender.

How the result is built

p* = [(Q + I)(1 + r) − B]/(G − B)

p* is minimum success probability for continuation to match immediate liquidation. Q is euro recovery today, I is new euro funding today, r is the one-year discount rate, G is euro lender recovery in a good outcome after one year, and B is euro recovery in the bad outcome.

  1. Start from equal present recoveries, Q = −I + [pG + (1 − p)B]/(1 + r). Grow today’s funds to the common future date.
  2. The additional future amount required above bad-state proceeds is (Q + I)(1 + r) − B. Each additional probability point shifts receipts from B toward G.
  3. With Q €200,000, I €20,000, r 5%, G €320,000 and B €100,000, p* = (€231,000 − €100,000)/€220,000 ≈ 59.55%.

When the reasoning holds

  • Good-state recovery exceeds bad-state recovery. If G and B are equal, probability of success cannot affect lender value.
  • The quoted discount and probabilities implement one consistent case convention. Avoid adding a second charge for the same default risk already built into probability-weighted receipts.
Terms in this lesson
Covenant
A promise or condition in a financing agreement, such as maintaining an agreed level of cash.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
Discount rate
Rate used to express a future payment in today’s money, reflecting time and the relevant risk.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Working capital
Money tied up in day-to-day operations. For a deal, agree which receivables, stock and payables count.
How to read the notation
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.

All notation examples

First example

Assume enforceable liquidation would yield €70,000 today after all costs. A restructuring offers €90,000 in one year with probability 80%, and €30,000 otherwise. At a 10% discount rate, its expected present recovery is (0.8 × €90,000 + 0.2 × €30,000)/1.10 ≈ €70,909.

Recognize deterioration before a payment is missed

A borrower can keep interest current by selling inventory below cost, stretching suppliers or drawing another facility. Look for changes in collections, disputed invoices, management turnover, repeated covenant waivers and falling margin even while payments continue. Compare the figures with the original credit assumptions.

Distinguish a liquidity problem, where cash arrives too late, from a viability problem, where future operating cash cannot sustain obligations. A rolling weekly forecast exposes payment dates. A medium-term forecast tests whether the business can produce returns after maintaining its assets.

Identify the bank’s real alternatives

A waiver temporarily relaxes a contractual right. A standstill can coordinate creditors while a plan is assessed. Restructuring changes payment promises and may require new working capital. Liquidation sells assets and distributes net proceeds by priority. The feasibility of each path depends on specific agreements and applicable law in the case.

Creditors may disagree because senior claims and junior claims gain differently from waiting. A plan that increases total enterprise value can still reduce the senior lender’s own expected recovery if it must fund new money or cede priority. Build separate cash flows for each stakeholder.

Compare economic recovery by date

Calculate expected present recovery on each feasible path, net of enforcement costs, new advances and delay. Apply probabilities to internally consistent outcomes and state the discount rate and risk treatment. Avoid subtracting expected loss again if cash flows already reflect default scenarios.

A plan may depend on owners injecting equity, retaining a key customer, replacing management or releasing collateral. Convert assumptions into conditions and milestones. Name the information needed, the deadline and the remedy if the milestone fails. Otherwise an extension can postpone recognition of loss.

Method

Decide on a workout

  1. Stabilize

    Identify imminent funding needs, independent cash controls and which essential suppliers or staff must be kept for operations to continue.

  2. Value

    Compare liquidation today with dated, conditional receipts under restructuring. Include extra cash advances and their priority.

  3. Negotiate

    Allocate recoveries across creditors, set verifiable milestones and retain fallback rights under the case terms.

Worked case

Should the bank provide rescue money?

Liquidation would pay the bank €350,000 today. A turnaround requires the bank to advance another €40,000 now. It then receives €550,000 in one year if successful and €220,000 otherwise. Use 10% for both branch cash receipts under the agreed comparison and assume 70% success.

Working

  1. Expected future receipts are 0.70 × €550,000 + 0.30 × €220,000 = €451,000. Their present value is €410,000. After the new advance, value today is €370,000.
  2. The advantage over liquidation is €20,000. The break-even success probability p solves (€220,000 + €330,000p)/1.10 − €40,000 = €350,000, giving p ≈ 63.33%.
  3. A credible revised estimate of 55% success produces (€220,000 + €181,500)/1.10 − €40,000 = €325,000, below liquidation. Evidence supporting the forecast can reverse the recommendation.

Interpretation

Demand a signed customer renewal and a dated cash-flow forecast if those determine the probability. Check whether a new-money claim is permitted and collectible under the stated priorities.

A common mistake

Comparing an undiscounted successful restructuring payment with liquidation cash available today.

How to correct it

Include unsuccessful branches, financing required to reach them, timing, and legal priority. Compare the bank’s net economic recovery on equivalent dates.

Quick check
If the probability of the €90,000 payment falls to 70%, which option has higher expected present recovery on these assumptions?

The expected future payment becomes €72,000. Discounting gives about €65,455, below €70,000 today. Feasibility, legal rights and uncertainties can still affect the decision.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

Immediate enforcement pays €120,000. A one-year extension pays €180,000 with probability 60%, otherwise €70,000. It costs €10,000 today and the discount rate is 5%.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Evaluate the extension against enforcement.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Find the approximate break-even success probability and explain the practical implication.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Expected receipts are €136,000. Present value net of cost is €136,000/1.05 − €10,000 ≈ €119,524, about €476 below immediate enforcement.

Part B 2 marks

Solve (€70,000 + €110,000p)/1.05 − €10,000 = €120,000, giving p ≈ 60.45%. The estimates are close, so small changes in timing, recovery or evidence about success can reverse the ranking.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A banker prefers restructuring because the best outcome exceeds liquidation proceeds. What probability would make that choice economically defensible?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would show the committee a break-even probability of about 59.55%. The company’s claim of a 60% recovery chance puts the bank only €952 ahead on present value. I would ask what signed customer order, confirmed new equity or sale agreement shifts that probability, and insist on milestones that preserve the liquidation fallback before treating the small modeled advantage as sufficient.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If another creditor’s priority reduces the bank’s good-state recovery from €320,000 to €290,000, does 60% still clear?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The new threshold is (€231,000 − €100,000)/(€290,000 − €100,000) ≈ 68.95%. At 60%, expected future recovery is €214,000, worth approximately €183,810 net of new funding. Immediate €200,000 liquidation has the higher value under the revised priority.
