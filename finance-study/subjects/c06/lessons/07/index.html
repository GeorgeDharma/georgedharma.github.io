Yield curve shapes

Expectations, term premiums and segmented demand can all affect the slope. An upward curve alone cannot identify which explanation dominates.

The starting point

A yield curve plots yields against time to maturity for comparable claims. A rising curve means later maturities currently carry higher quoted yields. Expectations about future short rates can affect the slope, and investors may require extra compensation for bearing long-term price risk, called a term premium.

Demand and supply at particular maturities can also matter. For example, a pension scheme trying to match long-dated obligations may value long bonds especially highly. An analyst separates these explanations using additional evidence such as policy-rate expectations, issuance and term-premium estimates, all of which carry uncertainty.

Theory and method

Nelson–Siegel yield curve

Level, slope and curvature factors · Preferred habitat and term premiums

A curve rises from 2.38% at six months to about 3.59% at ten years. Can you describe its shape without pretending it uniquely forecasts policy?

A rates desk often needs a smooth description across maturities even when only selected bonds trade. The Nelson–Siegel curve combines a long-run level, a slope effect strongest at the short end, and a middle-maturity hump or dip. Its parameters describe cross-sectional prices parsimoniously. Economic explanations, including expected policy, inflation, term premiums and investor demand for particular maturities, require further evidence. A fitted hump does not separately identify how much comes from each underlying mechanism.

How the result is built

y(t) = β₀ + β₁a(t) + β₂[a(t) − exp(−t/τ)], a(t) = [1 − exp(−t/τ)]/(t/τ)

y(t) is the model spot rate for maturity t in years, β₀ the long-run level, β₁ the short-end slope loading, β₂ the curvature coefficient, τ a positive time scale in years, a(t) the slope loading, and exp the exponential function.

  1. As maturity approaches zero, a approaches one and the curvature loading approaches zero, so the short-end limit is β₀ + β₁. At long maturities both loadings vanish and the limit is β₀.
  2. With β₀ = 3.5%, β₁ = −1.5%, β₂ = 2% and τ = two years, the short-end limit is 2% while the far end tends toward 3.5%.
  3. At t = 2, a ≈ 0.6321 and its difference from exp(−1) is roughly 0.2642. The fitted yield is 3.5 − 1.5 × 0.6321 + 2 × 0.2642 ≈ 3.08%.

When the reasoning holds

  • Fit consistent yield observations and day-count rules while excluding or modeling security-specific features. A liquidity-distorted issue can bend the estimated curve.
  • A three-factor curve is a parsimonious description. Exact pricing across all instruments or enforcing an arbitrage-free dynamic evolution requires additional modeling.
Terms in this lesson
Yield curve
The relationship between interest rates and time to maturity for a specified market and type of instrument.
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Coupon
The interest payment specified in a bond’s terms.
Forward
A contract to exchange an agreed amount at a set price on a future date.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Discount factor
The price today of one unit of currency payable at a particular future date under specified risk assumptions.
How to read the notation
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.
Basis points and percentage points
A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises by one percentage point, or 100 basis points. Its increase relative to the original rate is (5 − 4) ÷ 4 = 25%.
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.

First example

Under a deliberately simplified decomposition, a two-year yield of 4% could reflect expected short rates averaging about 3% plus a 1 percentage-point term premium. A 4% yield could instead reflect a higher expected short-rate path with a smaller premium.

Distinguish the curve pictured

A curve can display par yields, zero-coupon spot rates or forward rates. A par yield makes a hypothetical coupon issue worth face. A spot rate discounts one dated cash flow. A forward rate links two future dates through current discount factors. Shapes often rhyme, but the points have different meanings.

Construct the maturity comparison in the same currency and with similar credit, collateral and tax treatment. A three-month bill and a credit-risky ten-year corporate bond can produce a rising chart even when the underlying risk-free curve is flat.

Expectations and compensation

A longer yield reflects the compounded sequence of future short rates that would reproduce the investment, plus effects attributable to uncertainty and risk pricing. In a pure expectations model the forward rate closely reflects an expected future rate under specified assumptions. A term premium allows the two to differ.

Supply from governments and demand from insurers or pension funds can particularly affect specific maturities. Inflation expectations, real rates, policy uncertainty and balance-sheet constraints can all move nominal yields. A price chart alone rarely identifies a unique cause.

Read a change as a set of shocks

A parallel shift moves all maturities by the same number of basis points. A steepening increases the long-minus-short yield difference. It can arise from rising long rates or falling short rates, leaving different mark-to-market effects. A hump or butterfly move changes intermediate yields relative to the ends.

Compare inflation-linked yields, surveys or futures, policy announcements and issuance calendars where appropriate. These additional indicators have measurement assumptions too. Present a hypothesis as conditional evidence rather than a certainty about the future.

Method

Explain a curve movement

  1. Measure

    Identify curve definition, currency, comparable dates and maturity-specific changes. State the slope change in basis points.

  2. Infer

    Recover forward rates from spot discount factors if needed. Specify what term-premium assumption would be required to read them as actual forecasts.

  3. Test

    Select evidence that could distinguish changed short-rate expectations from shifts in long-term demand, issuance or risk premiums.

Worked case

One curve, two stories

One-year and two-year annual spot yields are 2% and 4%. Both relate to certain payments in the same currency. A policymaker asks what these prices say about the one-year rate starting a year from today.

Working

  1. The one-year forward rate equals 1.04²/1.02 − 1 ≈ 6.04%. It is a market-implied break-even reinvestment rate, conditional on the specified price conventions.
  2. A survey expecting a future short rate of 5% leaves a gap of about 1.04 percentage points. A term-premium explanation is possible, alongside measurement error or different forecast horizons.
  3. If a second curve has a 3% one-year yield and unchanged 4% two-year yield, its slope narrows to one point and forward becomes 1.04²/1.03 − 1 ≈ 5.01%.

Interpretation

A relatively small movement at the short end can materially alter a future implied period rate even when the quoted long yield remains fixed.

A common mistake

Equating every upward slope with a guaranteed policy hike.

How to correct it

Give the implied forward under the correct conventions, then state the expectations and premium assumptions needed to transform that price into a policy forecast.

Quick check
Long yields rise while short yields stay put. Which inference is justified by the curve alone?

Steepening describes an observed change in slope. Expected future rates, term premiums or bond-specific demand could contribute to the move.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

At date A the two-year yield is 3.5% and the ten-year yield is 4.0%. At date B the two-year is 2.7% and the ten-year is 3.9%.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Describe changes in level and slope.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Which holdings react to the changes and what else must be specified?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

The two-year falls 80 basis points and the ten-year falls 10. The long-minus-short spread rises from 50 to 120 basis points, a 70-basis-point steepening despite both yields falling.

Part B 2 marks

Under fixed positive cash flows lower discount rates support prices, but amounts depend on duration, maturity-specific sensitivities and other curve points. These two quotes alone do not determine an entire portfolio’s price change.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A curve rises from 2.38% at six months to about 3.59% at ten years. Can you describe its shape without pretending it uniquely forecasts policy?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would describe the rise with level, slope and curvature loadings, then distinguish the economic hypotheses. Under these parameter values the curve starts near 2% and tends toward 3.5% far out, with a moderate intermediate hump. To attribute it to future policy I would compare policy expectations, inflation instruments and issuance demand. The static curve itself prices maturity differences without uniquely explaining them.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if only the level coefficient rises 40 basis points, with every other coefficient fixed?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Every fitted spot rate rises by 40 basis points because β₀ enters each maturity with coefficient one. The original mathematical shape and maturity spreads are preserved, even though actual bond price changes will differ because their cash-flow durations differ.
