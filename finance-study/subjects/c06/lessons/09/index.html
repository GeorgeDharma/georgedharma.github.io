Callable and structured debt

A borrower may repay early, changing a bond’s effective life. Trace prepayment, securitization and priority in a simple pool of loans.

The starting point

A callable bond lets the issuer repay on specified terms before maturity. When interest rates fall, refinancing may become attractive. Investors can receive their principal just as newly available yields have also fallen. That possibility limits the price upside relative to a comparable bond without the call.

Mortgage pools can experience a similar change in timing when households prepay. A securitization gathers loan payments and assigns them to different classes. Read the payment rules in order. One class can receive principal first while another absorbs losses first, creating different risks from the same underlying pool.

Theory and method

Callable bond decomposition and backward induction

Issuer call option · Option-adjusted spread

A bond has identical promised coupons to a straight issue, but the borrower can call it after one year. How would you value that right on a rate tree?

When rates fall, continuing a high-coupon bond may be expensive for its issuer. The issuer may redeem at a specified call price instead. To the holder this is a ceiling on post-coupon continuation value at the exercise date. Start with future discounted contractual cash flows in each interest-rate state, then compare continuation and redemption state by state. Averaging the straight values first can miss exercise that occurs in only one branch.

How the result is built

Vcallable = [q min(K,Vu) + (1 − q)min(K,Vd)]/R; Voption = Vstraight − Vcallable

At the single call date, Vu and Vd are euro straight continuation values in up- and down-rate states, K is callable redemption in euros, q the pricing weight of the up state, and R the gross discount growth to that date. Prices are ex-coupon at the call node.

  1. At the call date let the straight value be €98 in the high-rate branch and €108 in the low-rate branch. At a €104 call price, repayment costs less than continuation only in the low-rate branch.
  2. Set q = 0.5 and R = 1.02. The straight issue is worth (98 + 108)/(2 × 1.02) ≈ €100.98. The callable issue is worth (98 + 104)/(2 × 1.02) ≈ €99.02.
  3. The issuer’s option is about €1.96 today, the discounted half-weighted €4 saving in the low-rate state. Adjusted spreads require consistently modeling the rate tree, exercise and discounting.

When the reasoning holds

  • The issuer optimally exercises and can finance redemption at the modeled terms. Credit constraints, call notice rules or refinancing costs can delay exercise.
  • The cash-flow values and call price use the same coupon timing and accrued-interest convention. Including a coupon on both sides can distort the choice.
Terms in this lesson
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Interest rate
The price of borrowing money or the return for lending it, quoted as a percentage over a stated period.
Principal
The amount borrowed or invested before interest is added.
Coupon
The interest payment specified in a bond’s terms.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
First example

A €100 pool finances €80 of senior claims and €20 of junior claims. Assume the junior class absorbs credit losses first and ignore timing and fees. A €12 loss leaves senior principal intact and reduces junior principal to €8.

An issuer’s option changes the holder’s claim

An otherwise comparable callable bond can be viewed as a straight bond minus a right held by the issuer to buy it back on specified dates and terms. When refinancing is attractive, the issuer can shorten the investor’s cash-flow stream. High coupons promised for many years may be cut short.

Yield to call assumes repayment on a specified call date. Yield to maturity assumes no earlier redemption. Analysts often compare yields across permissible redemption dates, but these scenario returns do not predict the actual date or value the option across all possible future rate paths.

Amortization and prepayment in pools

In a mortgage pool, scheduled principal gradually returns capital. Additional prepayments or refinancing can accelerate it. Faster prepayment returns money sooner when prevailing reinvestment yields may be low. Slower prepayment can extend duration when rates rise. Contract-specific caps and household behaviour matter.

A security backed by the pool can allocate interest, principal and losses differently across classes. Separate a principal payment made by a solvent borrower from a credit loss. Both can reduce pool balance, yet they have opposite implications for the cash received by investors.

Apply a stated waterfall exactly

Begin with available collections, subtract fees where required, then allocate interest and principal according to the case. Sequential-pay senior claims may receive principal first, while a junior class absorbs losses first. Different structures can use entirely different rules or protective triggers.

Track opening and closing balances by class after each event. A senior class can be exposed to prepayment and reinvestment even if its credit protection is substantial. Tranching reallocates uncertainty among investors. It does not create additional underlying collateral cash.

Method

Trace a structured claim

  1. Contract

    Identify who may redeem, the exercise price, permitted dates and any prepayment or distribution trigger.

  2. Waterfall

    Separate interest, returning principal and realized losses, then apply priorities and caps in the specified order.

  3. Stress

    Vary prepayment speed and credit losses separately and jointly. Calculate changes in both cash timing and loss allocation.

Worked case

A mortgage pool returns and loses principal

A €100m pool funds €75m senior notes and €25m junior notes. The case sends all principal collections to seniors first and all credit losses to juniors first. Ignore fees and interest. Borrowers prepay €20m, followed by a €30m credit loss on the remaining pool.

Working

  1. Prepayment sends €20m cash to senior investors, leaving €55m senior and €25m junior balances against an €80m pool. This scheduled distribution is money received, not economic principal loss.
  2. The first €25m of credit loss wipes out the junior claim. The remaining €5m reduces the outstanding senior balance from €55m to €50m. Remaining collateral is €50m.
  3. Senior investors have already received €20m and now hold a €50m claim, together €70m versus their initial €75m. They bear €5m loss and earlier-than-expected reinvestment exposure.

Interpretation

Changing only the ordering of classes can change timing and who absorbs initial credit losses, even when total cash generated by the underlying loans is identical.

A common mistake

Treating a €20m prepayment as a €20m credit loss because outstanding principal falls in both cases.

How to correct it

Record cash received as well as changes in principal. A credit write-down reduces the claim without delivering matching cash to the investor.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A callable bond costs €102 just after a coupon date. It pays €5 after one year and can be called then for €100, or pay €5 in year one and €105 in year two if held to final maturity.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Compute the one-year return if called and identify the relevant cash flows.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Explain why a large fall in market rates might fail to give this bond the gain of a straight two-year bond.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Year-one cash is €5 + €100 = €105. Return is 105/102 − 1 ≈ 2.94%. A call eliminates the year-two cash flow.

Part B 2 marks

The issuer is more likely to refinance and return €100 early. The holder loses a valuable future coupon and receives funds in a lower-rate environment. The right to call can cap price appreciation.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A bond has identical promised coupons to a straight issue, but the borrower can call it after one year. How would you value that right on a rate tree?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would value the straight claim on a calibrated interest-rate tree, compare each exercise-date continuation with the call price, and then work backward. Here the borrower calls only in the €108 state, reducing that payoff to €104. The callable discount to the straight issue is €1.96. I would also check call protection, financing friction and coupon conventions before attributing a yield difference to credit risk.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If refinancing incurs €3 of transaction cost paid by the issuer in the low-rate state, what happens to the holder’s payoff and the issuer’s saving there?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The issuer compares €104 redemption plus €3 external cost with €108 continuation, so calling still saves €1. The holder still receives €104. The €3 goes to the service provider. Exercise remains optimal, but the issuer’s private net option benefit is smaller.
