Convexity

Bond prices are curved functions of yields. A second-order term improves a duration estimate when the yield move is larger.

The starting point

Duration draws a tangent to the relationship between a bond’s price and yield. As the yield moves farther, the true price follows a curve. Convexity measures that curvature. Ordinary fixed-coupon bonds with fixed repayment dates generally have positive convexity.

With yields expressed as decimals, the second-order approximation adds half the convexity times the squared yield change to the duration estimate. The squared term is positive for either direction. A bond that can be called early may behave differently if lower yields encourage its issuer to redeem it.

Theory and method

Bond convexity and the barbell–bullet comparison

Second-order price sensitivity · Positive convexity

Can two default-free portfolios have equal initial value and duration but respond differently to a sizeable parallel yield move?

A bullet portfolio pays at one middle date. A barbell spreads present value across an early and a late payment. If both have the same average payment date they can match first-order duration. The long tail of the barbell places more weight on squared time, which increases convexity under a parallel spot-rate shift. Relative performance consequently depends on the size and pattern of the rate move, and on what the investor initially pays for each cash-flow pattern.

How the result is built

C = Σwₜt²; ΔP/P ≈ −DΔs + ½C(Δs)²

C is price convexity in years squared for the stated continuous parallel spot-rate shift, wₜ is cash flow t’s initial share of present value, D = Σwₜt is duration in years, Δs is the decimal annual spot shift and P is initial price.

  1. Start each portfolio at €100 present value. A bullet has 100% present weight at year three. A barbell has half at year one and half at year five. Both have duration three.
  2. For the bullet C = 3² = 9. For the barbell C = 0.5 × 1² + 0.5 × 5² = 13. The second derivative follows by differentiating exp(−tΔs) twice.
  3. For a 1-percentage-point shock in either direction, the barbell’s additional local convexity contribution is 0.5 × (13 − 9) × 0.01² = 0.0002, or about two basis points of return.

When the reasoning holds

  • The comparison holds time, default, options and cash-flow schedules fixed. Differing credit quality or calls can dominate this curvature difference.
  • Both positions undergo the same parallel proportional discount-factor change exp(−tΔs). A curve twist can favor either portfolio and invalidate the duration match.

Terms in this lesson
Convexity
Curvature. In fixed income it describes how a bond’s price sensitivity to yield changes as the yield moves.
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Coupon
The interest payment specified in a bond’s terms.
Duration
A measure used to estimate how strongly a bond price responds to a small interest-rate change.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Derivative
A contract whose value depends on something else, such as a currency, an interest rate or a share price.
How to read the notation
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.
A change · Δ
ΔS commonly means the new asset price less the old price. A move from €50 to €53 gives ΔS = €3. In options, delta can also name a sensitivity. A delta of 0.6 means a €1 rise in the underlying predicts about a €0.60 rise in the option, for a small move.
Subscripts · S₀ and S₁
A small index names the time or item. If S₀ = €40 and S₁ = €42, the price moves from €40 at time 0 to €42 at time 1. A subscript i can label a company instead of a date.

All notation examples

First example

Suppose modified duration is 5 and convexity is 30. A one percentage-point yield rise gives −5 × 0.01 + 0.5 × 30 × 0.01² = −0.04985. The approximate loss is 4.985%, slightly smaller than duration’s 5%.

Look beyond the tangent

Price as a function of a conventional bond yield slopes downward and usually bends upward when cash flows are fixed and positive. Two equal yield changes in opposite directions therefore have unequal effects. A fall adds slightly more price than duration alone predicts and a rise subtracts slightly less.

Mathematically, modified duration is minus the first price derivative divided by price. Convexity is the second derivative divided by price. In the second-order approximation the proportional change is −duration × Δy + ½ × convexity × (Δy)². Omitting the half doubles the curvature adjustment.

Estimate from three revaluations

Some instruments lack a convenient fixed cash-flow derivation. Reprice with rates down and up by the same small decimal amount h. Effective duration equals (Pdown − Pup)/(2P₀h). Effective convexity equals (Pdown + Pup − 2P₀)/(P₀h²). The curve-shift and valuation model used become part of the estimate.

Very small shocks can show numerical rounding error. Very large shocks capture nonlocal behaviour. A callable security can exhibit negative effective convexity as refinancing caps price gains when rates fall. Changing assumed exercise behaviour can alter both sensitivities.

Use convexity in an economic comparison

At the same initial price and duration, higher positive convexity can produce better modeled price performance for a symmetric yield move either way. The extra curvature may already be compensated through a lower yield or different risk exposures. Consequently, selecting the most convex bond alone is insufficient.

Compare comparable credit quality, liquidity, curve shape and option assumptions. A comparison for an instantaneous parallel shock differs from a total holding-period return, which also reflects coupons, reinvestment and passing time.

Method

Build the second-order estimate

  1. Measure

    Use comparable full prices with time held fixed and parallel shocks specified in decimals. Distinguish modeled effective quantities from fixed-cash-flow analytical ones.

  2. Apply

    Calculate the signed duration term first. Compute convexity separately using the square of the yield change and then add the two terms.

  3. Verify

    For a larger shock reprice the bond and inspect whether options or curve shape make the local approximation unstable.

Worked case

Curvature measured from prices

A bond prices at €100 today, €104.20 after a one-percentage-point yield fall and €96.20 after a one-percentage-point rise. Assume the revaluations hold all else fixed and use a decimal shock h = 0.01.

Working

  1. Effective duration = (104.20 − 96.20)/(2 × 100 × 0.01) = 4. Effective convexity = (104.20 + 96.20 − 200)/(100 × 0.01²) = 40.
  2. For a two-percentage-point yield rise, the first-order estimate is −8%. The convexity correction is 0.5 × 40 × 0.02² = 0.008, adding 0.8 percentage point and producing approximately −7.2%.
  3. For a two-point fall, duration gives +8% and curvature again adds 0.8 point, producing +8.8%. These are extrapolations from the local model.

Interpretation

The asymmetry provides a quick check on signs. A fitted negative convexity would subtract from both first-order estimates under the same convention.

At the board

See the curvature that duration misses

A bond pays a single 100 in two years. Its price at annual effective yield y is 100/(1+y)². At 5% it is 90.7029 and its modified duration is 2/1.05 = 1.9048 years. The duration estimate holds the slope fixed.

Annual yieldExact priceDuration-only estimate from 5%
4%92.455690.7029 × (1 + 1.9048 × 0.01) = 92.4306
5%90.702990.7029
6%88.999690.7029 × (1 − 1.9048 × 0.01) = 88.9753

The exact prices lie above the straight-line estimate on both sides. For this ordinary fixed payment, price is convex in yield. A one-point fall gains about 1.7527 price units, while a one-point rise loses about 1.7033. Quoting “duration 1.9” without the initial price and the yield change would not give a cash loss.

A common mistake

Reversing the sign of the convexity term when the yield shock is negative.

How to correct it

Square the rate change before applying convexity. The sign of this correction depends on convexity itself.

Quick check
For the same bond, what approximate gain follows from a one percentage-point yield fall?

Duration contributes +5%. Convexity contributes +0.015 percentage point, lifting the approximation to +5.015%.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

Two genuinely comparable bond positions have duration 5. Position A has convexity 30, B has convexity 70. A common yield shock of +1.5 percentage points hits both.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate the approximate proportional changes.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Would that difference by itself establish greater expected total return for B?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

For A, −5 × 0.015 + 0.5 × 30 × 0.015² = −7.1625%. For B, −7.5% + 0.7875% = −6.7125%. B loses 0.45 percentage point less.

Part B 2 marks

No. Initial yields, expected rate changes, coupon income, horizon, option behaviour and transaction costs also affect total return. The comparison conditions on one instantaneous shock.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Can two default-free portfolios have equal initial value and duration but respond differently to a sizeable parallel yield move?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would match the first derivative, then inspect the timing dispersion. The year-one and year-five barbell has the same duration as the year-three bullet but convexity 13 versus 9. Conditional on an instantaneous parallel shock it performs slightly better in either direction at these starting prices. I would compare carry, curve-shape exposure and transaction costs before claiming a superior holding-period investment.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if only the five-year segment sells off by one percentage point and the other dates do not move?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Only half the barbell’s initial present value moves. Its exact loss per €100 is €50 × [1 − exp(−5 × 0.01)] ≈ €2.44. The bullet has no cash at that point and remains unchanged under the stated isolated spot shock.
