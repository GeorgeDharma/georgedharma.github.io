Forward rates

Infer the rate between two future dates from discount factors. A market-implied forward rate can contain a term premium.

The starting point

A forward interest rate is a rate for an interval that starts in the future but is implied by prices available today. Match a direct investment to year two with a one-year investment combined with a pre-agreed reinvestment for the second year.

With annual compounding, the one-year forward growth factor from year one to year two equals the one-year discount factor divided by the two-year discount factor. The relation expresses consistent prices today. The short rate actually available next year may differ because expectations change and investors require compensation for risk.

Theory and method

Forward rate agreement settlement

FRA advance settlement · No-arbitrage forward rate

If a borrower’s forward rate agreement fixes 4% but the six-month reference resets to 6%, why is the immediate cash payment smaller than the interest difference?

A treasurer must borrow €2m for six months. Its variable loan actually pays interest at period end. The FRA protecting it may pay the compensation at the start of the period when the rate is fixed. Receiving the full future interest difference early would overcompensate the company because that cash can earn interest during the loan period. Discounting under the contract’s settlement convention makes the early receipt grow to the required offset at the end.

How the result is built

A = N(L − K)δ/(1 + Lδ)

A is the borrower-hedger’s euro receipt at reset under this standard simple advance settlement, N euro notional, L annual reference fixing, K annual contracted rate, and δ accrual fraction in years.

  1. The borrower owes end interest NLδ. Its target reference cost is NKδ, so a matched end-date hedge would pay N(L − K)δ.
  2. For N €2m, L 6%, K 4% and δ 0.5, the end-date difference is €20,000. Divide by 1 + 0.06 × 0.5 = 1.03 to obtain €19,417.48 at reset.
  3. Investing that settlement at the fixing produces €20,000 at the end. Actual reference interest €60,000 less the matured hedge receipt leaves €40,000, the 4% target for six months.

When the reasoning holds

  • The reset-date rule uses the stated reference and accrual convention. A contract settling in arrears or using a different discount reference has different cash timing.
  • The loan notional, dates and fixing must match and reinvestment at the specified rate must be feasible. Loan-specific credit margins or spreads remain separate.

Terms in this lesson
Forward
A contract to exchange an agreed amount at a set price on a future date.
Discount factor
The price today of one unit of currency payable at a particular future date under specified risk assumptions.
Interest rate
The price of borrowing money or the return for lending it, quoted as a percentage over a stated period.
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
How to read the notation
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.
Subscripts · S₀ and S₁
A small index names the time or item. If S₀ = €40 and S₁ = €42, the price moves from €40 at time 0 to €42 at time 1. A subscript i can label a company instead of a date.
A change · Δ
ΔS commonly means the new asset price less the old price. A move from €50 to €53 gives ΔS = €3. In options, delta can also name a sensitivity. A delta of 0.6 means a €1 rise in the underlying predicts about a €0.60 rise in the option, for a small move.
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.

All notation examples

First example

If €1 due in one year costs €0.96 and €1 due in two years costs €0.90, the second-year forward rate is 0.96/0.90 − 1 = 6.67%. The ratio exceeds one because the later payment costs less today.

A forward rate equates two investments

Suppose two routes deliver money at date two. Buy a two-year zero now, or buy a one-year zero and agree today on the rate earned from year one to year two. When the future rate is contractually fixed and both routes share risk and funding conventions, their values must be consistent.

With unit discount factors d₁ and d₂, lending one unit at year one and receiving 1 + f at year two has present value −d₁ + (1 + f)d₂. Setting it to zero gives 1 + f = d₁/d₂. This derivation identifies exactly which ratio to take.

State the accrual period and convention

For simple interest over a fraction δ of a year the growth factor is 1 + fδ. Solve f = (d(start)/d(end) − 1)/δ. For annual compounding over multiple whole years, take the appropriate root of the total growth factor. Quoting an unannualized six-month return as an annual rate changes the contract.

An overnight-indexed discount curve and a separate floating-rate projection curve can be used in particular contracts. A basic single-curve exercise assumes the same lending opportunity and convention throughout. A market quote linked to another reference rate may include basis and credit differences.

Implied price versus forecast

A forward summarizes prices available today. Investors may require compensation for committing to long positions, or value hedging at certain dates. That term premium can separate the forward rate from a forecast of the future realized short rate. A rate locked today can differ from what proves cheapest in hindsight.

A firm fixing future borrowing obtains budget certainty at the contracted rate. A favorable future movement for borrowers then produces an opportunity cost, while an adverse movement is offset. The value of the hedge is its effect across scenarios for the particular exposure.

Method

Recover a future growth factor

  1. Identify

    Place both endpoints and day fraction on a timeline and choose discount factors for the same currency and credit or collateral basis.

  2. Equate

    Set the present values of starting and ending cash equal, derive total growth, then annualize using the quoted convention.

  3. Compare

    Distinguish a no-arbitrage quote, a statistical forecast and the outcome a client wants to protect. Report what happens if future rates differ.

Worked case

A future six-month borrowing cost

A firm expects to borrow €2m six months from now for six months. The six- and twelve-month discount factors are 0.98 and 0.95. Assume the loan can be perfectly fixed against this curve, with no spread, and uses simple annual interest with δ = 0.5.

Working

  1. The six-month growth factor is 0.98/0.95 ≈ 1.031579. The annualized forward rate is 0.031579/0.5 ≈ 6.316%.
  2. Fixed interest for the period is €2m × 0.06316 × 0.5 ≈ €63,158. Its calculation applies the half-year fraction once.
  3. If the future unhedged annual rate proves 8%, its interest would have been €80,000, €16,842 higher. If it proves 4%, unhedged interest would be €40,000 and the lock costs roughly €23,158 in hindsight.

Interpretation

If the company’s own credit margin widens, fixing only the reference curve leaves the borrowing spread unprotected.

A common mistake

Treating a 3.16% six-month growth factor as both the annual rate and the six-month interest amount.

How to correct it

State the period first. A six-month effective increase of 3.16% corresponds to roughly 6.32% per year on a simple half-year basis.

Quick check
With respective one- and two-year factors of 0.98 and 0.95, which forward rate follows?

The one-year interval starts after year one. Divide 0.98 by 0.95 and subtract 1. Directly subtracting the factors ignores the amount being invested.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

Discount factors at years one and three are 0.96 and 0.84. A forward investment lasts both intervening years.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate total forward growth and the constant annual compound rate.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Why would dividing 14.286% by two give a different answer and when could that convention be used?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Growth is 0.96/0.84 ≈ 1.142857, a 14.286% two-year gain. Annual compound rate is √1.142857 − 1 ≈ 6.90%.

Part B 2 marks

The division produces a simple annual quote of 7.143% without interest on interest. It is valid only if that is the stipulated two-year simple-interest convention.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

If a borrower’s forward rate agreement fixes 4% but the six-month reference resets to 6%, why is the immediate cash payment smaller than the interest difference?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would identify the settlement date before quoting a gain. The borrower needs €20,000 to offset excess interest at the end, but this FRA pays at reset. Discounting by 1.03 produces €19,417.48, which grows to the required offset if reinvested at the fixing. If the borrower’s own spread changes or it borrows a different amount, the underlying cash cost can still deviate from the target.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if the borrower can only reinvest the settlement at 2% annual simple over the six months?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Its receipt grows to about €19,611.65 rather than €20,000, leaving actual net reference cost about €40,388.35. This is a reinvestment basis effect from possessing compensation early. Document the contract’s settlement and the treasury’s achievable investment rate.
