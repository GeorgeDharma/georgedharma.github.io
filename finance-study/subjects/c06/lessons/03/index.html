The spot curve

Each maturity has a discount factor. Start from simple instruments and solve successively for later factors using known cash flows.

The starting point

Payments at different dates can trade at different rates. A discount factor gives the price today of one certain unit of currency at one specific date. A spot curve is the collection of rates, or equivalent factors, for single payments at each maturity.

Bootstrapping builds the curve in order. Start with a price that isolates the first date. For the next bond, subtract the value of payments at dates already solved for. The remainder determines the factor for its last payment. The instruments must have consistent conventions, credit quality and currency for the exercise to describe one curve.

Theory and method

Par coupon identity from discount factors

Annuity factor · Bootstrapped spot curve

How can a curve that prices every dated payment imply a different par coupon at every maturity?

A dealer constructing a par bond asks what coupon would make an issuer’s promise worth exactly face value today. The discounted principal is already fixed by the discount factor at maturity. Coupons must supply the difference between that present principal and the face price. A euro of coupon paid at every date has its own present value, the coupon annuity. Dividing the required difference by that annuity gives the fair annual coupon rate under the chosen schedule.

How the result is built

cpar = (1 − dₙ)/Σδᵢdᵢ

cpar is the annual par coupon per unit of face, dᵢ is the discount factor for coupon date i, δᵢ its accrual year fraction, n is final payment index, and Σδᵢdᵢ is the present value of the unit-rate coupon stream.

  1. Write the unit-face par price as one = discounted maturity principal plus discounted coupons, or 1 = dₙ + cpar Σδᵢdᵢ.
  2. For annual discount factors 0.97, 0.93 and 0.88, the three-year coupon stream has annuity 2.78 and the principal supplies 0.88 of the target price.
  3. The remaining 0.12 requires coupon rate 0.12/2.78 ≈ 4.3165%. A directly observed 4.3165% par coupon and earlier factors could instead be used to solve for d₃.

When the reasoning holds

  • Full price and cash-flow dates must be aligned at the valuation date. Accrued interest can move an observed clean quote away from the correct equation.
  • One curve prices all cash flows in this comparison. A risky or callable issuer may need extra discounting or state-dependent treatment.

Terms in this lesson
Discount factor
The price today of one unit of currency payable at a particular future date under specified risk assumptions.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
Coupon
The interest payment specified in a bond’s terms.
Settlement
The final transfer of cash or securities needed to complete a transaction.
How to read the notation
Subscripts · S₀ and S₁
A small index names the time or item. If S₀ = €40 and S₁ = €42, the price moves from €40 at time 0 to €42 at time 1. A subscript i can label a company instead of a date.

All notation examples

First example

Suppose the one-year factor is 0.97. A two-year bond pays €4 after one year and €104 after two. Its price is €99.56. Year one contributes €3.88. Dividing (€99.56 − €3.88) by €104 gives a two-year factor of 0.92.

Use dated prices as building blocks

A discount factor d(t) gives the present cost of a unit paid at date t for a stated credit, collateral and currency convention. A yield on a coupon-bearing bond combines several dates. Two different coupon bonds of the same final maturity can therefore have different yields on the same underlying non-flat curve.

Once discount factors are available, multiply each cash flow by its matching factor and sum. With annual compounding a spot yield for date t equals [1/d(t)] raised to 1/t minus one. A par yield is the coupon rate making a hypothetical bond worth face value. It answers a different question.

Strip one known payment at a time

Bootstrapping uses short instruments to establish early factors. In the next bond, discount all cash flows except the last with factors already identified. Divide the remaining price by the final cash flow. Repeat across successively longer instruments. The procedure requires enough independent price information to separate dates.

In real curves, coupons occur at dates without a directly quoted instrument. An interpolation rule fills the gaps and affects the result. Check settlement dates, accrued interest, compounding and instrument quality before interpreting a puzzling factor as an arbitrage.

Test a new price against the curve

Price a withheld bond using the extracted factors and compare the model price with the executable full price. A discrepancy can arise from rounding, liquidity, a call option, different day count or different credit. Check these features before proposing to buy the cheaper and sell the richer stream.

A larger discount factor at a later date can imply a negative forward rate. That can occur under a negative-rate environment. Strictly positive state-consistent discount factors are fundamental. Monotonic decline requires further assumptions about rates or costless cash storage.

Method

Bootstrap a term structure

  1. Order

    Group instruments by actual cash-flow dates and rank from simplest short-dated payment to more complex later schedules.

  2. Strip

    Subtract known earlier present values from the full observed price. Solve the remaining equation for the new date’s factor.

  3. Validate

    Reprice the calibration bonds and an independent comparable instrument. State the conventions and dates where a rule supplied missing information.

Worked case

A three-date strip

Use annual dates and face €100. A one-year zero costs €97. A two-year 4% coupon bond costs €100.60, and a three-year 5% coupon bond costs €101.90. Assume comparable credit and no calls.

Working

  1. The first factor is 0.97. The two-year bond gives 4 × 0.97 + 104d₂ = 100.60, so d₂ = 0.93.
  2. The three-year bond gives 5 × 0.97 + 5 × 0.93 + 105d₃ = 101.90. The known coupons are worth €9.50 and d₃ = 0.88.
  3. A three-year zero paying €100 consequently prices at €88. A claim paying €20 in year two and €80 in year three costs €18.60 + €70.40 = €89.

Interpretation

Using the three-year spot rate to discount both pieces would discard observable information about the earlier payment. Identical final maturity is insufficient to identify identical cash flows.

A common mistake

Applying the published par-yield curve directly as if each figure were a zero-coupon rate.

How to correct it

Determine the curve definition and infer or obtain suitable discount factors before pricing individual dated payments.

Quick check
Using these factors, what is the value of €20 in one year plus €50 in two years?

Value each date separately. €20 × 0.97 + €50 × 0.92 = €19.40 + €46 = €65.40.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A one-year zero with face €100 costs €96. A two-year 5% annual bond with face €100 costs €99.30.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Derive the second-year discount factor and its annual spot yield.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Price a second two-year issuer-matched bond with €8 annual coupons and €100 principal.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

d₂ = (99.30 − 5 × 0.96)/105 = 0.90. The annual compound spot rate is √(1/0.90) − 1 ≈ 5.41%.

Part B 2 marks

8 × 0.96 + 108 × 0.90 = €104.88. Different coupons alter the mix of one- and two-year cash flows even with the same final maturity.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How can a curve that prices every dated payment imply a different par coupon at every maturity?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would start from a unit of face. Discounted principal contributes 0.88 and the sequence of coupons must cover the missing 0.12. The unit annuity is 2.78, giving a 4.3165% annual par coupon. If a screen shows only par yields, I would bootstrap or obtain discount factors before valuing an irregular coupon stream. A per-maturity par quote alone does not specify the price of each individual date.

Interviewer’s follow-up

A bond with the same final date pays an annual 6% coupon on €100 face. What is its model price?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Principal contributes €88, and coupons contribute €6 × 2.78 = €16.68. The value is €104.68 on this same curve. Its premium is also €100 × (6% − 4.3165%) × 2.78, apart from rounding. Both approaches discount exactly the same promised amounts.
