Prices and yield to maturity

Discount promised cash flows to find a bond’s price. Yield to maturity is the single rate reproducing that price under the quoted convention.

The starting point

A bond’s price is today’s value of its future payments. Discounting means dividing each cash flow by a growth factor that reflects how long one must wait. For annual payments and annual compound yield y, divide a year-t payment by (1 + y) raised to the power t.

Yield to maturity is the single rate that makes those discounted promised payments equal the observed price. Higher required yields reduce the price of a fixed schedule. A borrower can default and interim coupons can earn a different reinvestment rate, so the yield alone does not determine an investor’s eventual compound return.

Theory and method

Horizon return and the break-even sale yield

Holding-period yield · Rolldown analysis

An analyst says buying a two-year bond yielding 5% guarantees a 5% return over the coming year. Which future yield would actually deliver that outcome?

A manager may need to sell after the first annual coupon, while the issuer’s second payment is still outstanding. At that date the manager receives both the coupon and the market price of the remaining cash flow. A useful trade test works backwards from the client’s one-year target to the price needed at sale, then converts that price into a break-even remaining yield. A curve assumption can supply a forecast, but the market will determine the actual sale yield.

How the result is built

HPR = [C + (F + C)/(1 + y₁)]/P₀ − 1

HPR is one-year holding-period return on a two-year annual coupon bond, C each euro coupon, F euro face value, P₀ initial full euro price, and y₁ the annual yield on the remaining one-year bond immediately after the first coupon.

  1. Buy for €100 a bond paying €5 after one year and €105 after two. To produce 5% HPR it must provide €105 at the first horizon, including the first €5 coupon.
  2. The ex-coupon sale price required is €100. The remaining claim pays €105 a year later, so its break-even yield is 105/100 − 1 = 5%.
  3. A target HPR of 7% needs a €102 sale after coupon. The remaining yield threshold becomes 105/102 − 1 ≈ 2.94%. Lower or equal yields meet that target if other terms hold.

When the reasoning holds

  • The first coupon and sale occur at the same horizon, avoiding an additional reinvestment assumption. Cash at other dates must be accumulated consistently.
  • This is a fixed, performing bond with no early redemption or change in contractual cash. Credit events can alter both sale price and coupon received.

How to read the notation
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.

All notation examples

First example

A one-year bond promises €105. At a price of €100 its yield is €105/€100 − 1 = 5%. If its price falls to €98 with the same promise, yield rises to €105/€98 − 1 ≈ 7.14%.

A yield is an internal rate on promises

Set the amount paid today equal to the discounted stream of future contractual amounts. The rate solving that equation is yield to maturity under the chosen compounding convention. For an ordinary positive stream, increasing the rate reduces every future present value, so there is at most one economically relevant solution.

Coupon rate measures promised interest relative to face. Current yield divides annual coupon by today’s price, omitting any eventual price movement toward redemption. Yield to maturity includes that redemption but still uses the promised schedule even when the borrower may fail.

Why realized returns can differ

A person selling before maturity receives an uncertain sale price. A holder to maturity also faces coupon reinvestment and default risk. The yield reproduces the present value of the promises today. To obtain the same compound rate on end wealth, interim coupons must be reinvested appropriately and the promised payments must occur.

A zero-coupon bond has no interim coupon to reinvest. Its nominal compound rate to maturity can be locked in if bought at a known full price, held to redemption, and paid as promised. Currency, tax and purchasing-power effects can still change a particular investor’s outcome.

Compare compounding conventions

A quarterly nominal quote of 8% means 2% per quarter under the specified convention. Its effective annual rate is 1.02 to the fourth power minus one. A yield quoted under semiannual compounding must be converted before direct comparison with an effective annual yield.

Between coupon dates, the valuation requires time fractions consistent with the settlement convention. The clean price excludes earned coupon interest. Treating it as the full purchase cost can overstate the yield. State whether a calculation is on a coupon date.

Method

Estimate and verify a yield

  1. Timeline

    Enter full price today as an outflow and each promised receipt as an inflow at its actual date.

  2. Solve

    Choose a period rate, calculate present value, and adjust the rate until price matches. Convert period rate into the stated quotation.

  3. Reinvest

    For a realized horizon return, separately project actual coupons, reinvestment earnings and sale or redemption proceeds.

Worked case

A reinvestment-rate test

A two-year €1,000 bond pays a €60 coupon at the end of each year and returns principal at year two. Its annual compound yield at purchase on a coupon date is 5%. The first coupon can later be reinvested for only one year at 2%.

Working

  1. Price is €60/1.05 + €1,060/1.05² ≈ €1,018.59. The premium over face reflects a coupon rate above the market-required yield.
  2. Terminal wealth with 2% coupon reinvestment equals €60 × 1.02 + €1,060 = €1,121.20. Realized compound annual growth is approximately (1,121.20/1,018.59) raised to one half minus one, or 4.92%.
  3. If reinvestment instead earns 5%, terminal wealth is €1,123 and the annual compound outcome is exactly the original 5% before rounding.

Interpretation

A lower reinvestment rate can reduce terminal wealth even when the issuer meets every promise. A projected yield comparison and a client’s realized experience can therefore disagree.

A common mistake

Using the €60 coupon divided by €1,018.59 as yield to maturity.

How to correct it

That calculation gives current income yield. Check total discounted payments including the €1,000 redemption under the assumed date and compounding method.

What is the annual compound yield on a two-year bond costing €100 that pays only €121 at maturity?

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A two-year zero-coupon note costs €81 and promises €100 at maturity. After one year its market price is €85.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Compute its original annual compound yield and its one-year realized return if sold.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Find the yield available at the year-one price to a new investor holding for the remaining year.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Original yield is √(100/81) − 1 = 11.11%. A sale at €85 after one year produces 85/81 − 1 ≈ 4.94%.

Part B 2 marks

100/85 − 1 ≈ 17.65%, assuming payment as promised. The original buyer’s first-year return does not determine the remaining investor’s yield.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

An analyst says buying a two-year bond yielding 5% guarantees a 5% return over the coming year. Which future yield would actually deliver that outcome?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would map the client’s sale horizon. The first €5 coupon is fixed, but the €105 due a year later still has to be priced in the market. A 5% horizon result needs a €100 ex-coupon sale, which corresponds to a 5% remaining yield. I would show the result over a range of benchmark and credit spreads and distinguish forecast rolldown from a contractual promise.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If the client insists on 5% over one year with no dependence on the sale price, what type of position addresses the source of risk?

Compare with the follow-up answer

A performing one-year zero purchased at a price that locks the required annual growth aligns the final payment with the horizon. An enforceable forward sale for the required price can also address the bond-price risk, but introduces pricing, settlement and counterparty considerations.
