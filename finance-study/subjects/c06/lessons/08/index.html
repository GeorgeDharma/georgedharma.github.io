Credit and liquidity

A corporate spread can compensate for default loss, risk, liquidity or contractual features. Compare bonds with similar currency and maturity.

The starting point

Two bonds can promise similar payments yet have different yields. The yield spread is the difference between their quoted yields. A lender can demand compensation for possible default, distress during bad times, difficulty selling quickly, and embedded rights such as early redemption.

To investigate the spread, match currency, maturity, seniority and contract features. Consider trading frequency and the gap between buying and selling prices as evidence about liquidity. A yield spread is measured per year. Treating it directly as a probability of default also overlooks how much investors might recover.

Theory and method

Reduced-form default intensity and the credit triangle

Credit spread approximation · Recovery sensitivity

A five-year credit spread is 180 basis points. How would you turn it into a scenario for cumulative default, and what would that figure mean?

A bondholder receives extra spread while the issuer survives and loses a fraction of principal at default. In a stripped-down credit model the annual premium leg roughly balances a hazard rate times loss severity. This converts a market quote into a pricing-implied intensity under chosen recovery assumptions. It differs from a default frequency estimated from companies’ histories. Liquidity, optionality, discounting conventions and the precise recovery payment rule can all affect an actual traded spread.

How the result is built

λ ≈ s/(1 − R); Q(T) = 1 − exp(−λT)

s is the annual credit spread expressed as a decimal, R is the assumed fraction of exposure recovered, λ is a constant annual risk-neutral default intensity, T is years, and Q(T) is the model-implied probability of default by T.

  1. Over a small surviving interval dt, the model assigns probability about λdt to default and loss fraction 1 − R. Equate first-order premium sdt with the corresponding priced loss.
  2. Take s = 0.018 and R = 0.40. Their implied intensity is about 0.03 per year. Continuous survival over five years is exp(−0.03 × 5) = 0.8607.
  3. Cumulative default is approximately 13.93%. If an analyst instead forecasts 2% annual physical intensity, its five-year cumulative probability is about 9.52% under that separate forecast.

When the reasoning holds

  • Use a comparable credit spread under an explicit simplified continuous model. Bond-specific liquidity, tax, risk premiums or options may make the triangle inaccurate.
  • Intensity and recovery are held constant here. With a term structure, integrate the time-varying hazard and specify recovery timing and the premium leg consistently.
Terms in this lesson
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Probability of default (PD)
The chance that the borrower meets the specified default definition over the stated horizon.
Percentage point
The difference between two percentages. A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises one percentage point.
Basis point
One hundredth of a percentage point. A rise from 4.00% to 4.01% is one basis point.
Coupon
The interest payment specified in a bond’s terms.
How to read the notation
Basis points and percentage points
A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises by one percentage point, or 100 basis points. Its increase relative to the original rate is (5 − 4) ÷ 4 = 25%.
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.

All notation examples

First example

A five-year corporate bond yields 5.20% and a comparable government bond 3.50%. The spread is 1.70 percentage points, or 170 basis points. An infrequently traded corporate issue may carry a liquidity component even if the issuer’s repayment outlook is stable.

What sits inside a quoted spread

A nominal spread compares yields to maturity. Differences in coupon timing, curve shape, liquidity or embedded options can affect it even for bonds with similar final dates. A constant zero-volatility spread discounts each payment on a spot curve plus a constant adjustment. An option-adjusted spread additionally relies on an option and rate model.

Spreads are prices for bearing future uncertainty. Expected default loss is one component, but investors can demand more for losses concentrated in economically bad states. Trading costs and balance-sheet scarcity add further effects. Treating spread in basis points as default probability ignores even basic recovery arithmetic.

Default timing and recovery

A missed coupon, restructuring or bankruptcy can trigger different recoveries. A model must state how much arrives, when and with which legal rank. Recovery as a percentage of face value differs from recovery as a percentage of the immediately preceding market value. A first-stage simple bond model can assume end-of-year recovery.

A distressed market price might fall because projected survival declines, because recovery expectations fall, or because buyers demand a higher return. The same observed spread cannot uniquely identify all these ingredients. Use scenarios and comparable securities to bound inferences.

Assess liquidity separately

Look at executable bid and ask prices, market depth, trade frequency, issuance size and repo availability. Quoted midpoint yields can remain stale. In stress, both expected defaults and the cost of selling can increase together, making a simple before-and-after attribution ambiguous.

Match currency, duration, sector, issuer priority and optionality before comparing two issues. A government reference can itself have a liquidity benefit or other features. A residual spread after one modeled adjustment is still a residual until its other determinants are evaluated.

Method

Diagnose a spread

  1. Match

    Choose comparable benchmark cash flows and state whether the statistic is a nominal yield spread or a model-based measure.

  2. Model

    Write survival and default payouts, including recovery timing. Assess the pricing impact under explicitly stated compensation assumptions.

  3. Investigate

    Compare bid–ask costs and issue terms, and stress default and liquidity jointly. State what cannot be inferred from the quote alone.

Worked case

A one-year credit break-even

A one-year default-free investment turns €100 into €103. For a credit claim costing €100 assume default probability 2%, recovery €40 on default, no extra risk compensation and all receipts at year-end. What promise would match the safe expected proceeds?

Working

  1. Solve 0.98 × promised amount + 0.02 × €40 = €103. Promised amount is €102.20/0.98 ≈ €104.2857. Corresponding yield is about 4.29%.
  2. The actuarial spread over 3% is about 1.29 points. A market yield of 5% would give a nominal spread of two points and an extra 0.71 point over the modeled promise.
  3. If recovery instead falls to €20, the actuarial promised payment rises to (€103 − €0.40)/0.98 ≈ €104.69. This changes the unexplained portion without any change in trading liquidity.

Interpretation

The expectation uses assumed physical probabilities and zero additional risk premium. A market-implied risk-neutral default probability answers a different question.

A common mistake

Labelling the entire residual above expected loss a liquidity premium.

How to correct it

Check risk pricing, recovery assumptions, options, benchmark quality and trading evidence before attributing a residual to one cause.

Quick check
Two equally senior bonds from one issuer have similar maturities. One rarely trades and yields 0.4 point more. What hypothesis merits testing first?

The shared issuer and similar contract reduce some differences. Trading costs and available market depth can help explain the observed spread.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A one-year bond costs €100 and promises €107. The safe return is 2%. With zero extra priced risk under a simplified model, default pays €32 instead of €107.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Infer the break-even default probability.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Why would this inference change if lenders demanded a premium for bad-state losses?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

107(1 − p) + 32p = 102 gives 75p = 5 and p ≈ 6.67%. The face yield spread of five points is not the same as this probability.

Part B 2 marks

They could require expected receipts above €102. Part of the €107 promise would compensate risk bearing, and a lower physical default probability could be consistent with the observed price and recovery.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A five-year credit spread is 180 basis points. How would you turn it into a scenario for cumulative default, and what would that figure mean?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would give a conditional pricing number, then label it clearly. At 40% recovery the spread implies a risk-neutral intensity near 3%, leading to five-year default of about 13.93%. That is sensitive to recovery and the spread decomposition. To report an actual default forecast I would use borrower fundamentals, historical transition evidence and scenario estimates, then explain how its physical result relates to market pricing.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What happens if you assume 60% recovery while keeping the spread fixed?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Loss severity falls to 40%. The inferred pricing intensity rises to 0.018/0.40 = 4.5% and the five-year priced default probability rises to 1 − exp(−0.225) ≈ 20.15%. Lower loss per default requires more implied defaults to sustain the same spread under this model.
