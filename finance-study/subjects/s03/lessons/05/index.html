Cash conversion

Compare earnings with collections, inventory purchases and recurring investment. A high margin can coexist with persistently weak cash generation.

The starting point

A sale can enter earnings before cash arrives. A company can also buy inventory that it will expense only when sold. Cash conversion asks how much reported earning power actually becomes cash that can be reinvested, used to repay debt or distributed.

Start with an operating cash earnings measure. Subtract cash taxes, necessary operating investment and increases in working capital under a clearly defined convention. Visit several periods. A seasonal inventory build can reverse, while a pattern of late collections may consume cash every year.

Theory and method

Cash conversion cycle

Days sales outstanding · Days inventory outstanding · Days payable outstanding

A distributor tells its buyer that reducing the cash conversion cycle by five days saves five days of revenue. When is that shortcut wrong?

Imagine buying stock for €60 and selling it for €100. One fewer day of unpaid selling prices releases a different amount of cash from one fewer day of inventory at cost. The cash conversion cycle measures the time between operating cash outlay and customer collection using three operating clocks. To turn days into money, apply the correct sales, cost or purchase flow to each clock. Businesses with identical cycle lengths can require different cash investment.

How the result is built

CCC = DSO + DIO − DPO. Cash released ≈ −ΔDSO × sales/day − ΔDIO × cost/day + ΔDPO × purchases/day

CCC is cycle days. DSO, DIO and DPO are average collection, inventory and payment days. Δ is new minus old days. Sales, cost of goods sold and supplier purchases are the corresponding euro flows per day.

  1. With annual credit sales €36.5m on 365 days, daily sales are €100,000. Annual cost and eligible purchases of €21.9m each yield €60,000 per day on their respective bases.
  2. Cutting DSO by five days releases approximately €500,000. Cutting DIO by five days releases €300,000. Extending DPO by five days also releases €300,000 if purchase terms permit it.
  3. Check whether the release is a one-time balance change or a repeatable annual saving. At a 6% carrying cost, permanently releasing €500,000 saves about €30,000 annual funding cost.

When the reasoning holds

  • Flows and average balances must represent a fairly stable period. A post-holiday closing snapshot can distort all three apparent day counts.
  • Extending payable days may sacrifice supplier discounts or continuity. Estimate those consequences before claiming an economic gain.

Terms in this lesson
Working capital
Money tied up in day-to-day operations. For a deal, agree which receivables, stock and payables count.
Receivable
Money a customer owes for a sale already made.
Payable
Money a business owes a supplier for goods or services already received.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
EBITDA
Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. Check how the seller calculates and adjusts this measure.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
First example

A company earns €6m before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization. It pays €1m cash tax, spends €2m replacing equipment and ties up €1.5m in extra inventory and receivables net of payables. Simplified cash available before financing is €1.5m.

Why profit can arrive before cash

Accrual accounting records economic activity when earned or incurred. Cash follows the payment terms. Growth can therefore increase profit and simultaneously absorb cash as a business stockpiles goods, pays suppliers and waits for customers. A business can succeed commercially while needing more funding to sustain that success.

A simple operating working-capital measure adds receivables and inventory, then subtracts operating payables. An increase uses cash. If customers pay faster, an otherwise identical business can release cash without creating a new sale. If the business postpones supplier payments, the cash benefit can reverse sharply later.

Where capital expenditure belongs

EBITDA excludes depreciation. Depreciation spreads past investment across accounting periods, but worn machinery eventually needs cash replacement. Estimate maintenance requirements using operating evidence as well as recent spend. A seller can temporarily improve cash flow by postponing maintenance until after closing.

A simplified cash bridge starts from EBITDA and subtracts cash taxes, capital spending and increases in working capital under the stated definition. Other cash items may be relevant in an actual business. Define the intended measure before comparing companies that treat leases, interest or acquisitions differently.

Look through a flattering reporting date

Compare monthly or quarterly conversion across a complete seasonal cycle. An inventory build before peak season can reverse. Overdue stock that fails to sell may require a write-down and fresh funding. Review aging, collection and sale evidence to distinguish a timing effect from economic loss.

Assess whether cash improvement came from operations or financing. Selling receivables might move collections forward in exchange for fees or recourse, changing risk and timing. A rise in one period’s bank balance says little about the repeatable amount available to service debt.

Method

Build a cash-conversion bridge

  1. State the start and finish

    Define the operating earnings measure and whether the output is before financing, before or after growth spending, and available to whom. Keep the same period throughout.

  2. Reconcile operating balances

    Calculate receivables, inventories and payables at both dates. Check noncash movements and acquired businesses before treating every balance change as operating cash.

  3. Stress available cash

    Separate sustainable spending from deferred spending. Recalculate for slower collections or normalized supplier terms and compare the resulting cash with debt or investment commitments.

Worked case

A distributor showing healthy profit

EBITDA is €8m, cash tax €1.3m and essential equipment spend €2m. Receivables increase €1.4m, stock rises €0.8m and supplier payables rise €0.5m. Assume these are the only operating balance changes and all arose in cash-based operations.

Working

  1. Working capital increased €1.4m + €0.8m − €0.5m = €1.7m. The increase in payables financed part of the cash tied up elsewhere.
  2. Cash after essential investment and tax, before financing, is €8m − €1.3m − €2m − €1.7m = €3m. Relative to EBITDA this conversion is 37.5%.
  3. If €0.5m of supplier credit is unusually overdue and must be paid now, comparable cash is €2.5m. Test payment history and contractual dates before calling the supplier financing sustainable.

Interpretation

The bridge says where cash went. Whether more inventory is sound growth or unwanted goods requires physical and commercial evidence.

A common mistake

Subtracting the entire closing receivable balance from this year’s earnings instead of the movement attributable to this year.

How to correct it

Cash conversion concerns the amount newly tied up. Compare opening with closing on a consistent perimeter and distinguish collections of preexisting receivables.

Quick check
Under the same simplified cash bridge, operating cash earnings are €5m, cash taxes €1m, maintenance investment €1m and working capital falls €0.5m. What cash is available before financing?

A fall in working capital releases cash. Add €0.5m after deducting taxes and investment: €5m − €1m − €1m + €0.5m = €3.5m.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

EBITDA is €4m, cash tax €0.5m and maintenance investment €0.9m. Receivables fall €0.4m, inventory rises €0.6m, and payables are unchanged. Assume no other cash effects.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate simplified cash available before financing.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    A machine replacement of €0.3m was delayed to January. Assess the sustainable comparison.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Working capital rises €0.2m because the €0.4m released from collections only partly finances inventory. Available cash is €4m − €0.5m − €0.9m − €0.2m = €2.4m.

Part B 3 marks

This year’s actual cash stays €2.4m. If €0.3m is necessary annual replacement spending excluded from €0.9m, normalized cash is €2.1m. Inspect the asset schedule, service records and expected payment date.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A distributor tells its buyer that reducing the cash conversion cycle by five days saves five days of revenue. When is that shortcut wrong?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would ask which of the three clocks moved. With €100,000 daily credit sales, five days faster collection brings forward €500,000 once. Inventory and supplier payments turn at €60,000 a day, so the same five days releases €300,000 there. I would then test whether the change persists, whether the seller temporarily stretched payments, and what the operational trade-off costs.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Collections improve five days but customers receive a permanent 1% discount on €36.5m annual sales. At a 6% funding cost, does financing alone justify it?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The release saves about €30,000 a year in financing, while the sales discount costs €365,000 annually before other effects. The financing case is short by about €335,000 each year. Lower defaults or higher volume could add benefits, but would need independent estimates.
