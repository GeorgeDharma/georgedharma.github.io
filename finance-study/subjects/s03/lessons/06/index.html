Normal working capital

Estimate the level required to operate through a normal cycle. Check seasonality, growth and supplier-payment changes before choosing a target level.

The starting point

A buyer expects enough operating assets to keep trading on day one. Typical operating working capital includes customer receivables and inventory less supplier payables. The sale contract specifies the exact items and an agreed normal amount, often called the target or peg.

A year-end snapshot can mislead when inventory peaks before a holiday season. Review monthly history, recent growth and payment practices. If a seller postpones supplier payments, the buyer may inherit unusually large bills even though the bank account at signing looks healthy.

Theory and method

Working-capital peg from operating days

Normalized net working capital · Like-for-like closing comparison

A seasonal business doubled its sales to customers who pay in advance. Should its working-capital target also double?

A retailer delivering machinery to public agencies may wait sixty days for payment, while private customers pay deposits before shipment. Sales can grow without a matching increase in receivables when the new customers pay differently. Build a normal closing requirement from the customer and supplier mix, seasonal inventory and agreed accounts. This estimates what working assets the buyer actually needs to continue operating. Compare the result with delivered balances on exactly the same definitions.

How the result is built

Peg = Σⱼ(salesⱼ/day × normal DSOⱼ) + cost/day × normal DIO − purchases/day × normal DPO

j is a customer group, DSO its normal collection days, DIO days of inventory, and DPO supplier payment days. The peg is normalized operating working capital in euros under the case’s defined accounts.

  1. Credit customers generate €60,000 daily sales and normally pay in 40 days, producing €2.40m receivables. Cash customers contribute zero to normal receivables for sales settled immediately.
  2. Cost is €40,000 a day and normal inventory 30 days, implying €1.20m stock. Purchases of €40,000 daily with 20 payment days imply €0.80m supplier balances.
  3. The defined peg is €2.40m + €1.20m − €0.80m = €2.80m. At €2.50m delivered, a euro-for-euro formula reduces seller proceeds by €0.30m.

When the reasoning holds

  • Contract definitions in this example exclude cash customer advances as balances. If outstanding customer deposits are included, add them with the required sign to both peg and actual.
  • The operating-day inputs must match the closing season and normal growth mix. Expansion requiring extra stock or different supplier terms changes the peg.
Terms in this lesson
Working capital
Money tied up in day-to-day operations. For a deal, agree which receivables, stock and payables count.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Receivable
Money a customer owes for a sale already made.
Payable
Money a business owes a supplier for goods or services already received.
Benchmark
A reference chosen in advance for comparison, such as a market index or an equal-weight portfolio.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
First example

Two firms agree on €2.4m normal working capital with a euro-for-euro adjustment. At closing, eligible receivables are €2.1m, inventory €1.5m and payables €1.4m. Actual working capital is €2.2m. The price drops €0.2m under these terms.

What the buyer expects to receive

An operating-business price normally assumes the business has the stock, receivables and supplier support it needs to trade. A working-capital target, often called a peg, specifies a benchmark. The parties agree whether the purchase price moves euro for euro when defined closing balances differ from that target.

Think of a shop delivered with empty shelves and all customers having already paid. Future buyers must finance restocking even if the historic profit figure was correct. The target protects the economic package on which the operating valuation was agreed. An excessively high target can also transfer value unfairly to the buyer.

Select an operating period that makes sense

Average monthly balances reduce sensitivity to one unusual date, but can be misleading for a fast-growing or seasonal company. A wholesaler may always build inventory before winter. Delivering a summer average at a winter closing could leave a shortfall even if the annual average was measured accurately.

Relate balances to activity. Receivable days can be approximated by average receivables divided by credit sales times the number of days, using comparable periods. Inventory and supplier days require appropriate cost or purchase bases. A stable percentage of sales is a shortcut that needs verification if product or payment mix changed.

Consistent definitions protect both sides

Exclude the same items from historical targets and actual closing balances. Items treated as debt-like elsewhere should be checked for duplicate inclusion. A one-time old tax balance may be economically different from normal supplier credit, but a surprise reclassification at closing can bias the adjustment.

Review collection, stock and supplier behavior near the measurement date. Faster discount collections, delayed stock purchases or stretched supplier payments can make closing appear attractive for cash while impairing operations. The contract and the economic interpretation should both be visible in the analysis.

Method

Estimate and challenge a peg

  1. Normalize the history

    Build monthly balances for the agreed perimeter. Flag changes in accounting, growth, seasonal peaks and unusual payment actions. Explain any judgmental exclusions explicitly.

  2. Choose the comparison

    Compare comparable months, average cycles and activity-scaled balances. Select a benchmark matched to the closing season and ongoing business scale, with sensitivity to plausible alternatives.

  3. Calculate the transfer

    Apply the actual contract formula and signs. Show exactly whose payment increases, compare the result with ordinary operating needs, and check any overlap with net debt.

Worked case

A growing winter wholesaler

Each of the last two comparable November closings required operating working capital equal to 20% of annual sales at that operating scale. Current annual sales are €30m. A full-year monthly average from the smaller prior business is €4.6m. The contract uses an agreed €6m peg and actual closing working capital is €5.1m.

Working

  1. The activity-scaled comparable month indicates 20% × €30m = €6m. This supports the agreed peg if selling terms and seasonal patterns remained comparable.
  2. Actual working capital is €0.9m below €6m. With a euro-for-euro clause, equity proceeds fall €0.9m. Against the €4.6m historic average they would instead rise €0.5m.
  3. The €1.4m difference between these two outcomes makes the benchmark economically material. Check whether current sales growth is backed by comparable inventory and customer credit needs.

Interpretation

A target mechanically scaled by sales can be too high if customers begin paying in advance. Compare days and operating behaviour before selecting it.

A common mistake

Adjusting actual working capital for distorted balances while leaving the same distortion in the historical target, or vice versa.

How to correct it

Rebuild both series using one definition and comparable accounting principles. Present any contractual exception separately and quantify its effect.

Quick check
A seasonal seller typically holds €9m of working capital at a September closing but averages €6m annually. Which benchmark deserves particular attention?

The buyer needs sufficient assets for the closing season. Comparable monthly data help distinguish normal seasonal funding from a genuine shortfall.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

Usual September working capital was €4m on €20m sales. The business now has €25m sales with unchanged mix and terms. Actual closing working capital is €4.7m.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Use a proportionally scaled September peg and calculate the adjustment.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    What if new cash-in-advance customers account for all growth?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

The historic ratio is 20%, implying €5m for the current September. Actual is €0.3m below the peg, so the buyer pays €0.3m less under a euro-for-euro formula.

Part B 3 marks

Those extra sales may require little customer credit. Build separate receivable, inventory and payable drivers for old and new customers. The €5m peg may overstate delivered operating needs.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A seasonal business doubled its sales to customers who pay in advance. Should its working-capital target also double?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would split sales by collection terms and size inventories and suppliers separately. In this case cash customers add sales but no receivables, while the credit customers need €2.4m. After stock and suppliers the target is €2.8m. I would test the relevant month and the buyer’s operating capacity before accepting an annual sales multiple as the purchase agreement benchmark.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Credit customers now pay in 50 days with the same sales. What price effect follows if the parties update the peg and actual delivered working capital stays €2.50m?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Ten additional days at €60,000 create €0.60m more normal receivables. The updated peg is €3.40m, giving a €0.90m delivered shortfall. Seller proceeds fall another €0.60m versus the original comparison. A signed fixed peg would require an agreed amendment before this economic change altered the contract.
