Comparing external vulnerability

Use comparable source vintages, units and definitions. Compare a commodity exporter with a diversified economy before making a policy judgment.

The starting point

An economy can need foreign currency for imports, interest or debt principal even when domestic output is strong. Reserves measured against external debt due soon indicate one aspect of resilience. Verify whether short-term debt is defined by original or remaining maturity.

Compare data for the same date and convert balances into consistent currencies. Examine whether reserves are usable and how export receipts would respond to the same shock. A concentrated commodity exporter may face a different cash squeeze from a diversified exporter with the same headline ratio.

Theory and method

Guidotti–Greenspan reserve adequacy rule

Short-term external debt coverage · Remaining maturity

A country reports enough reserves for all debt originally issued short term. Why can it still fail a one-year reserve-coverage test?

An international bond issued ten years ago can require dollars for its full principal next week. A one-year reserve benchmark asks how much external debt falls due in the next year if access to new market finance stops. The remaining-maturity measure catches obligations reaching their final year regardless of when they were issued. A usable reserve measure should also disclose pledged or inaccessible assets. Additional pressure can come from current-account deficits, resident outflows and payment dates across institutions.

How the result is built

Coverage = usable foreign reserves / external debt due within one year by remaining maturity

Numerator and denominator use the same foreign-currency value and date. Remaining-maturity debt includes originally short debt now due plus the next year's principal on originally long-term claims, without overlap. One times is the conventional benchmark.

  1. A country reports $30bn gross reserves, $6bn pledged and $15bn originally short-term liabilities due this year. Another $12bn of longer-issued debt matures this year.
  2. Usable reserves are $24bn and total remaining-maturity obligations $27bn. Coverage is 24/27 = 0.889 times. Gross reserves over the narrower original debt would show 30/15 = 2.0 times.
  3. In a no-rollover case debt alone leaves a $3bn shortage before current-account needs. A one-times reading would still not prove that the sovereign can direct reserves to every private borrower.

When the reasoning holds

  • The benchmark imagines a period of lost market access. Actual rollover, committed official credit and active policy responses can change funding needs.
  • Use a consistent residency definition and access assumptions. Official reserve assets may not be promptly usable against privately owed maturities.
Terms in this lesson
Principal
The amount borrowed or invested before interest is added.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
First example

Country X has $24bn usable reserves and $20bn external debt due within a year, giving coverage of 1.2 times. Country Y has $18bn and $15bn, also 1.2. A simultaneous fall in X's main export price could still make its future dollar inflows more vulnerable.

Debt, reserves and the current account

The current account records flows of goods, services and income with the rest of the world plus current transfers. An external financing requirement can reflect both a current-account deficit and maturing debt. A country with a small current deficit may still face a large foreign-currency maturity wall.

External debt is defined by the residence of creditor and debtor rather than by currency. Domestic-currency debt held by nonresidents can be external debt. Dollar debt between two residents need not be. Separately examine the actual currencies needed for payment.

How much reserve cover is really available?

Gross official reserves can include resources committed as collateral, tied to particular official operations, or needed to maintain policy functions. A specified usable-reserve measure should disclose each deduction. Official reserves are also not the same as cash owned by private borrowers with international debt.

Debt at original short maturity excludes longer-term contracts now close to repayment. Remaining maturity captures payments due within the chosen horizon, whatever their original term. Select the denominator that matches the liquidity question and reconcile any statistical definition that differs.

Comparability beyond a single ratio

Reserves divided by next-year debt, months of imports and foreign-currency banking liabilities each describe different pressures. An economy dependent on one export may lose foreign receipts when world prices fall. A diversified exporter with the same initial cover can have a different downside.

Compare the same coverage, period and source vintage. Distinguish gross from net measures and people or firms with access to reserves from those without it. Use scenarios for rollover and export receipts instead of treating a universal ratio cutoff as conclusive.

Method

Constructing an external funding table

  1. Harmonize observations

    Record source date, reporting units, residency perimeter, instrument coverage and original or remaining maturity definition.

  2. Calculate needs and resources

    Identify maturing external claims and net current-account funding needs, then compare with usable liquid resources and committed inflows without double counting.

  3. Stress the composition

    Vary export earnings, refinancing and residents' capital movements coherently, considering which borrowers and authorities can access currency.

Worked case

Two countries, two reserve numbers

Country X shows $33bn gross reserves, with $5bn already pledged, and $20bn of external debt originally issued short-term due this year. Another $8bn of older long-term debt also matures this year. Assume all amounts are in dollars.

Working

  1. Using the stated usable measure gives $28bn reserves. Remaining-maturity debt totals $28bn. Their coverage ratio is 1.0, substantially below the naive gross/original ratio of 33/20 = 1.65.
  2. A $4bn current-account deficit and no new financing would raise the simple total external funding need to $32bn, leaving a $4bn gap versus usable reserves if complete mobilization were feasible.
  3. If investors roll half of all maturing debt, they provide $14bn and the remaining gross need after rollover is $18bn. Reserves cover that arithmetic need, subject to actual ownership, timing and minimum policy balances.

Interpretation

A drop in cocoa export prices could widen the current-account deficit and coincide with reduced rollover. A joint scenario gives more information than the initial reserve ratio.

A common mistake

Assuming all recorded reserves can pay any resident firm's debt immediately ignores legal ownership, exchange restrictions and other uses.

How to correct it

Keep the national aggregate and actual access to currency separate and report how usable resources were defined.

Quick check
One dataset counts only newly issued short-term loans, while another includes long-term bonds maturing next month. What must an analyst do before comparing near-term repayment pressure?

A bond issued ten years ago still requires cash next month. Remaining maturity aligns the measure with the near-term funding question, while a common date makes the stocks comparable.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

Country Y has $24bn reserves of which $6bn are pledged. External debt due within a year includes $10bn of original short-term debt and $8bn of maturing longer-term bonds. The current-account deficit is $3bn.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate usable-reserve coverage of remaining-maturity debt and the unfinanced gap if nothing rolls.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    If a published indicator instead divides gross reserves by short-term debt at original maturity, calculate it and explain the difference.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Usable reserves are $18bn and debt due is $18bn, giving 1.0 times coverage. Adding the $3bn deficit gives $21bn need and a $3bn gap before new financing.

Part B 3 marks

It reports 24/10 = 2.4 times. It includes pledged assets and excludes $8bn of long-term debt now due, so it answers a different coverage question.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A country reports enough reserves for all debt originally issued short term. Why can it still fail a one-year reserve-coverage test?

Compare with a spoken answer

The one-year test concerns dollars required over the next year, whatever the debt's original term. Here $12bn of old bonds mature alongside $15bn originally short debt, while $6bn of the stated reserves is pledged. The relevant coverage is about 0.89, with a $3bn debt-only gap in a no-rollover scenario. I would then list the other external flows and who can use reserves.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Half the $27bn debt rolls and there is a $5bn current-account deficit. What cushion remains if all $24bn usable reserves really can be mobilized?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Renewal supplies $13.5bn. Remaining need is $13.5bn of maturities plus the $5bn deficit, or $18.5bn. Usable reserves exceed that by $5.5bn before other flows and any policy reserve floor.
