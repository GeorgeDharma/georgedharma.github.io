Maturity and rollover

Gross financing need includes maturing obligations as well as new deficits. A concentrated maturity can cause trouble even if long-run debt ratios decline.

The starting point

A government may have a manageable debt stock yet face a month in which many bonds must be repaid together. Rollover risk is the risk that new funding will be unavailable, too short or too expensive when the old debt matures.

Construct a maturity calendar. In a simple cash budget, gross financing need includes maturing principal plus the overall cash deficit, which already includes interest. Deduct usable cash balances and other identified sources to find the amount still needing market financing. Avoid counting interest twice.

Theory and method

Gross financing needs and rollover break-even

Maturity-wall analysis · Treasury funding gap

How high must creditors' rollover participation be for a treasury to avoid an emergency auction?

A ministry has bonds maturing, an overall budget deficit, a finite cash buffer and committed outside funding. Some existing bondholders may reinvest. Solving for the minimum fraction that must roll gives the treasury a decision threshold it can compare with binding commitments and prior auction behavior. If the required fraction exceeds one, even full renewal cannot close the gap. Keeping a cash floor intact prevents the analysis from spending the same operating buffer needed for salaries.

How the result is built

u* = (M + B − C − N)/M

M is maturing principal, B the overall cash deficit including interest, C cash available above the required minimum, N committed new non-rollover funding, and u* the rollover fraction needed to close the period. All money terms use the same currency.

  1. Maturities are 40bn and deficit 6bn, so gross need is 46bn. Usable cash is 8bn, committed new funding 10bn.
  2. Required renewal is 46 − 8 − 10 = 28bn. Divide by 40bn maturing to find a break-even rollover of 70%.
  3. At actual renewal of 55%, existing creditors supply 22bn. A 6bn residual remains after cash and new commitments, despite the treasury still having operating cash below its protected floor.

When the reasoning holds

  • Renewed funds and new sources must settle prior to the relevant maturities. A quarter-wide surplus can conceal an earlier missed payment.
  • The deficit definition already includes interest. Adding interest again or counting expected renewal as committed funding twice overstates the requirement or understates the gap.
Terms in this lesson
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Principal
The amount borrowed or invested before interest is added.
First example

A treasury has €18bn of maturing bonds and a €4bn overall deficit. Gross financing need is €22bn. With €5bn of usable reserves, it must find €17bn from new borrowing or other sources in this simplified case.

Gross financing need and net borrowing

Net borrowing can look modest when new bonds replace old principal. Gross financing need asks how much cash must be found when obligations fall due, including maturing principal and current deficit under the specified definition. A large short-term stock can force frequent returns to the market.

If using an overall cash deficit, it already includes interest. If starting instead from a primary deficit, add interest and principal separately, plus any stated non-budget funding items. Cash balances and committed credit lines reduce the amount still to raise, not the original maturing obligations.

Rollover and maturity transformation

Average maturity is a useful but incomplete summary. A debt book can contain many long bonds yet still have a dangerous maturity wall tomorrow. Examine buckets by month, instrument, currency and holder, along with which investors are likely or obliged to renew.

Issuing longer debt can reduce rollover frequency but lock in a higher term premium. Prefunding increases cash and near-term resilience while carrying funding costs and potentially exposing the cash reserve to currency or custodian risk.

Liquidity can change solvency

A government with a credible long-run fiscal plan may face a self-reinforcing run if short-term creditors expect others to exit. A sharp yield increase raises future interest costs, and forced short borrowing can create another near-term wall.

Conversely, repeated refinancing may postpone recognition of an unsustainable primary position. Assess both the dates of cash needs and whether the implied future debt path can be serviced at plausible rates. A large opening cash balance buys time. It need not repair a structural deficit.

Method

Constructing a rollover calendar

  1. Identify cash due

    List principal and interest by date and currency, then identify non-interest deficit or surplus and any earmarked payments.

  2. Match available sources

    Separate committed resources, usable cash above the operating minimum, expected rollover and genuinely new issuance.

  3. Test the residual gap

    Apply a lower rollover fraction or later settlement date and calculate the first time cash falls short.

Worked case

A bond auction comes up short

This year's maturing domestic principal is 24bn, overall budget deficit 4bn including interest, and the treasury holds 7bn cash but must preserve a 2bn operating floor. Domestic lenders will renew 75% of the maturing amount.

Working

  1. Gross financing need is 24 + 4 = 28bn. Usable cash is 7 − 2 = 5bn. Renewal provides 18bn if it occurs on time.
  2. The remaining requirement for new sources is 28 − 5 − 18 = 5bn. Counting the full 7bn cash would violate the treasury's own minimum.
  3. If renewal falls to 50%, it brings 12bn and the new financing gap rises to 11bn. A longer-term maturity extension on part of the 24bn could reduce the current cash burden if creditors agree.

Interpretation

The maturity extension can lower today's gross financing need without reducing face debt. Future cash and present-value costs still have to be compared.

A common mistake

Confusing a rollover rate of 75% with a 75% increase in debt treats replacement of old principal as wholly new borrowing.

How to correct it

Follow the cash raised and principal retired separately, and reconcile opening debt, net issuance and closing debt.

Quick check
A treasury has 12bn principal maturing and a 3bn overall cash deficit that already includes 1bn interest. Before using reserves, what is its gross financing need?

Add the 12bn principal maturity and 3bn overall deficit. Adding the 1bn interest again would double-count a payment already in the deficit.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

The treasury has 18bn principal due, a 2bn primary deficit, 3bn interest payments and 4bn usable cash. Investors indicate they will refinance two thirds of maturing principal.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate gross financing need and remaining funding required after cash and expected renewal.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    If actual renewal is only one third, recompute and explain why a falling projected five-year debt ratio does not remove the concern.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Need is 18 + 2 + 3 = 23bn. Expected refinancing is 12bn. After 4bn cash, 7bn remains to be raised.

Part B 3 marks

Renewal is 6bn and the gap 13bn. Dated principal must still settle now. An eventual improvement cannot supply immediate cash unless it restores enough reliable funding.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How high must creditors' rollover participation be for a treasury to avoid an emergency auction?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

The required threshold is 70%. Gross needs are 46bn, and cash plus committed non-rollover funds cover 18bn. The other 28bn must come from 40bn of maturities. A 55% renewal raises only 22bn and leaves 6bn to raise. I would focus on binding participation by each maturity date and currency before assuming the threshold will be reached.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If the treasury insists on keeping another 4bn cash untouched, what participation is now necessary?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Usable cash falls from 8bn to 4bn. The renewal requirement is 46 − 4 − 10 = 32bn. Dividing by the same 40bn maturity raises the break-even rate to 80%.
