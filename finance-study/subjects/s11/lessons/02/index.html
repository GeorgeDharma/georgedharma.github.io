Debt dynamics

Roll debt forward using interest, growth, the primary balance and other adjustments. State whether the balance is measured as a surplus or deficit.

The starting point

A government's debt ratio compares its debt stock with one year's economic output. Interest raises the debt numerator, economic growth raises the GDP denominator, and a primary surplus provides cash before interest to reduce borrowing. A primary deficit does the opposite.

With no valuation changes, multiply the opening debt ratio by one plus the effective nominal interest rate, divided by one plus nominal GDP growth. Then subtract the primary surplus expressed as a share of this year's GDP. Use rates and growth measured on the same nominal basis.

Theory and method

Sovereign debt dynamics and the debt-stabilizing primary balance

Interest–growth differential · Snowball effect

Can a country run a primary deficit while keeping its debt ratio stable?

A growing tax base can make inherited debt smaller relative to national income even as interest accrues. If nominal GDP grows faster than the effective interest charged on existing debt, this denominator effect can allow a limited primary deficit at a stable debt ratio. When interest exceeds growth, the same calculation can require a surplus. The distinction depends on rates applied to the whole inherited stock, the definition of the primary balance and any additional debt recognition.

How the result is built

bₜ = [(1+i)/(1+g)]bₜ₋₁ − pₜ + aₜ; p* = [(i−g)/(1+g)]bₜ₋₁ + aₜ

b is debt divided by GDP, i effective nominal interest, g nominal GDP growth, p current primary surplus/GDP, a debt-raising adjustment/current GDP, and p* the primary surplus that stabilizes b. Rates and ratios as fractions.

  1. Write current debt as prior debt plus interest minus the primary surplus and plus other adjustments. Divide every term by current GDP.
  2. Starting b = 0.60 with i = 3% and g = 5% gives inherited ratio 0.60 × 1.03/1.05 = 0.58857.
  3. With no other adjustments, a primary deficit of 1.1429% of current GDP restores 60%. Recognition of liabilities worth 2% of GDP instead requires a 0.8571% surplus.

When the reasoning holds

  • Use nominal interest with nominal GDP growth. Real growth alone distorts the effective interest–growth comparison when prices change.
  • Effective interest on existing debt can differ from today's auction yield. Repricing speed and currency structure matter for a multiyear forecast.
Terms in this lesson
Primary balance
Government revenue minus non-interest spending under the sign convention used in this course.
Interest rate
The price of borrowing money or the return for lending it, quoted as a percentage over a stated period.
Revenue
Amount earned by selling goods or services. The customer may pay later.
Probability of default (PD)
The chance that the borrower meets the specified default definition over the stated horizon.
Liability
Something owed to another party, such as a loan or an unpaid supplier bill.
Percentage point
The difference between two percentages. A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises one percentage point.
First example

Start with debt equal to 60% of GDP. At 5% interest and 2% nominal growth, the inherited ratio becomes 60 × 1.05 ÷ 1.02 = 61.76%. A primary surplus of 2% of current GDP reduces the closing ratio to about 59.76%.

The government's accounting identity

Start a year with a nominal debt stock. Interest adds to it if unpaid from other resources. A primary surplus means revenues exceed non-interest spending, providing cash to cover interest or reduce debt. A primary deficit increases borrowing. Other transactions can change the stock independently.

Dividing by current GDP gives debt ratio this year equal to prior ratio times (1 + effective nominal interest)/(1 + nominal GDP growth), minus this year's primary surplus ratio, plus stock–flow adjustments. The surplus and adjustments must use the same current-GDP denominator.

The interest–growth differential

If the interest rate exceeds nominal growth, inherited debt rises relative to GDP in the absence of a primary surplus. The exact surplus needed to stabilize the ratio is prior debt ratio times (interest − growth)/(1 + growth) plus any debt-raising adjustment.

The common approximation using interest minus growth ignores the growth denominator. It may work for small rates but can mislead in volatile inflation or recession. Today's market yield differs from the average effective interest on existing debt until liabilities reprice.

Solvency, feasibility and uncertainty

A mechanically stabilizing surplus is useful but is not a policy forecast. Compare it with historical fiscal capacity, politically feasible adjustment, the effect of taxes and spending on output, and the maturity structure. A government can meet a long-run ratio target and still face an imminent cash crisis.

A present-value budget constraint links inherited obligations with future primary balances under assumptions about borrowing and terminal debt. It does not imply that every country with a particular debt ratio faces the same probability of default.

Method

Calculating a stabilizing balance

  1. Rebuild inherited debt

    Apply the effective interest and nominal GDP growth factor to the opening ratio, retaining sufficient decimal precision.

  2. Reconcile the primary position

    Deduct a surplus or add a deficit measured over current GDP, and separately include specified valuation or recognition adjustments.

  3. Solve for policy

    Set closing ratio equal to the chosen target and solve for the surplus. Check feasibility and the currencies and maturities that could upset the path.

Worked case

A recession and an unexpected liability

Debt starts at 75% of GDP. Effective nominal interest is 6%, nominal GDP shrinks 2%, and the state recognizes a liability equal to 1.5% of current GDP. A proposed primary surplus equals 3% of current GDP.

Working

  1. Inherited debt becomes 75 × 1.06/0.98 = 81.122% of current GDP. Contraction makes the denominator smaller at the same time interest raises the numerator.
  2. After the new liability and primary surplus, the closing ratio is 81.122 + 1.5 − 3 = 79.622%, up about 4.62 percentage points.
  3. To hold the original 75% ratio in these circumstances requires a primary surplus of 81.122 + 1.5 − 75 = 7.622% of current GDP.

Interpretation

The 7.622% result describes the accounting requirement under fixed assumptions. If an aggressive consolidation deepens the downturn, the growth assumption and required surplus can change.

A common mistake

Using real GDP growth with a nominal interest rate treats inflation inconsistently and can create an artificial debt explosion.

How to correct it

Use nominal growth with nominal effective interest, or convert both to compatible real terms and adjust the rest of the identity consistently.

Quick check
Opening debt is 50% of GDP. Interest is 4%, nominal growth is 4%, and the government runs a primary deficit of 1% of current GDP. Without other adjustments, where does the ratio end?

Equal interest and growth leave the inherited debt ratio at 50%. A primary deficit adds one percentage point, giving 51%.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

Debt is 80% of GDP. Effective interest is 5%, nominal growth 3%, and a debt-raising adjustment equals 0.5% of current GDP. The primary deficit is 1% of current GDP.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate next year's ratio.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Calculate the primary surplus that would stabilize debt at 80% and explain why the quoted yield on a new bond need not replace 5%.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Inherited debt is 80 × 1.05/1.03 = 81.553%. Add 0.5 and the 1.0 deficit to obtain 83.053% of GDP.

Part B 3 marks

Required surplus is 81.553 + 0.5 − 80 = 2.053% of GDP. The effective interest applies across outstanding contracts. A new issue's yield initially affects only new or repricing liabilities.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Can a country run a primary deficit while keeping its debt ratio stable?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Yes, under the specified financing and growth assumptions. With 60% opening debt, 3% effective interest and 5% nominal growth, inherited debt declines to about 58.86% of current output. A primary deficit near 1.14% of GDP can leave the ratio stable. I would test what happens if rates reprice or growth weakens before treating that room as a durable spending allowance.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If interest stays at 3% but nominal GDP falls 2%, what primary surplus stabilizes the same opening ratio?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The required surplus is 0.60 × (0.03 − (−0.02))/0.98 = 0.030612, or about 3.06% of current GDP, with no other adjustments. A swing from growth to contraction can reverse the fiscal space calculated in the initial case.
