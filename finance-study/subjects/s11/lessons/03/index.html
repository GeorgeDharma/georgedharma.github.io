Inflation and currency composition

Local inflation can erode some nominal claims. Depreciation raises the local value of foreign-currency debt and can reveal balance-sheet vulnerabilities.

The starting point

A government that collects taxes in local currency but owes dollars faces a mismatch. The quantity of dollars it owes can stay fixed while the local-currency amount needed for repayment jumps. Look through headline debt to the currency of each contract.

Unexpected domestic inflation reduces the purchasing power of fixed-rate local nominal claims. Inflation-indexed claims, short-term borrowing that reprices, and foreign-currency debt respond differently. None of these changes guarantees that refinancing or servicing the debt becomes easier.

Theory and method

Foreign-currency balance-sheet valuation

Net foreign-currency position · Currency mismatch

A ministry has unchanged dollar debt and a large dollar reserve. Can a depreciation still weaken its ability to pay?

An exchange-rate change revalues foreign-currency debts and assets in home money. A gross debt figure adjusts only the foreign liability, while a net economic position also reflects eligible foreign assets. The treasury's immediate payment capacity is a third calculation. A central bank can own reserves that do not legally belong to the treasury, and a future export tax receipt may arrive after a bond payment. Separate currency, control and dates before describing a hedge.

How the result is built

ΔDgross = FᴸΔS; ΔDnet = (Fᴸ − Fᴬ)ΔS

Fᴸ is foreign-currency liability principal, Fᴬ eligible foreign-currency assets on the chosen perimeter, S local currency per foreign unit, and ΔD the translation change in gross or net debt in local currency. Hold foreign amounts fixed.

  1. With $5bn liabilities and $2bn eligible reserves, a move from 10 to 12 local units per dollar adds 5 × 2 = 10bn local to gross debt.
  2. Reserve valuation rises 2 × 2 = 4bn local. Net foreign liability increases only 6bn local on a consolidated economic basis.
  3. If $1bn principal is due next week and only $0.4bn of reserves can be accessed before then, the dated funding need is still $0.6bn before new borrowing.

When the reasoning holds

  • Assets and liabilities must be in the same currency and appropriately included. A euro reserve does not perfectly hedge a dollar obligation.
  • The translation sensitivity holds foreign notionals fixed and excludes growth, indexation and hedging contracts. Cash funding additionally depends on enforceability and settlement.
Terms in this lesson
Principal
The amount borrowed or invested before interest is added.
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Interest rate
The price of borrowing money or the return for lending it, quoted as a percentage over a stated period.
Coupon
The interest payment specified in a bond’s terms.
Revenue
Amount earned by selling goods or services. The customer may pay later.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
First example

A government owes 60bn in local currency and $4bn. At 10 local units per dollar total debt is 100bn local. At 12 per dollar the foreign portion becomes 48bn and the total 108bn, even without new borrowing. GDP is held fixed in this comparison.

Not all nominal debt behaves alike

Fixed-rate local-currency principal loses purchasing power when the domestic price level rises unexpectedly. An inflation-linked obligation adjusts according to its indexation terms, which can include a lag. A floating-rate bond can become more expensive if monetary policy responds through higher interest rates.

Borrowing in domestic currency does not immunize future budgets. If lenders anticipate future inflation, they may demand higher coupons, shorter maturities or stronger indexation when refinancing. Tax revenues and spending may adjust at different speeds.

Currency risk is a balance-sheet question

Express the exchange rate as local units per foreign unit. A rise in this quote means the local currency depreciates and the local carrying value of an unchanged foreign principal increases. Apply the percentage change only to the portion exposed to that currency.

Look for natural offsets such as foreign-currency reserves or tax revenues linked to export proceeds. An asset hedge may protect consolidated net worth yet be legally unavailable for the treasury's particular payment. State whether the analysis uses gross debt, net exposure or treasury cash.

What is held fixed in a ratio?

A currency translation moves debt immediately, while annual GDP, tax receipts and domestic prices may also change in the months after a shock. A pure sensitivity holding GDP fixed isolates direct revaluation. A broader macro scenario includes those secondary effects explicitly.

When both domestic inflation and depreciation occur, count indexed debt and foreign-currency debt according to their separate terms. Adding their percentages to the whole initial debt stock would misapply the shocks to instruments that do not share them.

Method

Modeling a currency and inflation shock

  1. Split the stock

    Record fixed domestic, indexed domestic and each foreign-currency liability in original units, with any eligible offset separately.

  2. Revalue instruments

    Apply the correct exchange-rate quote, indexation rule and timing to each slice before summing in local currency.

  3. Recalculate the denominator

    Report a fixed-GDP sensitivity first if useful, then add a stated nominal GDP path and any changes in interest or fiscal balances.

Worked case

Different claims in one treasury

Initial debt comprises 50bn fixed local, 20bn local indexed to prices and $3bn at 10 local units per dollar. Local GDP is 200bn. Assume a 10% index adjustment and an exchange move to 12, with no new borrowing.

Working

  1. Starting debt is 50 + 20 + 30 = 100bn local, or 50% of GDP. Fixed local principal remains 50 while indexed principal grows to 22 and foreign principal becomes 36.
  2. Total closing local debt is 108bn. At unchanged GDP the ratio becomes 54%. Of the 8bn increase, 2bn comes from indexation and 6bn from foreign-currency revaluation.
  3. If nominal GDP instead grows to 216bn the ratio stays 50%, despite the larger local principal due. The cash burden of dollar payments may still rise relative to the treasury's own local receipts.

Interpretation

A ratio that does not move can conceal a meaningful change in currency payment requirements. Separately examine foreign-currency maturities and usable foreign-currency liquidity.

A common mistake

Applying higher domestic prices automatically to every nominal contract overstates revaluation of unindexed fixed principal.

How to correct it

Read the currency and index clauses for each instrument and distinguish purchasing-power changes from changes in cash amounts owed.

Quick check
Debt consists of 80bn local units plus $2bn. The exchange rate moves from 5 to 6 local units per dollar. How much does the local value of total debt increase?

Each of the $2bn now costs one more local unit, raising total debt from 90bn to 92bn. Only the foreign-currency part is mechanically revalued in this example.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

A state owes 60bn fixed local, 10bn fully price-indexed local and $2bn at 15 local units per dollar. The exchange moves to 18 and the index rises 20%. Initial GDP is 200bn.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate local debt before and after and the ratio using unchanged GDP.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    The state also has $1bn of unencumbered reserves. What part of the direct 6bn currency revaluation is economically offset on a net asset basis?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Initial debt is 60 + 10 + 30 = 100bn. New debt is 60 + 12 + 36 = 108bn. The ratio rises from 50% to 54% on unchanged GDP.

Part B 3 marks

The reserve's local value rises 3bn, offsetting half the direct 6bn rise in dollar debt. It does not reduce the gross debt figure or erase the 2bn index increase.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A ministry has unchanged dollar debt and a large dollar reserve. Can a depreciation still weaken its ability to pay?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Yes. Consolidated reserves reduce the accounting exposure from the $5bn gross position to $3bn net, so depreciation adds 6bn local to net obligations. But only $0.4bn of usable currency is available against the $1bn payment next week. I would require a dated source for the remaining $0.6bn and confirm which institution has authority to draw on the reserve.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If the treasury has a confirmed forward purchase of $0.6bn settling a month later, is the coming payment covered?

Compare with the follow-up answer

A later forward can protect the price of dollars on its own settlement date but cannot deliver next week's $0.6bn. The treasury would need an earlier deliverable arrangement, bridge financing or agreed payment timing, and should report the forward and maturity mismatch separately.
