Sovereign Debt and Emerging-Market Finance
Advanced finance / Lesson 8 of 12

Emerging-market institutions

Domestic-currency market depth, resident investors and monetary credibility alter refinancing options. Country categories alone do not establish risk.

The starting point

Two governments with the same reported debt ratio may face very different refinancing choices. A deep domestic bond market can provide longer maturities in the currency in which governments collect taxes. A narrow market can instead concentrate exposure in a handful of banks.

Investigate who owns the bonds, when they can sell, which currency is owed and how policy credibility influences borrowing costs. Local-currency financing reduces a direct currency mismatch but still exposes the issuer to interest rates and future refinancing.

Theory and method

Original sin in international finance

Eichengreen–Hausmann–Panizza original sin · Currency mismatch

A country sells local-currency bonds to nonresidents. Which vulnerability has it reduced, and which one may remain?

An exporter earns dollars, an ordinary taxpayer pays local currency, and a global investor may demand bonds in a reserve currency. The original-sin literature studies the difficulty some countries have borrowing abroad in their own currency. A government that issues local-currency debt internationally can reduce its direct obligation to deliver more domestic currency after depreciation. Foreign holders can nevertheless sell in a global retreat, leaving the state exposed to liquidity, price and rollover shocks. Currency denomination and ownership need separate evidence.

How the result is built

Foreign-currency mismatch = foreign-currency liabilities − accessible foreign-currency assets; holder residence and currency are separate classifications

Both mismatch terms are foreign units on a stated public or national perimeter. Residence identifies who lends, denomination the currency owed, and accessible assets the resources genuinely available to that borrower.

  1. Compare 100bn domestic-equivalent debt with 70bn local currency and 30bn dollar-linked. Foreign investors own 50bn, of which 40bn is local.
  2. A 10% dollar appreciation adds 3bn to gross local-valued liabilities on the dollar-linked piece, before any financial hedge. The 40bn local debt held abroad does not mechanically revalue its local principal.
  3. Yet foreign sales of that 40bn can depress prices or raise future coupon costs. A reserve position or dollar-linked revenue can also offset part of the separate 30bn currency mismatch.

When the reasoning holds

  • The classification describes the currency promised rather than just contract location. A locally issued dollar-linked note can retain foreign-currency exposure.
  • Gross currency denomination cannot establish net mismatch without the asset and revenue side or the timing of access. Original sin is an institutional financing constraint.
Terms in this lesson
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Interest rate
The price of borrowing money or the return for lending it, quoted as a percentage over a stated period.
Settlement
The final transfer of cash or securities needed to complete a transaction.
Benchmark
A reference chosen in advance for comparison, such as a market index or an equal-weight portfolio.
Yield curve
The relationship between interest rates and time to maturity for a specified market and type of instrument.
Coupon
The interest payment specified in a bond’s terms.
First example

Country A funds long-lived projects using five-year local-currency bonds held by several investor groups. Country B relies on three-month dollar borrowing. With similar debt ratios, B must return to lenders more often and needs foreign currency sooner if tax receipts are local.

Institutions affect the contracts available

A deep domestic bond market has more than a large stock of paper. Investors need a reliable issuance calendar, credible settlement, pricing across maturities and enough trading interest to exit without a major price concession. A benchmark yield curve can help price long-dated obligations.

Credible monetary and fiscal arrangements can encourage investors to accept longer local-currency fixed-rate contracts. If lenders expect high inflation or unpredictable policy, they may demand indexation or rapid repricing. Those contractual changes shift risk even when the headline debt ratio is identical.

The investor base can diversify or concentrate risk

Resident pension funds may provide stable demand when foreign investors exit, but residents' capacity depends on their own liabilities and investment rules. If domestic banks hold most government bonds, drawing on their balance sheets can crowd out private lending or intensify the bank–sovereign link.

Foreign ownership of local-currency bonds reduces the treasury's direct foreign-currency mismatch while leaving vulnerability to global outflows. Domestic ownership of foreign-currency debt still leaves a currency mismatch for the government. Classify residence and denomination separately.

Comparing financing strategies

Maturity, currency, rate reset and concentration determine the size and timing of future exposures. A cheaper short foreign loan may simply exchange a visible coupon saving for contingent currency and rollover losses. A longer local issue may be costly but provide predictable payments in the tax currency.

Institutional reform takes time. Regular issuance, reliable data and transparent auction rules can improve access, while forced purchases may suppress observable yields and conceal risk. An empirical comparison should identify the mechanism rather than reasoning from a country label.

Method

Building a comparable country profile

  1. Separate three dimensions

    Classify liabilities by currency of payment, residence or concentration of holders, and remaining maturity or reset date.

  2. Link to local resources

    Compare payment currency with the tax base and foreign receipts, and measure liquidity and depth at the sizes needed by the treasury.

  3. Test investor behaviour

    Apply coherent withdrawal and reinvestment assumptions to each holder group and identify which institutions could realistically replace a departing group.

Worked case

Identical debt ratios, different funding needs

Both countries owe 80bn against GDP of 100bn. In A, 20% of the debt matures in a year and 10% is in dollars. In B, 50% matures and 60% is in dollars. Hold GDP and exchange-adjusted local prices fixed initially.

Working

  1. Annual principal maturity is 16bn in A and 40bn in B, before interest or new deficits. This is a funding difference even though both debt ratios start at 80%.
  2. A 10% rise in the local price of the dollar increases reported gross debt by 0.8bn in A and 4.8bn in B, assuming unhedged fixed foreign principals.
  3. If A's locally denominated debt is largely held by overseas funds, it can still experience abrupt selling. Treasury currency risk and the investor-base outflow channel are distinct and require separate data.

Interpretation

A credible comparison should examine the currency and timing of tax receipts and reserves as well as the liability schedule before declaring one debt profile definitively safer.

A common mistake

Using creditor nationality as a substitute for currency denomination can reverse the conclusion about the treasury's direct exposure.

How to correct it

Record the contract's currency and the creditor's residence in distinct fields and check cross-classified holdings where possible.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A country's 100bn debt is 70% local-currency and 30% dollar-linked. Nonresidents own 50bn, including 35bn local-currency bonds. All figures use the initial exchange rate.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Classify dollar and local holdings by resident versus nonresident holders.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    With 10% dollar appreciation, find the direct local-value increase and identify why nonresident selling is a separate risk.

Part A 3 marks

Nonresidents hold 35bn local and 15bn dollar-linked. Residents hold the remaining 35bn local and 15bn dollar-linked.

Part B 3 marks

Dollar-linked debt adds 3bn in local value. Sale of nonresident-held local debt can depress prices or increase future funding costs without directly changing its local principal.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A country sells local-currency bonds to nonresidents. Which vulnerability has it reduced, and which one may remain?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

The ability to sell local-currency liabilities abroad relaxes the particular denomination constraint described as original sin. The treasury owes the contracted local amount even if its currency weakens. But foreign lenders can exit, yields can jump, and maturities still need refinancing. I would request holdings by currency and residence, alongside maturity, market depth and accessible foreign assets, before assessing overall resilience.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Suppose 20bn of dollar-linked debt is replaced with local-currency debt held by the same foreign investors. What changes under a 10% dollar rise?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Gross dollar-sensitive principal falls from 30bn to 10bn, reducing direct translation from 3bn to 1bn local. Holding foreign ownership constant leaves a potential nonresident outflow channel, although its pricing and market effects can change with the new contract.
