Stress scenarios

Combine growth, rates, currency and financing needs in coherent scenarios. Distinguish a sensitivity that varies one input from a macroeconomic scenario.

The starting point

A sensitivity calculation changes one assumption and holds the rest constant. A macroeconomic scenario links several plausible changes. A commodity shock can simultaneously reduce tax revenue, depress GDP, weaken the currency and increase the yield required on new borrowing.

Separate new interest cost from revaluation of debt already owed. Long-term fixed-rate debt does not all reprice the moment market yields rise. State how much matures, what is indexed and how quickly the shock enters cash interest and the debt ratio.

Theory and method

Stochastic debt sustainability analysis

Debt fan chart · Joint macro-fiscal scenarios

Does the ratio in an average macroeconomic scenario equal the average ratio across plausible outcomes?

During a recession a treasury can face slower GDP growth, higher borrowing rates and a weaker primary balance at the same time. Debt is divided by GDP, so even simple state outcomes can make the expected ratio differ from a calculation at averaged rates. A fan chart uses a distribution of joint paths to show dispersion around a baseline. Its probabilities are conditional on chosen shocks, dependence and fiscal reactions. Start with a transparent two-state table before presenting a precise-looking percentile.

How the result is built

E[b₁] = Σₛpₛ{b₀(1+iₛ)/(1+gₛ) − sₛ + aₛ}

s indexes joint scenarios, pₛ their probabilities, b debt/GDP, i effective interest, g nominal GDP growth, sₛ primary surplus/current GDP, aₛ stock adjustment/current GDP, and E and Σ the probability-weighted average and sum.

  1. Start at 70% debt/GDP. In a 75% ordinary state interest and growth are both 4%, and a 1% surplus gives closing debt of 69%.
  2. In a 25% stress interest is 6%, growth minus 4%, and a deficit 2% with no other adjustment. Debt becomes 70 × 1.06/0.96 + 2 = 79.292%.
  3. The two-state expected ratio is 0.75 × 69 + 0.25 × 79.292 = 71.573%. The 25% stress also identifies a separate chance of exceeding a 75% monitoring level.

When the reasoning holds

  • Joint state probabilities and fiscal responses must be coherent. Independently sampling variables can break crisis correlations and understate the tail.
  • Read model percentiles as conditional outputs. A two-state exercise cannot describe rare crises outside the stated states or produce a reliable empirical frequency.
Terms in this lesson
Revenue
Amount earned by selling goods or services. The customer may pay later.
Percentage point
The difference between two percentages. A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises one percentage point.
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Primary balance
Government revenue minus non-interest spending under the sign convention used in this course.
Coupon
The interest payment specified in a bond’s terms.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
First example

A state owes 100bn at fixed rates. Only 20bn is refinanced for a full year under a scenario in which the new borrowing rate rises two percentage points. With no other repricing, the extra annual interest is 0.4bn, even though market values could change across all the bonds.

A scenario is a financing narrative

A single sensitivity changes one parameter. A macro scenario instead describes jointly consistent changes in growth, primary revenue, interest costs, exchange rates and financing conditions. Link each move to a cause, such as a collapse in copper prices for a commodity exporter.

A fall in exports can affect tax receipts, currency and reserves simultaneously. Avoid counting the same tax shortfall both in a revised primary balance and again as a separate stock–flow adjustment. Each item should have one place in the debt and cash reconciliation.

Rate shocks take time to enter the budget

A yield rise marks down the market value of existing fixed-rate bonds, but it does not immediately raise their contractual coupons. New borrowing, floating-rate debt and maturing debt that reprices transmit the shock to cash interest at different speeds.

Build a repricing calendar by instrument. A one-year estimate may understate the long-run effect as more debt rolls over, whereas a shock imposed on the full book immediately can overstate first-year financing pressure. Include the maturity wall and available liquidity.

Testing realism and responses

A fan chart can show a range of paths from a specified joint distribution. Its intervals are conditional on the model and historical relationships, which may fail during regime change. A deterministic adverse scenario remains informative even without a credible probability.

Compare projected growth, borrowing rates and primary surpluses with past outcomes and peer experience. Test responses that the government could actually execute under stress. A nominal asset sale may be delayed or generate proceeds below the base-case valuation.

Method

Assembling the adverse path

  1. Define initial exposures

    Establish opening debt, currency mix, maturities, rate resets, usable cash and the base-case primary and GDP path.

  2. Apply shocks in sequence

    Separate revaluation on inherited debt, contractual interest, new issuance at changed yields and primary financing flows.

  3. Identify the decision point

    Calculate closing debt ratio and gross financing need. Find which assumptions would first exhaust liquid resources or prevent market access.

Worked case

A commodity exporter under pressure

A state starts with 100bn local debt equivalent, including 30bn foreign-currency principal. Baseline GDP is 150bn. Under a stress the dollar rises 20%, nominal GDP falls to 145bn, cash interest is 5bn, and the primary deficit is 3bn. Assume interest excludes currency translation.

Working

  1. Foreign principal revaluation adds 30 × 20% = 6bn. Closing debt is 100 + 6 + 5 + 3 = 114bn if those flows are financed by borrowing and there are no other adjustments.
  2. The new ratio is 114/145 = 78.62%, against an opening 66.67%. Both the larger numerator and smaller denominator explain the change.
  3. If 20bn principal also matures and rolls at par during the year, gross financing need before other sources is 20 + 5 + 3 = 28bn, using the stated amounts due. The rollover itself does not add another 20bn to closing principal.

Interpretation

A fuller scenario would separate the currency denomination of maturing principal and the dates of any higher yields on replacement debt. The case fixes those flows to isolate the accounting.

A common mistake

Adding all maturing principal to year-end debt even when it is refinanced at par records the replacement borrowing but fails to remove the retired claim.

How to correct it

Keep a stock roll-forward and a separate gross cash calendar. Reconcile the maturity's retirement and refinancing explicitly.

Quick check
A government has 50bn fixed-rate debt. Of this, 10bn immediately refinances for a full year at a rate three percentage points higher. What is the extra first-year cash interest?

Only the refinanced 10bn bears the additional 0.03 for the full year. The other contractual coupons stay fixed under the stated scenario.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

Opening debt is 70bn, including 20bn foreign currency at initial rates. The local price of foreign currency rises 15%. Interest is 4bn, primary surplus 1bn, and GDP falls from 100bn to 95bn. Assume no other stock changes.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find closing debt and the debt ratio.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    If 12bn principal matures at the stated post-shock amount, what is the cash financing need before reserves? Why is it different from the debt increase?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Revaluation adds 3bn. Closing debt is 70 + 3 + 4 − 1 = 76bn. The ratio is 76/95 = 80%, up from 70%.

Part B 3 marks

Gross need is 12 + 4 − 1 = 15bn. The 12bn maturity is a cash rollover requirement, while the 3bn revaluation increases measured debt without itself being a separate current cash outflow.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Does the ratio in an average macroeconomic scenario equal the average ratio across plausible outcomes?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would calculate debt within each joint state before averaging. Debt divided by a stressed GDP denominator is nonlinear, and fiscal responses can move with growth. Here the expected ratio is 71.57%, but the adverse state reaches 79.29% and breaches a 75% warning level. For a minister I would report the distribution, financing calendar and assumptions alongside the central path.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Stress probability doubles to 50% while both paths stay unchanged. How do the expected ratio and breach probability change?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Expected ratio becomes (69 + 79.292)/2 ≈ 74.146%. It remains below 75%, while the modeled breach probability rises from 25% to 50%. A policy that only monitors the average would miss the increase in downside risk.
