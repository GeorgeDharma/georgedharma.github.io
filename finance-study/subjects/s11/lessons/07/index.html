Banks and the sovereign

Banks may hold public bonds and governments may support banks. A shock can therefore return to the party where it started and amplify losses.

The starting point

Banks often hold government bonds as investments or as collateral for borrowing. A fall in their price can reduce bank equity if recognized in the relevant accounts. It can also reduce the amount of cash the bonds raise as collateral, independently of accounting recognition.

The link works in the opposite direction too. A fragile banking system may need public support, adding to government borrowing or guarantees. Investors may then demand higher sovereign yields, lowering bond prices again. Track the cash, valuation and fiscal links separately.

Theory and method

Sovereign–bank nexus

Bank–sovereign feedback loop · Collateral channel

Why can the loss of collateral capacity exceed a bank's marked loss on sovereign securities?

A bank uses government bonds to fund short-term borrowing. When sovereign yields rise, bond prices fall and cut eligible collateral value. A lender may simultaneously apply a larger haircut, reducing cash advanced on each remaining euro of collateral. These are two separate ways to weaken funding. If the state then rescues the bank, its own funding needs may increase and feed back to bond prices. Trace amounts in equity, liquidity and the public budget on separate ledgers.

How the result is built

Funding capacity = V(1−h); ΔK ≈ −VDΔy

V is starting market value of the sovereign position, h the collateral haircut, K bank equity when the price move is recognized, D modified duration and Δy absolute yield rise in decimals. Recompute funding on the stressed market value.

  1. A bank holds 50bn bonds with duration 4. A two-percentage-point yield rise implies approximately an 8% price fall, or 4bn, before convexity.
  2. At 10% initial haircut the bonds support 45bn of borrowing. At stressed value 46bn and a 20% haircut, funding drops to 36.8bn.
  3. The funding-capacity decline is 8.2bn while the marked equity loss is 4bn. It would be wrong to charge both as independent losses against the same equity.

When the reasoning holds

  • The duration estimate is first-order and assumes a compatible yield shift. Large changes or callable securities need full repricing.
  • Only pledged-eligible collateral produces this funding channel. Equity recognition depends on accounting classification and hedges. Bank-support decisions are contingent.
Terms in this lesson
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
First example

A bank holds €20bn of sovereign bonds measured at market value with €5bn equity. A 10% bond-price fall costs €2bn, leaving €3bn equity before tax or hedges. A later government recapitalization could transmit part of this damage back to public finances.

Why the two balance sheets are connected

Banks can hold government securities for investment, collateral and liquidity management. Their value and eligibility affect bank capital and funding. Governments depend on banks for bond placement, credit transmission and payments, and may face pressure to support them in a crisis.

A higher sovereign yield reduces the price of existing fixed-payment securities. Recognition in reported equity depends on the portfolio's classification and the applicable accounting framework. Economic value and collateral availability can change even if immediate reported accounting recognition is limited.

Cash, capital and credit supply

A haircut on sovereign collateral reduces funding available against it. If the bank must sell more securities, it can put additional pressure on prices. Shrinking lending can also weaken domestic output and tax receipts, adding a real-economy channel to the direct bank–state balance-sheet link.

A sovereign guarantee for bank debt can support funding immediately but creates contingent public exposure. A cash recapitalization raises the bank's equity while using government funding or assets. It is a mistake to count the bank's entire asset stock as an automatic public loss.

Mapping successive rounds

A stress exercise can model the initial sovereign shock, bank valuation loss, required bank response and government's potential financing need. A second-round increase in yields should be presented as a scenario with an explicit causal assumption, rather than applied automatically as an accounting identity.

Link dollar losses to bank size and equity rather than reporting market percentages alone. For the state, compare any actual support with GDP, liquid reserves and refinancing needs. Size and currency concentration determine how quickly a link can become destabilizing.

Method

Tracing the loop

  1. Revalue bank holdings

    State the bond position, duration or price shock, accounting treatment and any hedge before attributing a loss.

  2. Determine the bank response

    Apply the capital or liquidity condition to the stressed balance sheet. Identify recapitalization, asset sales or secured funding needed.

  3. Return to the sovereign

    Estimate cash or contingent support and model only the price, growth or financing feedback justified by a stated scenario.

Worked case

A haircut hits funding before recovery

A bank has €8bn equity, including €15bn of sovereign bonds measured at market value among its assets. It can initially use the full bonds as collateral at a 10% haircut. A sovereign shock cuts bond prices 20% and the haircut rises to 25%.

Working

  1. Bonds fall from €15bn to €12bn, a €3bn loss. With no tax or hedge effects and immediate equity recognition, equity falls from €8bn to €5bn.
  2. Secured funding capacity goes from €13.5bn to €9bn, a €4.5bn fall. The funding reduction is distinct from the €3bn valuation loss and should not simply be deducted from equity again.
  3. If the state injects €2bn cash equity financed by issuing debt, bank equity increases to €7bn and public gross debt rises €2bn under the simplified assumptions. The government also acquires an asset whose recovery is uncertain.

Interpretation

Whether that support calms markets or worsens sovereign pricing depends on fiscal capacity and confidence. It is not predetermined by the initial loss.

A common mistake

Adding the decline in collateral borrowing capacity to the price loss as a second equity loss double-counts different measures.

How to correct it

Track equity, liquidity and sovereign financing in separate columns, and specify any actual forced-sale loss if it occurs.

Quick check
Sovereign bonds held by domestic banks fall in value, banks weaken, and the government borrows to recapitalize them. Which development would reinforce the same feedback loop?

Higher required yields lower the price of existing fixed-payment bonds, all else equal. That can further weaken banks holding them and increase expected calls on the government.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

A bank owns €10bn sovereign bonds booked at market value and has €4bn equity. The bonds fall 15%. The collateral haircut increases from 10% to 20%, on the same bond position.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Find bank equity and the change in funding capacity.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    A €1bn state-financed equity injection follows. Explain changes in bank and sovereign accounting and one potential feedback.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Equity falls €1.5bn to €2.5bn. Funding falls from €9bn to 0.80 × €8.5bn = €6.8bn, a €2.2bn decline.

Part B 3 marks

Bank equity rises to €3.5bn. If financed by new government borrowing, public gross debt rises €1bn while the state receives equity. Additional sovereign funding pressure may raise yields and hurt remaining bank bond values.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why can the loss of collateral capacity exceed a bank's marked loss on sovereign securities?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

The bond decline and the haircut work together on funding. Here a 4bn mark reduces the asset to 46bn, and lenders then advance only 80% rather than 90%. Capacity falls by 8.2bn. Equity loses the 4bn if the mark is recognized. The remainder is a liquidity problem unless it actually forces another loss. Public support can connect both balance sheets again.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The bank had hedged the entire bond price change with a derivative that pays next month. Is next-day funding restored?

Compare with the follow-up answer

An effective hedge can offset the eventual economic price loss, but the underlying bonds can still support only 36.8bn at the new haircut. Unless the derivative or its proceeds can secure same-day cash, the next-day liquidity problem remains.
