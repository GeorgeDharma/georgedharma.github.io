Sovereign spreads

Compare yields with an appropriate benchmark. Global risk appetite, liquidity and local fundamentals can all change a sovereign spread.

The starting point

A bond yielding 8% cannot be judged expensive or cheap until it is compared with an appropriate reference. A sovereign spread is the yield difference against a benchmark. Match currency, maturity and, where possible, payment structure so the difference has a clear interpretation.

Investors may demand compensation for expected default losses, uncertainty and difficulty trading. A spread can widen during a global sell-off even if the government's finances barely change. Look at peers and trading conditions before attributing all of a move to domestic policy.

Theory and method

Risk-neutral sovereign bond pricing with recovery

Reduced-form one-period credit model · Credit spread

A one-year bond yields 8%. Can you infer the government's actual default probability by subtracting the 3% risk-free yield?

A bond price combines its promised repayment with what investors expect to receive if the issuer defaults. Even in a simple model, a given price discount implies a different default probability when recovery is high. The pricing probability also includes compensation investors demand for bearing bad-state losses and may differ from a historical frequency. A liquid benchmark, comparable maturity and explicit recovery convention are essential before turning observed prices into conditional model outputs.

How the result is built

P = [F(1−q) + RFq]/(1+r); q = [1 − P(1+r)/F]/(1−R)

P is today's price, F promised principal at one year, R fraction of face recovered at year-end on default, r one-year risk-free rate, and q the risk-neutral default probability under this model.

  1. Take face 100, price 92, risk-free rate 3% and 40% recovery. The risk-neutral expected terminal payment required at that price is 92 × 1.03 = 94.76.
  2. A default costs 60 relative to full redemption, so q = (100 − 94.76)/60 = 8.733%. The promised yield from price is 100/92 − 1 = 8.696%.
  3. Comparing that yield directly with the 3% benchmark gives 5.696 percentage points. The probability translation also accounts for severity and discounting.

When the reasoning holds

  • The model assumes a zero-coupon claim, fixed recovery paid at maturity, common currency and no separate liquidity premium. Other bond structures require another pricing model.
  • q is a market-consistent pricing probability if the assumptions hold. Physical default estimates require a separate model and evidence.
Terms in this lesson
Benchmark
A reference chosen in advance for comparison, such as a market index or an equal-weight portfolio.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Percentage point
The difference between two percentages. A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises one percentage point.
Basis point
One hundredth of a percentage point. A rise from 4.00% to 4.01% is one basis point.
Credit spread
Extra yield over a chosen reference yield. Credit risk, liquidity and market conditions can all affect it.
How to read the notation
Basis points and percentage points
A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises by one percentage point, or 100 basis points. Its increase relative to the original rate is (5 − 4) ÷ 4 = 25%.

All notation examples

First example

A dollar sovereign bond yields 7.0% and a comparable dollar reference yields 4.2%. The spread is 2.8 percentage points, or 280 basis points. If both yields rise 0.5 percentage points, the refinancing yield rises but the spread is unchanged.

The benchmark does much of the work

A sovereign spread compares the sovereign yield to a reference yield with comparable currency, maturity and payment structure. A local-currency bond and a foreign-currency government bond can reflect different inflation and monetary risks. Matching just the number of years does not solve that mismatch.

A change in the reference yield can alter the headline financing cost even if the spread is stable. Conversely the spread can widen even while the country's own yield falls, if the reference falls faster. Decompose changes in both quantities before attaching an explanation.

Expected loss and compensation for risk

In a simple approximation a credit spread reflects a default probability times loss given default, plus compensation for uncertainty, liquidity and other features. A market-implied default probability is model-dependent and often risk-neutral rather than a direct forecast of actual frequency.

In a one-period zero-coupon example, discounted expected repayment under a chosen pricing probability supplies a transparent price. A more realistic coupon bond also depends on the timing of missed payments, recovery basis and changing discount rates.

Distinguishing local from global shocks

A global tightening can raise spreads across many comparable countries while country-specific fiscal or political news changes one issuer relative to that group. Use benchmarks and matched peers to test the hypothesis, taking account of differing duration, commodity exposure and market depth.

Quoted yields on thinly traded bonds may be stale. A large bid–offer spread can make a reported price change look like a credit event. Consider actual trades, associated maturities, exchange rates and the dates of public information.

Method

Reading a spread report

  1. Match instruments

    Express both yields on comparable currency, maturity and compounding bases, and state the benchmark.

  2. Reconcile changes

    Convert percentage-point differences into basis points and distinguish the change in total yield from the change relative to the reference.

  3. Test explanations

    Compare the move with peers and liquidity, then identify evidence needed for a particular domestic credit story.

Worked case

A falling yield and a widening spread

A dollar bond's yield falls from 8.0% to 7.6%. A comparable dollar benchmark falls from 4.5% to 3.5%. A matched peer's spread increases from 330 bp to 370 bp.

Working

  1. Sovereign spread begins at 8.0 − 4.5 = 3.5 points, or 350 bp. It ends at 7.6 − 3.5 = 4.1 points, or 410 bp. The spread widens 60 bp despite a 40 bp fall in yield.
  2. The 100 bp benchmark decline plus 60 bp spread increase reconciles to the 40 bp yield decline. The peer widens 40 bp, so relative widening against that peer is 20 bp.
  3. The 20 bp difference motivates a country-specific investigation. It does not prove domestic credit quality caused the move because liquidity, issuance size or different global risk exposures could differ.

Interpretation

For an imminent auction, the absolute 7.6% may matter to the interest bill. For an attribution of risk pricing, the spread and matched peer comparison answer different questions.

A common mistake

Converting spread directly into a physical default frequency assumes away recovery uncertainty, risk premiums and liquidity compensation.

How to correct it

State a pricing model and recovery convention if translating prices into probability, and label the resulting probability accordingly.

Quick check
A comparable benchmark yield falls from 4% to 3.5% while a sovereign yield remains 6%. How does its spread change?

The initial spread was 2 percentage points. The new spread is 2.5 percentage points, a 50-basis-point widening even though the sovereign yield did not move.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A five-year dollar sovereign yield rises from 6.4% to 7.0%. The corresponding reference rises from 3.0% to 3.8%. A comparable peer spread narrows by 5 bp.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate initial and final spreads and attribute the movement in total yield.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Compare the spread change with the peer and identify one reason to be careful in interpretation.

Part A 3 marks

Spread goes from 340 bp to 320 bp, narrowing 20 bp. The benchmark adds 80 bp and the spread subtracts 20 bp, producing the observed 60 bp yield increase.

Part B 3 marks

The sovereign narrows 15 bp more than its peer. Differences in tradability, duration, issue supply or exposure to global conditions could account for some of the relative move.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A one-year bond yields 8%. Can you infer the government's actual default probability by subtracting the 3% risk-free yield?

Compare with a spoken answer

No. I would need the bond price and promised payments, comparable discounting and a recovery assumption. In the zero-coupon example, price 92 and 40% recovery imply about 8.73% pricing probability even though the yield spread is about 5.70 points. Liquidity and risk compensation can affect the market price, so I would not present this output as a forecast of actual defaults.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Keep price 92 and risk-free rate 3%, but assume only 20% recovery. What changes?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Each default now loses 80 of face rather than 60. The same 5.24 shortfall in expected terminal payment implies q = 5.24/80 = 6.55%. The observed price and yield spread stay fixed, while the model implies a lower default probability.
