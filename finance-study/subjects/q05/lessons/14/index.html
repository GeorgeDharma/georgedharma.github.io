Merger arbitrage in cash and stock deals

Theory and terminology

Merger arbitrage and market-implied deal probability

risk arbitrage · deal spread · stock-for-stock hedge · break price

What you should be able to explain

  • Infer a discounted two-outcome closing weight and stress its dependence on the break price and timetable.
  • Size a fixed-ratio stock hedge and calculate both outcomes after share-borrow fees.
  • Distinguish a conditional annualized spread from a strategy's expected return.

Identify what the contract promises

A cash offer pays a fixed amount per target share if the transaction closes. Buying the target captures the cash spread on completion. There is no contractual acquirer-share delivery to hedge. A fixed-stock offer instead pays h acquirer shares per target share. To hedge that delivery, buy one target share and sell h borrowed acquirer shares. Upon closing, deliver the received shares against the short.

If the deal breaks, the target can fall towards its standalone value and the acquirer may rise. Both legs can lose money. A floating exchange ratio or a collar changes the shares delivered as prices move. Read the consideration formula, dividend terms, approval conditions and outside date before choosing the hedge. A merger agreement does not guarantee that stock can be borrowed until closing.

Turn a price into a conditional closing weight

In a simplified cash deal suppose completion and failure both resolve on the same date. The offer pays K on success and the share is worth B on failure. Discount both outcomes at a common cash discount factor. Solving for the weight that reproduces the target price gives a market-implied closing weight under this two-state pricing assumption.

This weight is sensitive to B, to settlement date and to interim cash flows. A break price is a scenario for the future standalone stock, not necessarily its pre-announcement close. A priced loss in bad markets can push the implied weight away from the real-world success probability. A competing bid introduces a third outcome and a single share price cannot identify three state weights.

Read annualized spreads and empirical evidence carefully

For a fixed cash offer the gross successful-deal return is K divided by the purchase price, minus one. Raising their ratio to one divided by the holding period in years puts it on an annual compound basis. That figure assumes timely completion and repeatable reinvestment at the same rate. Delay alone lowers the annualized figure, while the probability and loss on failure need their own estimates.

Mitchell and Pulvino found that historical risk-arbitrage returns co-moved more strongly with the market in severe declines. That observed association describes a sample and does not establish what caused an individual deal to fail. For an investable test, timestamp announcements and amendments, include withdrawn deals, model tradable entry prices and borrow fees, and compare outcomes in stressed and ordinary markets.

Method

Build the result

P=D[qK+(1−q)B], so q=(P/D−B)/(K−B). Fixed-stock close spread per target share = hA₀−P.

P is today's target price, K and B are cash-deal close and break values per target share at the common horizon, D is its discount factor, and q is the implied pricing weight on closing. A₀ is today's acquirer price and h is acquirer shares per target share.

  1. Carry today's target price forward to the resolution date by dividing by D. Starting from the failure payoff B, a closing weight q adds q times the gap K−B.
  2. Subtract B and divide by K−B. If the result falls outside [0,1], revisit the break value, dates or additional outcomes before interpreting any probability.
  3. For a fixed-stock offer the long target delivers hA at completion, where A is the then-current acquirer price. The short gains h(A₀−A). Their sum less entry price is hA₀−P, before borrowing and trading costs.
Worked case

What does a three-dollar cash spread imply?

A target costs $51. A signed cash offer promises $54 in six months. Assume a failed deal leaves the target worth $39 on the same date, no dividends or fees, and a six-month discount factor of 0.98. A separate analyst estimates a 90% real-world close probability.

Working

  1. The visible spread is $54−$51=$3 per share. Conditional on timely success the gross return is 3/51=5.88%, or (54/51)²−1=12.11% annualized. If only the close date slips to nine months, that annualized figure becomes (54/51)^(4/3)−1=7.92%.
  2. Today's $51 is equivalent to $51/0.98=$52.0408 at six months. The price-implied closing weight is (52.0408−39)/(54−39)=86.94%.
  3. Against that financed entry, completion earns $54−52.0408=$1.9592. Failure loses $39−52.0408=−$13.0408. Even several successful trades could be undone by one break of this size.
  4. Using the analyst's own 90% estimate, expected terminal proceeds are 0.90×54+0.10×39=$52.50. Discounted at the cash rate this exceeds the $51 entry by $0.45 before risk compensation and costs.

Decision and interpretation

A $3 announced spread translates to $0.45 of discounted model surplus under the analyst's assumptions. The desk would size against the $13.04 financed break loss and revise the analysis when approval timing or recovery changes.

Quick check
A cash target costs $99. At one common resolution date success pays $105 and failure leaves $81. The discount factor is 0.99. Which closing weight fits the two-state price?

Carry $99 forward to $100, then solve (100−81)/(105−81)=19/24=79.17%. Using today's $99 against future payoffs without discounting gives the wrong 75%.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

A fixed-stock deal promises 0.8 acquirer shares for each target share. Buy 100 targets at $38 when the acquirer trades at $50. In three months the deal closes with the acquirer at $44, or it breaks with the target at $27 and the acquirer at $53. Borrowed shares cost 5% per year on their initial short notional for exactly one quarter. Ignore dividends, interest, commissions and margin changes. Size the hedge, compute both net P&Ls, and assess a 90% closing estimate.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked solution
  1. Short 100×0.8=80 acquirer shares, initial short notional $4,000. The quarter's share-borrow fee is $4,000×0.05×0.25=$50 in either state. Short-sale proceeds serve as collateral and are not profit.
  2. Completion delivers 80 shares worth $3,520, so the target leg loses $280 versus its $3,800 cost. The short earns 80×($50−$44)=$480. Net P&L is −280+480−50=+$150.
  3. On failure, the target loses 100×($27−$38)=−$1,100 and the short loses 80×($50−$53)=−$240. Net loss is $1,390. A 90% success estimate gives 0.90×150−0.10×1,390=−$4. The zero-profit probability is 1,390/(1,390+150)=90.26%.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A target trades below the announced offer. What is the fund getting paid to bear, and how would you measure it?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I read what each target share receives and when, then value the close and break outcomes. I short the promised number of acquirer shares for a fixed-stock offer. The remaining spread can compensate for deal failure, delay, trading costs and the difficulty of financing the position.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The clerk now discovers the stock offer guarantees a fixed dollar value with a floating share count. Can the desk leave the fixed 80-share short in place?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Re-read the fixing window and any caps or floors. The number delivered now depends on the reference acquirer price, so a permanent short of 80 creates a residual acquirer-price exposure. Recompute the hedge from the actual payoff and stress the fixing, borrow and failure scenarios.
