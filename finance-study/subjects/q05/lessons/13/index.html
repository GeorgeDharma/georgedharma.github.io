Test a factor premium with Fama–MacBeth regressions

Distinguish a time-series factor loading from a cross-sectional price of exposure.

Theory and terminology

Fama–MacBeth two-pass regressions

factor risk premium · cross-sectional asset pricing

What you should be able to explain

  • Distinguish a time-series factor loading from a cross-sectional price of exposure.
  • Calculate period-by-period slopes, an average premium and an illustrative standard error, and diagnose limitations.

Estimate who bears the risk in the first pass

An exposure, or beta, measures an asset's return sensitivity to a factor return. In a one-factor time-series regression, estimate beta as the covariance of asset excess returns with the factor divided by the factor variance. It is possible for many assets to load significantly on a source of variation even if bearing more of that variation earns no higher expected return.

Use a prior estimation window or a design appropriate to the empirical question. An investable forecast cannot estimate today's exposures using next year's data. Document the factor definition, the risk-free return, the dates and whether the exposures are held constant or re-estimated. Nearly identical betas leave little cross-sectional variation with which to identify a premium.

Price the exposure each period in the second pass

At each later date, regress that period's realized excess returns across assets on their previously estimated betas. Include an intercept unless the model restriction being tested calls for setting it to zero. The slope λₜ is return per unit of beta for that period. It is a realized estimate whose sign may change. Repeating the regression creates a time series of slopes.

Average these slopes to estimate the typical price of exposure and examine the average intercept or pricing errors to assess fit. A characteristic regression, such as returns on lagged size and book-to-market, can also use monthly cross-sections. Its slope prices a change in that characteristic and does not automatically represent a price of covariance risk. Keep the economic interpretation tied to the actual regressor.

Where the standard error comes from

Under independent dates with stable slope distribution, the standard error of their average is the sample standard deviation of the period slopes divided by the square root of the number of dates. Each monthly slope is already built from the whole cross-section. Treating every asset-month as an independent observation would overlook common news within a month.

If slopes are serially correlated, estimate a time-series long-run variance, for example using a justified HAC bandwidth. Estimated betas also introduce errors-in-variables and may attenuate slopes or affect inference. A Shanken-type correction applies under particular asset-pricing assumptions. Portfolio grouping or separately estimated betas may help, with their own costs. Four months suffice to show the calculation. A credible research test needs a longer record.

Method

Build the result

Rᵉᵢ,ₜ=αₜ+λₜβ̂ᵢ+uᵢ,ₜ; λ̄=(Σₜλₜ)/T; SE(λ̄)=sλ/√T for independent dates

Rᵉᵢ,ₜ is realized return above cash on asset i in month t. β̂ᵢ is the previously estimated dimensionless exposure, αₜ is the cross-sectional intercept, λₜ the return per unit beta, and u the residual. T is number of months and sλ the sample standard deviation of the fitted slopes.

  1. For each asset fit a time-series regression over its exposure window and store a slope known before each evaluation period.
  2. For each evaluation month, regress returns across assets on those stored slopes. With an intercept the scalar coefficient is the cross-sectional sum of centered beta times centered return divided by the sum of squared centered betas.
  3. Average the monthly coefficients. For independent months use the variation of those coefficients through time to estimate the standard error. Base dependent-period inference on a suitable long-run variance instead.
Worked case

Does factor exposure earn more across three test portfolios?

Pre-estimated betas for low, middle and high portfolios are 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5. Monthly percentage excess returns in that order are (0,3,2), (3,1,1), (0,1,4), and (1,0,3). Use an intercept each month, fixed betas and independent dates only for the illustrative standard error.

Working

  1. Centered betas are −0.5, 0, +0.5 and their squared sum is 0.5. Since they sum to zero, the slope numerator is 0.5 times high return minus 0.5 times low return. Thus each slope is high minus low in percentage points per unit beta.
  2. The four slopes are 2, −2, 4 and 2. Their average is (2−2+4+2)/4=1.5 percentage points per month per unit beta.
  3. Squared deviations around 1.5 sum to 0.25+12.25+6.25+0.25=19. The sample standard deviation is √(19/3)=2.517 points and standard error is 2.517/2=1.258 points.
  4. The ratio of mean to standard error is about 1.19. The observed positive average is imprecise even under the favorable independence assumption.

Decision and interpretation

Report the positive, uncertain estimate. Expand the time sample and examine serial dependence, alternative factors and exposure precision before interpreting it as a priced risk or building a tilt.

Quick check
A portfolio has beta 1.5 and another 0.5, and the estimated monthly price of risk is −2 percentage points per unit beta. What does the fitted cross-sectional relation imply?

Their beta difference is one. Multiplying by the period slope gives the fitted spread. Asset-specific residuals can make each realized spread differ from the fitted relation.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

Low and high portfolios have pre-estimated betas 0.8 and 1.2. Their high-minus-low realized returns in four independent months are +0.4, 0.0, +0.8 and −0.4 percentage points. Estimate the monthly premium, its standard error and a limitation.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked solution
  1. The beta gap is 0.4. Dividing each return spread by 0.4 gives monthly slopes +1, 0, +2 and −1 percentage points of return per unit beta.
  2. Their average is 0.5. Squared deviations sum to 5, so sample standard deviation is √(5/3)=1.291 points and standard error is 1.291/2=0.6455 points.
  3. The ratio is about 0.775. Four dates provide weak evidence. With only two assets, a cross-sectional line with intercept also fits exactly each month and offers no within-month model fit test.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

If several portfolios move with a factor, how can an empirical researcher test whether greater exposure actually earns a premium?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I estimate each portfolio's sensitivity to the factor first. Each month I compare realized excess returns across portfolios with different pre-estimated sensitivities. I then study the average and time variation of the monthly slopes, allowing for dependence and errors in the estimated exposures.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Can a test with a thousand stocks be treated as having a thousand independent observations for the average monthly price of risk?

Compare with the follow-up answer

No. A large cross-section may improve estimation of one month's slope. Its contribution to the time-average remains one monthly slope. Dependence over months and first-pass estimation error still require attention.
