Linear models and regularization

Penalizing coefficient size can reduce estimation noise. Select the penalty using training data and keep the final test separate.

The starting point

With many noisy predictors, an ordinary regression may fit small historical quirks with large offsetting coefficients. Regularization adds a penalty for coefficient size to the fitting objective. Ridge regression penalizes squared coefficients and typically shrinks them. Lasso penalizes absolute coefficients and can shrink some exactly to zero.

A penalty parameter controls the trade-off between following the training data and keeping the model stable. Standardize features using training-period estimates so a feature’s units do not dictate its penalty. Choose the parameter on chronological validation data, then evaluate the frozen procedure on a later untouched test period.

Theory and method

Elastic net and soft thresholding

Lasso · Ridge regression

When does a penalized linear model set a signal exactly to zero, and how does it shrink a signal that survives?

A research team can reduce reliance on noisy return signals by imposing two distinct penalties. An absolute-value penalty creates a threshold below which a weak estimated signal receives no coefficient. A squared penalty continuously shrinks the coefficients that survive and can stabilize correlated predictors. In a standardized single-coordinate example the exact solution exposes both operations. Real multi-feature models optimize the coordinates jointly or iteratively and select penalty strength on future-like validation data.

How the result is built

b* = sign(z) max(|z|−λ₁,0)/(1+λ₂)

b is a dimensionless standardized coefficient, z its one-coordinate unpenalized estimate under unit quadratic curvature, λ₁ the absolute-value penalty, λ₂ the squared penalty strength and sign(z) its sign. Both penalties are nonnegative in this normalization.

  1. Minimize ½(b−z)² + λ₁|b| + ½λ₂b². For b>0 the derivative is (1+λ₂)b−z+λ₁, and for b<0 its penalty sign reverses.
  2. At zero the subgradient interval contains the required value precisely when |z|≤λ₁. Outside that band solve the appropriate smooth first-order condition.
  3. For λ₁=0.4 and λ₂=1, raw z values 0.3, 1.2 and −1.0 produce coefficients 0, 0.4 and −0.3 respectively.

When the reasoning holds

  • The displayed closed form assumes a one-coordinate quadratic with unit curvature. General feature correlations and scaling modify the coordinate update and its interpretation.
  • Fit means, scales and penalty selection within training data only. A feature discarded at a chosen λ may still contain economically useful information in another regime.

How to read the notation
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.
Summation · ∑
The symbol asks you to add the terms for the listed items. For weights of 0.6 and 0.4 and returns of 5% and 2%, ∑ wᵢrᵢ means 0.6 × 5% + 0.4 × 2% = 3.8%. A standalone capital sigma (Σ) can also name a covariance matrix in portfolio equations.
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.

All notation examples

First example

Suppose a standardized one-feature ridge objective is (b − 2)² + λb². At λ = 0 the fitted coefficient is 2. At λ = 1, setting the derivative 2(b − 2) + 2b to zero gives b = 1. Predictions respond less to a unit change in the feature.

Why shrink coefficients

When predictors such as two related valuation ratios move together, an ordinary regression can assign one a large positive slope and the other a large negative slope while fitting historical returns closely. Small changes in data then produce large changes in the separate coefficients and unstable trades.

Ridge chooses coefficients to minimize prediction squared-error loss plus λ times their squared magnitude. Increasing λ discourages extreme coefficients. Under this standard convention the intercept is normally left unpenalized after centering. Scaling and the use of sum versus mean loss affect the numerical meaning of λ.

Derive ridge in one dimension

For centered x and y, minimize Σ(yᵢ − bxᵢ)² + λb². Differentiating gives −2Σxᵢyᵢ + 2bΣxᵢ² + 2λb = 0. Thus b = Σxᵢyᵢ/(Σxᵢ² + λ). For λ > 0 the estimate is a fraction of the unpenalized slope.

Lasso replaces b² by |b|. The kink at zero can make a coefficient exactly zero when the fit improvement from including it is too small. Correlated features can receive unstable individual selections even when a penalized model’s overall predictions are stable.

Tune on future-like data

The penalty trades bias against estimation variance. A value minimizing training squared error alone favors little or no regularization, since an unconstrained training fit can only improve that in-sample objective. Use successive time-ordered validation windows to select the penalty for the actual forecasting objective.

Fit feature means, scales and preprocessing on each training fold and apply them to validation. After a procedure has been selected, refit on permitted training history before one final evaluation. Looking at the final test to select λ turns it into another tuning sample.

Method

Estimate and compare

  1. Center and scale

    Use only the eligible training information and store transformations for later input values.

  2. Solve several penalties

    Compute slopes along a small defensible penalty set. Record which scaling and loss conventions define each value.

  3. Select with chronological validation

    Evaluate the same forecasting or trading loss across later validation origins, then freeze the selection rule for final testing.

Worked case

A noisy exposure estimate

Suppose centered training observations yield Σx² = 20 and Σxy = 30. Use the sum-of-squares ridge convention and compare λ = 0, 10 and 40. A new validation point has x = 2 and observed centered outcome 1.8.

Working

  1. Slopes are 30/20 = 1.5, 30/30 = 1 and 30/60 = 0.5. Corresponding predictions at x = 2 are 3, 2 and 1.
  2. Squared validation losses are 1.2² = 1.44, 0.2² = 0.04 and (−0.8)² = 0.64. The middle penalty is best on this date.
  3. One date is a noisy basis for selection. Average the prespecified loss across several time-ordered validation origins and report whether neighboring penalties behave similarly.

Interpretation

The raw numerical penalty 10 would imply a different amount of shrinkage if the predictor were measured in cents rather than euros without rescaling.

A common mistake

Interpret a lasso coefficient of zero as proof that the predictor has no economic effect.

How to correct it

Its contribution under the fitted penalty and other available predictors was too small to retain. Correlation, weak sample information and measurement noise can all affect selection.

Quick check
For that same objective, what is b when λ = 3?

The optimum satisfies (1 + λ)b = 2. Substituting λ = 3 gives b = 2/4. A stronger penalty produces greater shrinkage in this example.

Independent exam problem

For one centered predictor, Σx² = 12 and Σxy = −18. A candidate λ = 6 uses squared-error sum plus λb².

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate ordinary and ridge slopes, then predict y at x = −2 under each.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    If y = 2.5 in that single validation observation, which wins on squared error, and what would justify a choice?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Ordinary slope is −18/12 = −1.5 and ridge slope −18/18 = −1. Predictions are 3 and 2 respectively. The regularized prediction is less extreme.

Part B 4 marks

Both have error magnitude 0.5 and squared error 0.25. Use multiple chronological validation points under a declared objective, with any tie rule decided without touching the held-out test.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

When does a penalized linear model set a signal exactly to zero, and how does it shrink a signal that survives?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

In the standardized example, the absolute-value penalty can exactly balance the gradient at zero when the unpenalized signal is at most 0.4 in magnitude. Past that threshold, subtract 0.4 from the magnitude and divide by two for the ridge component. Production decisions still require chronological tuning and total portfolio turnover, especially when several features tell similar stories.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If the lasso penalty is removed but the ridge penalty remains one, what happens to z=0.30?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Set λ₁=0. The estimate becomes 0.30/(1+1)=0.15. The quadratic penalty shrinks continuously and generally does not produce a zero coefficient for a nonzero one-coordinate estimate. This differs from the thresholded elastic-net result.
