Baselines and evaluation

Compare with a naive forecast or equal-weight rule using identical dates. Define whether the objective is prediction error, risk-adjusted return or another economic outcome.

The starting point

A complex signal needs a comparison that could have been used at the same time. For a return forecast this might be zero or a historical mean. For a portfolio it might be an equal-weight rule over the identical universe. Apply consistent timing, rebalancing, constraints, dividends and costs to both.

Choose the scoring objective before judging success. Accurate return forecasts do not automatically create profitable trades. Tiny correctly signed moves can be too small to cover spreads, and a few large losses can dominate the average. Inspect risk, turnover and drawdowns together with the chosen headline measure.

Theory and method

Mean–variance certainty equivalent and active performance

Quadratic utility approximation · Risk aversion

A strategy earns two additional percentage points per year but is substantially more volatile. Which comparison would turn that into an explicit investor decision?

A portfolio committee can value additional expected return while imposing a penalty for variability. A mean–variance certainty equivalent expresses this trade-off in return units using an explicit risk-aversion coefficient. It is exact for normally distributed wealth or return under a correspondingly scaled exponential utility specification and useful as an approximation under other conditions. The result depends on whose risk aversion is relevant, on the measurement horizon and on whether expected returns already include costs.

How the result is built

CE ≈ μ − ½γσ²

CE is a certainty-equivalent return in decimal units over the stated horizon, μ expected net decimal return, σ its standard deviation and γ the risk-aversion coefficient for this return-based comparison.

  1. Start from a normal return with exponential utility of negative γ times return. Its exponential moment depends on −γμ + ½γ²σ².
  2. Equate utility of a certain CE with expected utility and solve for CE. Under alternative preferences interpret the expression as a mean–variance decision rule.
  3. At γ=3, a 9% mean and 20% volatility give CE=0.09−1.5×0.04=3%. A 7% mean and 12% volatility give 0.07−1.5×0.0144=4.84%.

When the reasoning holds

  • Use horizon-consistent mean and variance for comparably funded, net returns. Changing annualization or comparing gross returns to net changes the decision.
  • Material skew, illiquidity, investor constraints and non-normal tails can make a mean–variance summary incomplete. Examine losses in relevant stressed states separately.
Terms in this lesson
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
Benchmark
A reference chosen in advance for comparison, such as a market index or an equal-weight portfolio.
Percentage point
The difference between two percentages. A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises one percentage point.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
First example

A strategy earns 1.4% in one month before costs and pays 0.5% in trading costs. An equal-weight benchmark earns 1.1% gross and pays 0.1%. Net returns are 0.9% and 1.0%, giving the strategy a net active return of −0.1 percentage point.

Compare with a feasible decision

A model earns 8% per year in simulation. That figure may reflect broad equity exposure rather than the proposed forecast. An eligible benchmark should face the same trading dates, currency treatment, dividends, universe and binding restrictions. If the mandate permits holding cash, specify its return.

An equal-weight baseline is informative when a complex optimizer claims to improve allocations among those same stocks. A matched risk or market benchmark can address another question. Choose comparisons that a real investor could have followed and name the claim each tests.

Relate predictive loss to investment loss

Mean squared prediction error scores forecast distances in return units. A ranking strategy instead buys relatively high scores, so rank accuracy might matter more than the absolute level. Yet excellent ranks can translate to little profit if return spreads are too small to cover implementation costs.

For a benchmark-relative strategy, active return equals portfolio minus benchmark return. Tracking error is volatility of active returns, and the information ratio divides mean active return by that volatility under the same horizon convention. Comparing a high-risk model with a low-risk benchmark on total return alone can obscure differences in exposure.

Risk through the investment path

Compound holdings through time to measure investor wealth. Maximum drawdown is the largest observed decline from a running wealth peak to a subsequent trough. Because losses and gains compound, a 20% drop needs a 25% rise to recover the original peak.

Trading costs and cash contributions must be assigned consistently before computing drawdowns or excess returns. Short histories give fragile risk estimates. A favorable observed information ratio can be driven by a single unusual month.

Method

Construct a fair scorecard

  1. Fix comparable inputs

    Apply the same information dates, costs, currency, reinvestment and eligibility assumptions to the model and baseline.

  2. Choose the decision metric

    State whether the goal is accurate forecasts, highest net absolute wealth, excess return under a risk budget or another defined objective.

  3. Expose dispersion

    Show cumulative wealth and drawdowns along with average active returns and uncertainty across periods.

Worked case

A favorable mean with weak compounding

A model earns +20% and −15% over two months. A simple benchmark earns +3% and +1%. Both series are net of costs. Start each with €100.

Working

  1. The model’s arithmetic mean is 2.5% versus 2% for the benchmark. Mean arithmetic active return is (17% − 16%)/2 = 0.5% per month.
  2. The model ends with 100 × 1.20 × 0.85 = €102, a 2% gain. The benchmark ends at 100 × 1.03 × 1.01 = €104.03, a 4.03% gain.
  3. The model declines 15% from its €120 peak, while the benchmark reaches new peaks on both dates. Wealth and drawdown reveal risk hidden by the slightly higher arithmetic mean.

Interpretation

Which statistic should lead depends on whether the user is evaluating forecasts, an actual investment path or average active exposure.

A common mistake

A larger percentage of correctly signed stock forecasts guarantees higher net portfolio return.

How to correct it

A strategy can correctly predict many tiny moves and miss a few large declines. Analyze trade sizes, costs and magnitudes as well as directional accuracy.

Quick check
If the mandate is to beat that benchmark after costs, what is the appropriate comparison for this month?

Matching the treatment of implementation costs isolates the actual difference in investor returns. A comparison mixing gross and net numbers would favor the more expensive rule.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A strategy earns +8% and −8% while its feasible benchmark earns +1% and zero over the same two periods. Start from €100 each.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Calculate final wealth, total return and largest observed drawdown for both.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    What further information is needed before comparing risk-adjusted performance?

Part A 5 marks

Strategy wealth is 100 × 1.08 × 0.92 = €99.36, a −0.64% return and an 8% drawdown from 108. Benchmark wealth is €101, a +1% return and zero observed drawdown.

Part B 3 marks

Specify the chosen risk metric and benchmark, collect enough comparable observations and determine dependence, costs and exposure conventions. Two realized periods cannot establish a stable volatility or information ratio.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A strategy earns two additional percentage points per year but is substantially more volatile. Which comparison would turn that into an explicit investor decision?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would show the committee the extra two-point expected return beside the extra variance and state its chosen risk penalty. At γ=3 the lower-volatility benchmark has the higher certainty equivalent, 4.84% versus 3%. This conclusion depends on the assumed preferences and the forecasts. I would supplement it with liquidity, drawdown and tail-risk evidence.

Interviewer’s follow-up

At what γ do the two strategies tie before any change in costs?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Set 0.09 − 0.5γ×0.04 equal to 0.07 − 0.5γ×0.0144. The solution is γ = 2×0.02/(0.04−0.0144)=1.5625. Investors with lower γ favor the higher mean under this decision rule. At exactly that coefficient either strategy gives the same modeled certainty equivalent.
