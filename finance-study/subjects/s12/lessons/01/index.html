Investor objectives

Distinguish horizon, spending commitments, capacity for loss and willingness to accept loss. Turn the mandate into measurable constraints.

The starting point

An endowment may aim to fund teaching for decades but must also pay this year's scholarships. Its investment policy translates that purpose into deadlines, amounts, currencies and acceptable shortfalls. Different parts of the same portfolio can have different horizons.

Risk capacity concerns how much loss the investor can financially absorb while meeting commitments. Risk tolerance describes willingness to accept uncertain outcomes. A confident committee with an unavoidable payment next month can still have little capacity for loss on the cash needed for that payment.

Theory and method

Roy’s safety-first criterion

Safety-first ratio · Shortfall probability

A scholarship fund must earn 1% over the next year to cover its minimum payment. Which of two normal return forecasts gives it the better chance of doing so?

A scholarship trustee has to write a cheque even after a poor market year. Divide the distance between the expected return and the minimum acceptable return by the usual size of year-to-year fluctuations. This converts two differently scaled forecasts into comparable distances above the floor. Under a normal distribution, a greater distance places less probability below the floor. When the full distribution is unknown, mean and volatility alone cannot identify the exact chance of missing the payment.

How the result is built

SFR = (μ − L)/σ; P(R < L) = Φ(−SFR) for a normal return

R is the annual portfolio return, μ its expected value, L the required annual return and σ the annual return standard deviation, all as fractions. SFR is Roy’s unit-free safety-first ratio. P is probability and Φ is the standard normal cumulative probability.

  1. Portfolio A expects 7% with 10% volatility. A 1% floor sits (7 − 1)/10 = 0.60 standard deviations below its mean.
  2. Portfolio B expects 9% with 20% volatility. Its higher expected return places the floor only (9 − 1)/20 = 0.40 standard deviations below its mean.
  3. Under the given normal forecasts, the lower-tail probabilities are 27.43% and 34.46%. Select A if the objective is minimizing the chance of missing this particular cheque.

When the reasoning holds

  • The floor and return horizon must reflect the same payment, inflation basis and funding currency. A five-year nominal average does not establish next year’s purchasing power.
  • The normal probability calculation assumes a symmetric bell-shaped forecast. Skewed or jump-prone returns require the actual modeled tail, and loss severity is a separate decision criterion.
Terms in this lesson
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
How to read the notation
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.

All notation examples

First example

A foundation has €5m and owes €600,000 in six months. With no other cash inflows, a policy holding at least the €600,000 in suitable liquid assets protects that payment under the policy assumptions. The remaining €4.4m has a different investment horizon.

From purpose to an investable mandate

An investor's goal is often a payment rather than a percentage return. Name the beneficiary, amount, currency and date of that payment. A university owes salaries this month and may also intend to sustain a scholarship programme for decades. An average horizon obscures the near-term obligation.

An investment policy statement converts the goal into operational rules, such as minimum liquid assets at a date, permissible leverage or a range for risky exposure. Assign authority for exceptions, define monitoring frequency and specify which changes justify revisiting the policy.

Nominal returns, purchasing power and spending

A nominal return measures growth in currency units. A real return measures the goods those units can buy. The exact relation is one plus nominal return equals one plus real return times one plus inflation. Adding inflation and a real target is only an approximation.

Spending can occur before or after investment returns and can relate to opening or ending wealth. A fund aiming to preserve purchasing power after distributions must state the convention. Fees, taxes where applicable and nonrepeatable contributions also affect the required portfolio return.

Capacity, tolerance and financing constraints

Loss capacity is the financial ability to withstand losses without abandoning commitments. Willingness to accept volatility or drawdown is a preference. A client can be emotionally comfortable with losses while lacking financial capacity to absorb them, or have substantial wealth but strong aversion to uncertainty.

Define feasible actions if markets fall. An institution able to delay discretionary construction has different capacity from one that must pay pensions on time. A contingency described as possible but lacking board approval or funding should not be counted as available liquidity.

Method

Writing a measurable objective

  1. Segment the cash flows

    Put binding and discretionary obligations on a currency and date calendar, identifying genuinely secured contributions.

  2. Derive the return requirement

    Express spending, inflation and fees using one timing convention, then solve for the return that preserves the required ending balance.

  3. Test feasibility

    Compare downside balances and liquidity at payment dates, including whether an independent manager has authority to take or hedge specified risk.

Worked case

A scholarship endowment protects next year's grants

The endowment starts with €10m. It will pay €0.4m grants at year-end and wants its post-grant wealth to equal today's €10m in purchasing power. Expected inflation is 3%. Ignore fees and new gifts.

Working

  1. The required post-grant balance is €10m × 1.03 = €10.3m. Before paying grants the portfolio must therefore reach €10.7m, implying a 7% nominal return on opening wealth.
  2. If it instead earns 5%, pre-grant wealth reaches €10.5m and post-grant wealth €10.1m. In opening purchasing power that is €10.1m/1.03 ≈ €9.806m.
  3. A 7% objective is a planning requirement rather than a promised result. Earmarking a dependable payment reserve can protect this year's grants while exposing the longer-term inflation objective to different risks.

Interpretation

If grants had been due today, only €9.6m would earn returns and the required rate would differ. The timing of a liability changes the investment objective even when its face amount does not.

A common mistake

Turning a long-run target into a guaranteed one-year return can justify risk that endangers the very payment the client must make.

How to correct it

Specify horizon, downside capacity and cash reserve together, then identify contingent actions if returns disappoint.

Quick check
A university likes taking risk but must pay €1m in three months and has no backup funding. Which constraint best reflects that obligation?

The near-term bill restricts the university's capacity for loss on that portion of the money. Its longer-term goals still matter for funds beyond the scheduled payment.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A fund begins with €5m and must pay €0.3m at year-end. Its target is to preserve the opening fund's purchasing power after the grant, with 2% inflation and no fees or gifts.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Solve for required nominal return and test a 6% realized return.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    What difference would an unavoidable grant due tomorrow make to the permissible risk, even for a patient institution?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Required ending post-grant wealth is €5.1m, hence €5.4m before grants, requiring 8%. At 6% the fund grows to €5.3m before grants and €5m afterward, €0.1m below target.

Part B 2 marks

It creates an immediate liquidity and loss-capacity constraint on the grant amount. Only funds beyond that dated commitment can rely on a long investment horizon.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A scholarship fund must earn 1% over the next year to cover its minimum payment. Which of two normal return forecasts gives it the better chance of doing so?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would choose A for the 1% minimum. Its expected cushion is six percentage points and its volatility is ten, so missing the target requires a move of 0.60 standard deviations. B’s larger expected cushion buys only 0.40 standard deviations. Under the normal forecasts A still misses about 27 times in 100 modeled years. I would show that absolute risk to the trustees before they agree the mandate.

Interviewer’s follow-up

A donor will make good small shortfalls, but the fund closes after a loss exceeding 20%. Can you keep the same ranking?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The relevant floor has changed and the consequence of crossing it is severe. Under the original normal models a −20% year is 2.70 standard deviations below A’s mean and 1.45 below B’s. I would validate this extreme tail with stress scenarios and calculate the size of the expected loss on breach before relying on the ranking.
