Constraints and rebalancing

Set concentration, liquidity and trading limits. A rebalancing rule trades off drifting exposures against costs of restoring target weights.

The starting point

A portfolio can drift from its chosen risk level without anyone trading. If shares rise faster than bonds, they become a larger share of the whole. Rebalancing means trading, or directing fresh cash, to bring weights toward an agreed target.

Managers may review on a calendar or trade when a weight crosses a tolerance band. Wider bands reduce turnover and costs but permit greater drift. Check trading costs, taxes where relevant and the availability of cash before deciding how far to rebalance.

Theory and method

No-trade region under proportional transaction costs

Rebalancing band · No-trade interval

A €10m fund is 68% in shares against a 60% target. Why might the least costly correction stop short of the target even when the drift warrants trading?

A trustee dislikes departures from policy but pays a charge on every euro shifted. A small departure has a small incremental risk cost, while moving another euro costs the same rate whether the portfolio is far from target or almost there. Beyond a boundary, reducing drift earns more than it costs. Once the marginal gain from trading equals that charge, the manager can stop. The following one-period example puts both effects into the same monetary scale so the boundary can be calculated.

How the result is built

J(w) = κ(w − t)² + c × abs(w − w₀); band = [t − c/(2κ), t + c/(2κ)]

J is the modeled one-period cost as a fraction of fund value. w is the final equity weight, t its target and w₀ the starting weight. κ is the penalty on squared weight deviation and c is the cost per fraction of the fund transferred. All weights are decimals.

  1. Set t = 0.60, w₀ = 0.68, κ = 0.50 and c = 0.02. Each additional unit of weight drift above target increases the penalty at a marginal rate of 2κ(w − t).
  2. Equating that marginal penalty to the transfer cost gives a two-point half-band. A starting weight below 58% or above 62% moves to the nearest boundary.
  3. From 68%, selling six percentage points transfers €600,000 and costs €12,000. Residual drift carries a €2,000 modeled penalty. The combined €14,000 compares with €32,000 for no trade or €16,000 for returning fully to target.

When the reasoning holds

  • The cost is proportional to the amount transferred and the drift penalty is this specified quadratic approximation. Fixed ticket charges, taxes or future contributions can change both the trigger and trade size.
  • Portfolio weights in this example use the fixed pre-trade capital base for sizing, with costs budgeted separately. A legally binding hard limit must be checked independently.
Terms in this lesson
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
First example

A portfolio starts with €60 in shares and €40 in bonds. Shares rise to €72 while bonds stay at €40. The share weight reaches 64.29%. Restoring a 60% target requires €67.20 of shares, so €4.80 moves from shares to bonds before costs.

When a portfolio breaches a limit

Strategic weights drift when assets earn different returns. To find the new weight, grow each initial holding by its own return, then divide by the new total. Adding a security's return to its original percentage weight is not a valid weight calculation.

A hard concentration cap and a discretionary target band have different consequences. The policy should specify the trigger, the destination weight, who may trade and any exception. Returning to the nearest boundary requires less trading than returning to the central target.

Turnover costs and implementation

Rebalancing can require bid–offer costs, fees, taxes and market impact. If costs scale with traded value, a bigger or more frequent correction is dearer. Transferring fresh contributions into underweight assets or meeting withdrawals from overweight assets can reduce asset sales.

Some assets cannot trade on demand. A portfolio with a 30% private-asset target may breach its band when liquid markets fall even if private marks update only quarterly. Committed future capital calls and the price impact of forced sales should influence the execution plan.

Rebalancing and investment beliefs

A fixed-mix policy buys relative losers and sells relative winners. It enforces a chosen risk profile but can underperform a trending buy-and-hold portfolio. Its primary justification is consistency with investor objectives, not a universal bonus from repeatedly trading.

Risks can also change without material weight drift. A portfolio still at its original equity weight may become riskier when equity volatility rises or bonds and equities become more correlated. A weight-based rule should be supplemented by the risk and liquidity checks the mandate actually specifies.

Method

Preparing executable trade orders

  1. Revalue holdings

    Include dividends, external flows and current valuations before testing the mandate's bands and caps.

  2. Choose the destination

    Apply the policy's stated central target or boundary using total portfolio value after planned flows.

  3. Check cash and costs

    Use incoming funds efficiently, quantify gross purchases and sales, and verify trading limits and post-trade weights.

Worked case

A cash contribution reduces turnover

A fund holds €72m shares and €48m bonds, initially the 60/40 target. Shares rise 25% while bonds stay flat, producing €90m shares and €48m bonds. It then receives €12m cash for investment and wants to restore target weights.

Working

  1. Before the contribution the share weight is €90m/€138m = 65.22%. Returning immediately to 60% would require selling shares down to €82.8m, a €7.2m sale.
  2. After the contribution portfolio value is €150m. Target shares are €90m and target bonds €60m. Investing all €12m new cash in bonds restores the target without selling any shares.
  3. If the policy required an immediate adjustment before the known contribution could settle, the first sale might still be necessary. The apparent saving depends on actual timing and discretion.

Interpretation

A written rule for using near-term flows can reduce unnecessary trades while preserving a hard bound on the risk the client is willing to carry.

A common mistake

Treating a target weight and an absolute cap as interchangeable can delay a mandatory sale or trigger avoidable turnover.

How to correct it

Quote the policy's exact trigger, tolerance and destination, and check which constraints apply before and after cash flows.

Quick check
A €200 portfolio holds €130 in shares and €70 in bonds. If its policy requires a return to a 60% share target now, what trade is required before costs?

The target share holding is €120. Moving €10 of the existing €130 to bonds restores €120/€200 = 60% without changing total capital.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A fund holds €55m shares and €45m bonds with a 50% central share target and an upper bound of 54%. A €5m cash contribution settles immediately. The policy tests after contributions and returns to target only on a breach.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate the share weight after placing the contribution in bonds. Does the upper trigger require a sale?

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    If the policy instead demanded an immediate return to 50%, find the transfer needed after investing the contribution in bonds.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Total is €105m and shares are €55m, giving 52.38%. This is below the 54% upper bound, so the stated rule does not require a sale although the share weight exceeds the target.

Part B 3 marks

Target share value is €52.5m. Sell €2.5m shares and buy €2.5m bonds, ending with €52.5m in each class before fees.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A €10m fund is 68% in shares against a 60% target. Why might the least costly correction stop short of the target even when the drift warrants trading?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would sell €600,000 and finish at 62% under the given mandate. The excess equity weight initially carries a marginal penalty above the transaction charge. After six percentage points have been sold, the two rates are equal. Taking off the last two points would consume more in fees than the model values their removal. I would record both the actual expense and the assumption used to value drift.

Interviewer’s follow-up

A committed €1m contribution arrives tomorrow and can be put entirely in bonds. What happens if the risk committee permits waiting one day?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Equity remains €6.8m while total capital becomes €11m, so its weight falls to 61.82%, already inside the band. Under a one-day wait with no intervening price move or hard limit breach, the contribution eliminates the modeled need for a sale. Verify receipt and recalculate from actual market values before closing the exception.
