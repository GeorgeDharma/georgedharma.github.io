Measuring returns

Chain subperiod returns for a time-weighted measure. Money-weighted performance reflects the timing and size of contributions or withdrawals.

The starting point

A client who contributes heavily just before a decline can lose money while the manager's sequence of percentage returns is positive. Time-weighted return divides the record at external cash flows and chains the growth factors. It measures the experience of one unit invested throughout.

Money-weighted return instead solves for the discount rate that equates dated contributions and withdrawals with ending value. It reflects how much capital actually experienced each period. Record the dates, currencies and treatment of fees before comparing results.

Theory and method

Modified Dietz return

Time-weighted external cash flows · Money-weighted return approximation

A fund begins a 30-day month at 100, receives 40 at the close of day 15 and ends at 146. What return can you estimate, and what one extra valuation would improve it?

An investor adds money in the middle of a month. Subtracting that contribution from the change in wealth isolates investment gain, but dividing by beginning assets understates the capital used to earn it. Modified Dietz adds the contribution to the denominator in proportion to the fraction of the period it was available. This produces a capital-weighted approximation from limited records. A valuation immediately before the flow lets the analyst measure each investment interval separately and link the returns.

How the result is built

RMD = (V₁ − V₀ − Σᵢ Cᵢ)/(V₀ + Σᵢ wᵢCᵢ), with wᵢ = (D − dᵢ)/D

RMD is the period’s Modified Dietz return. V₀ and V₁ are opening and closing portfolio values. Cᵢ is signed external flow i, positive for a deposit. D is days in the period and dᵢ the closing day of that flow. wᵢ is the fraction of the period remaining. Σᵢ sums all flows. Values and flows use the same currency units.

  1. The 30-day gain is 146 − 100 − 40 = 6. The day-15 deposit has weight (30 − 15)/30 = 0.50, giving a capital denominator of 100 + 20 = 120.
  2. The estimate is 6/120 = 5.00%. If the same deposit, ending value and gain instead relate to a day-3 flow, the denominator is 136 and the estimate is 4.41%.
  3. Suppose the actual mark just before the day-15 deposit is 101. The second interval starts at 141, so the exact linked time-weighted result is (101/100) × (146/141) − 1 = 4.58%.

When the reasoning holds

  • State whether flows occur at the start or close of each day. Moving a large flow across a valuation boundary changes its weight and potentially the subperiod to which performance belongs.
  • A large flow combined with a volatile return path can make the estimate differ materially from the linked return. External withdrawals can also produce a very small denominator that makes the estimate unstable.
Terms in this lesson
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Discount rate
Rate used to express a future payment in today’s money, reflecting time and the relevant risk.
Net present value (NPV)
Today’s value of incoming cash minus today’s value of outgoing cash.
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
How to read the notation
Subscripts · S₀ and S₁
A small index names the time or item. If S₀ = €40 and S₁ = €42, the price moves from €40 at time 0 to €42 at time 1. A subscript i can label a company instead of a date.
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.

All notation examples

First example

A fund starts with €100, earns 20%, receives €180 and then loses 10%. It ends at €270 after total contributions of €280. Its linked time-weighted return is 1.20 × 0.90 − 1 = 8%, although the client's net cash result is a €10 loss.

The two return questions

A time-weighted measure asks what happened to one unit continuously invested through each subperiod, independent of client contributions and withdrawals. Value the portfolio immediately before external flows, calculate each subperiod's growth factor, then multiply the factors and subtract one.

A money-weighted measure answers what return reconciles the investor's actual dated contributions, distributions and ending wealth. It depends on whether large amounts arrived before a good or bad period. That dependence can be appropriate when the investor controls cash flows or cannot redeploy capital freely.

Solving for an internal rate

For equal annual spacing, place investor contributions as negative and withdrawals or terminal wealth as positive. The internal rate of return is the discount rate making their net present value zero. With irregular dates, use actual year fractions and a declared day-count convention.

Some cash-flow patterns with multiple sign changes have multiple rates or no economically meaningful unique rate. Inspect the cash timeline and the discounted-value profile when a reported internal rate looks unusual. A cash-weighted return also depends on consistent interim valuations if capital remains invested.

Gross, net and annualization choices

Use dividends, interest and fees in the appropriate measure. A manager's transaction costs or investment management fees are generally part of the relevant gross/net return definition. A client's subscription is an external flow that should be neutralized in a time-weighted calculation.

Annualizing a multiperiod result uses compound growth, raising its growth factor to one divided by elapsed years. Multiplying a volatile monthly average by twelve does not deliver the investor's realized annual compound return. Start and end dates anchor any performance claim.

Method

Reconstructing investor performance

  1. Mark the cash events

    Record who controls each flow, its exact date and portfolio value immediately before and after it.

  2. Link subperiods

    Remove external flows at the boundary, then multiply the pure investment growth factors rather than adding percentage returns.

  3. Solve the money equation

    Discount the investor's dated cash contributions and receipts to one date, then interpret divergence in terms of capital exposed during each period.

Worked case

A large deposit before a weaker year

A manager receives €100 at the start of year one. It grows to €120. The client then contributes €105, making year-two opening wealth €225. The second year ends at €236.25, with no other flows.

Working

  1. Manager time-weighted growth is 1.20 × (€236.25/€225) = 1.26, a two-year return of 26%. Its compound annual equivalent is √1.26 − 1 ≈ 12.25%.
  2. The investor's annual rate solves 100(1 + r)² + 105(1 + r) = 236.25. The positive growth factor is about 1.0992, giving an annual money-weighted return near 9.92%.
  3. More money participated in the lower-return 5% second year. Time-weighted and money-weighted measures both use the observed cash history but answer different performance questions.

Interpretation

Check the money-weighted result by growing the first contribution for two years and the later contribution for only one. Applying the same two-year growth to both overstates ending wealth.

A common mistake

Treating an external deposit as performance inflates return whenever new money arrives.

How to correct it

Split valuation periods at external flows or, for a money-weighted measure, enter each flow with its actual investor sign and date.

Quick check
A portfolio gains 10% in the first subperiod and loses 10% in the next. What linked time-weighted return applies regardless of an external contribution between them?

One unit grows to 1.10 and then falls to 0.99. The product of the growth factors gives a 1% loss. The cash contribution affects the investor's money-weighted result.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

An investor contributes €100 at the start. After one year the account is €120 and a further €100 is deposited. One year later it is €231, with no other flows.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find total and annualized time-weighted return.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Find the annual money-weighted return and explain why it differs.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Subperiod returns are 20% and €231/€220 − 1 = 5%. Linked return is 26% and annualized return is √1.26 − 1 ≈ 12.25%.

Part B 4 marks

Solve 100(1 + r)² + 100(1 + r) = 231. The growth factor is 1.10, giving r = 10%. More capital experienced the lower second-year return.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A fund begins a 30-day month at 100, receives 40 at the close of day 15 and ends at 146. What return can you estimate, and what one extra valuation would improve it?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I can estimate 5% with Modified Dietz. I remove the 40 deposit from the gain and credit the fund with using that money for half a month. For manager comparison, I would request the value immediately before the deposit. A value of 101 splits the month into a 1% gain followed by a 3.55% gain, which compound to 4.58%. I would label both methods and the flow timing.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The 40 came from selling securities inside the same fund and stayed in its cash account. Does it still enter as an external cash flow?

Compare with the follow-up answer

No. A sale exchanges one asset already inside the fund for another. The external cash flow is zero. If the consolidated fund really opened at 100 and closed at 146 with nothing entering or leaving, the portfolio return is 46%. I would verify the cash account is included in both valuations before accepting that result.
