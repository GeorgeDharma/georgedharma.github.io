Active and passive choices

An index can offer broad exposure while embedding concentration and turnover. An active strategy must justify its extra risk and cost against an appropriate comparator.

The starting point

An index records how a specified collection of investments performs under published rules. A tracker follows those rules while an active manager deliberately differs from them. Choosing an index still involves a decision about markets, weighting, concentration and trading when membership changes.

Compare an active strategy with an investable benchmark that fits its mandate. Work with returns after recurring fees and relevant trading costs. A gross advantage can disappear after costs, and a higher return from bearing more market risk may say little about security selection.

Theory and method

Cremers–Petajisto Active Share and tracking error

Holdings-based activeness · Active risk

Two funds each move 20% of the benchmark from one stock to another. Can one have seven times the benchmark-relative volatility of the other?

An index fund owns two stocks equally. A manager raises the first to 70% and cuts the second to 30%. A fifth of the money has moved to a different holding, regardless of how the stocks behave. A pharmaceutical stock exchanged for a close peer can move almost in tandem with the security sold. A switch between unrelated businesses can create a much wider range of outcomes relative to the index. Holdings and relative returns answer different questions about the same trade.

How the result is built

AS = ½ Σᵢ abs(wPᵢ − wBᵢ); TE = SD(RP − RB)

AS is the fraction of holdings differing from benchmark. Σᵢ sums over all securities i. wPᵢ and wBᵢ are their portfolio and benchmark weights. RP and RB are same-period portfolio and benchmark returns. SD is standard deviation and TE is tracking error over that horizon.

  1. The absolute weight departures are 20 and 20 percentage points. Halving their sum avoids counting the same money on the sale and purchase, giving 20% Active Share.
  2. Relative return is 0.20 × (first stock’s return minus second stock’s return). If each stock has annual volatility 15% and their correlation is 0.98, the return difference has variance 2 × 0.15² × (1 − 0.98) = 0.0009.
  3. The spread volatility is 3%, so fund tracking error is 0.60%. With zero stock correlation, spread volatility becomes 21.21% and tracking error becomes 4.24%, more than seven times as large.

When the reasoning holds

  • The simple holdings calculation uses two fully funded long-only portfolios measured at the same instant against a suitable index. Derivatives and offsetting leverage need explicit exposure treatment.
  • The ex ante tracking error uses future covariance estimates and fixed current weights. Realized tracking error uses the observed variability of relative returns and can diverge when correlations change.
Terms in this lesson
Benchmark
A reference chosen in advance for comparison, such as a market index or an equal-weight portfolio.
Percentage point
The difference between two percentages. A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises one percentage point.
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
First example

A tracker earns 6.0% gross and costs 0.1 percentage points for the year. An active portfolio earns 6.6% gross and costs 0.9 points. Net returns under this simple convention are 5.9% and 5.7%, so the tracker leads by 0.2 points.

Passive investing still requires design choices

A capitalization-weighted index gives the largest market values the most weight. Firms with high share prices relative to fundamentals can therefore dominate exposures, and index membership criteria may omit small or less accessible firms. Choosing an index selects a portfolio mandate.

Replication can hold every index constituent, sample similar exposures or use contracts. Fees, taxes, timing of dividend receipts, cash balances and index changes create tracking difference. A small average cost gap and high volatility of the gap describe different implementation qualities.

What is an active bet?

An active weight is the portfolio weight minus the benchmark weight. For a fully invested portfolio and benchmark with the same universe, active weights sum to zero, but positive and negative differences can still be large. Active share is one half of the sum of their absolute differences.

High active share describes different holdings. It does not guarantee high skill or benchmark-relative volatility. Two different holdings can move almost identically. Conversely a modest active position in a volatile asset can contribute substantial tracking risk.

Skill, cost and benchmarking

Compare gross and net returns with the same currency, period and fee treatment. If active managers collectively hold the market, their asset-weighted performance before costs must aggregate to the market under a common universe. Higher implementation costs then matter to the average after-cost comparison.

A manager's outperformance can reflect exposure to broad risks, deliberate security selection or chance. Select an investable comparator consistent with the mandate before inspecting outcomes. A convenient weak benchmark can inflate apparent skill without changing the actual investment portfolio.

Method

Assessing active versus indexed implementation

  1. Define the alternative

    Select a feasible representative index product, its holdings rules, fee and benchmark-return convention.

  2. Measure differences

    Compute active weights, active share, gross active return and all-in net active return under common accounting.

  3. Attribute the outcome

    Assess whether systematic exposures, concentration, transaction costs or distinctive security decisions explain the gap, using evidence over a meaningful horizon.

Worked case

Three names, one active decision

A benchmark holds 50% in A, 30% in B and 20% in C. A manager holds 65%, 20% and 15%. The three shares return 8%, 4% and zero over one period, including dividends.

Working

  1. Active weights are +15, −10 and −5 percentage points, summing to zero. Active share is half the sum of their absolute values, or 15%.
  2. The benchmark earns 0.5 × 8% + 0.3 × 4% = 5.2%, while the active portfolio earns 0.65 × 8% + 0.2 × 4% = 6%. Gross active return is 0.8 percentage points.
  3. With an active implementation cost of 0.9 points and a tracker cost of 0.1 points, both net 5.1% under the simple additive cost convention. Distinct positions created a gross advantage exactly consumed by extra cost.

Interpretation

Real fee schedules can be charged on changing asset values. State the convention. The case uses a direct percentage-point deduction for transparency.

A common mistake

Comparing an active fund net of fees with a hypothetical index gross of all implementation costs confuses manager choice with unavailable execution.

How to correct it

Report the gross index and a feasible net tracker separately, and match the same currency and total-return convention.

Quick check
A comparable tracker earns 5.8% after costs. An active fund earns 6.5% before an annual charge of 0.9 percentage points. Under this simple fee convention, how does the active fund finish?

The active net return is 5.6%. Subtracting the tracker's 5.8% gives minus 0.2 percentage points. A fair evaluation should then examine the risks taken by each.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

A benchmark holds 40%, 40%, 20%. An active fund holds 55%, 25%, 20%. The assets return 10%, 2%, and 0%. Fund cost is 1.0 point and tracker cost 0.2 points in the period.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Find active share and gross active return.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Compare net outcomes and explain why this period does not establish persistent skill.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Active weights are +15, −15 and zero points, so active share is 15%. Benchmark return is 4.8%, active gross is 6.0%, giving 1.2 points gross advantage.

Part B 3 marks

Active nets 5.0% and the tracker 4.6%, a 0.4-point advantage. A favourable return on an overweight name in one period could reflect common exposure or chance. Repeated risk-adjusted evidence is needed.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Two funds each move 20% of the benchmark from one stock to another. Can one have seven times the benchmark-relative volatility of the other?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Yes. A 20-point overweight funded by a 20-point underweight gives 20% Active Share in either case. What the client actually experiences depends on the volatility of the return spread between the pair. The highly correlated pair produces 0.60% forecast tracking error and the unrelated pair produces 4.24%. I would ask for a benchmark justification and a contribution-to-risk report alongside the holdings statistic.

Interviewer’s follow-up

An otherwise perfect index tracker charges a constant 0.40 percentage point each year in this model. What would each statistic show?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The holdings match, so Active Share is zero. If the annual return difference is exactly −0.40 percentage point in every observation, its standard deviation is also zero. The average tracking difference is −0.40 percentage point. Reporting that difference shows the fee drag even though neither measure of variability or holdings departure detects it.
