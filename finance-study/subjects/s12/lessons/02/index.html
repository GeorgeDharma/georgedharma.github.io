Asset allocation

Choose a long-run mix of equity, bonds, real assets and cash. Use the investor’s future obligations and currency as reference points.

The starting point

Strategic asset allocation sets a long-run mix across assets such as shares, bonds and cash. It matters because each asset responds differently to growth, inflation and interest rates. The relevant risk for a pension fund includes its ability to meet benefits as they fall due.

Start with liabilities, needed liquidity and base currency. Then examine how candidate assets behave together. Expected portfolio return is the weighted average of expected asset returns. Portfolio volatility also depends on how their returns move together, so averaging volatilities is generally inadequate.

Theory and method

Markowitz global minimum-variance portfolio

Two-asset minimum-variance weight

Why can adding an asset with 20% volatility to one with 10% volatility reduce total risk, and exactly how much should a variance-minimizing investor hold?

A treasurer currently invests every euro in asset B. Moving a small amount to asset A gives up some B exposure while adding a return that does not fluctuate in lockstep with B. Near the all-B position, this substitution changes their joint exposure immediately. The standalone variance of the small holding in A enters quadratically with its weight. The balance changes as the holding grows, producing a particular mix with the lowest combined variance.

How the result is built

w* = (σB² − c)/(σA² + σB² − 2c)

w* is the fraction in A that minimizes portfolio variance, leaving 1 − w* in B. σA and σB are annual standard deviations of decimal returns. c is the covariance between their annual returns, in squared-return units.

  1. Take σA = 0.20, σB = 0.10 and correlation 0.25. Their covariance equals 0.25 × 0.20 × 0.10 = 0.005.
  2. For weight w in A, variance is w² × 0.04 + (1 − w)² × 0.01 + 2w(1 − w) × 0.005. Differentiating gives 0.08w − 0.01.
  3. Setting that slope to zero gives w = 0.125. The variance is 0.009375 and its square root is 9.68%, below B’s 10%. The positive curvature confirms a minimum.

When the reasoning holds

  • Both assets are fully invested and the two estimated covariances and variances refer to the same horizon and currency. A currency overlay or leverage changes the allocation problem.
  • The unrestricted formula can imply short selling. If borrowing or shorting is forbidden and the stationary point lies outside zero to one, evaluate the feasible boundary.

Terms in this lesson
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Interest rate
The price of borrowing money or the return for lending it, quoted as a percentage over a stated period.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
How to read the notation
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.

All notation examples

First example

A €10m portfolio targets 60% equity with expected annual return of 7% and 40% bonds with expected return of 3%. Its expected return before costs is 0.60 × 7% + 0.40 × 3% = 5.4%. This is an assumption-weighted estimate, not a promised payment.

Expected return and covariance

For fixed starting weights, expected portfolio return is the sum of each asset's expected return times its weight. For two assets, variance also includes each weighted variance and twice the product of weights, volatilities and their correlation. Take a square root to report volatility.

Correlation describes how returns move together, not whether either asset is individually safe. Two individually volatile assets can partly offset fluctuations. Correlations estimated during calm years can rise under stress, especially where apparently distinct assets share economic exposures.

An efficient set depends on assumptions

A mean–variance efficient mix offers the highest assumed expected return for its estimated risk level, or the lowest risk for its expected return. The result depends on the input sample, investment horizon, allowed short sales and leverage, and restrictions on illiquid holdings.

Expected return estimates are noisy. Small changes can push an unconstrained optimization toward large concentrations. Inspect allocations under alternative estimates and consider transparent benchmark weights or bounds. A mathematically optimal output can be impractical once turnover, taxes and liquidity matter.

Liabilities change the relevant measure

A pension scheme may care about uncertainty in its surplus, defined as assets minus the value of promised benefits. An asset that fluctuates in isolation can hedge inflation-linked or long-dated obligations if its value moves with those liabilities.

Distinguish strategic long-run exposures from tactical short-term deviations and the manager selection within each asset class. Choose a currency and liability horizon before interpreting portfolio risk. A two-year allocation exercise and a thirty-year pension question need different cash-flow scenarios.

Method

Evaluating a proposed mix

  1. Calculate weighted means

    Use consistent expected returns, currencies and horizons and test that weights sum to the intended net investment.

  2. Calculate joint risk

    Include the covariance cross term before taking a square root and inspect how the estimate responds to a stressed correlation.

  3. Test the obligation

    Compare projected asset outcomes with liabilities, liquidity requirements and actual implementation limits before accepting the allocation.

Worked case

Diversification can disappear in a shared shock

A fund allocates 50% to asset A and 50% to B. Expected annual returns are 8% and 4%, and volatilities are 20% and 10%. Assume annual correlation zero for the base case.

Working

  1. Expected return is 0.5 × 8% + 0.5 × 4% = 6%. Variance at zero correlation is 0.5² × 0.20² + 0.5² × 0.10² = 0.0125, giving volatility of 11.18%.
  2. If correlation rises to 0.8 with the other inputs fixed, the cross term adds 2 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.20 × 0.10 × 0.8 = 0.008. Volatility becomes √0.0205 = 14.32%.
  3. The return assumption remains 6% in this sensitivity. The loss of diversification comes from more synchronized outcomes, not from either asset's individual volatility increasing.

Interpretation

The policy committee might review whether both labels hide exposure to the same interest-rate, growth or liquidity shock before increasing either weight.

A common mistake

Averaging the two volatilities implicitly gives the risk for perfectly positively correlated returns under nonnegative weights.

How to correct it

Compute covariance from the stated correlation and take the square root only after adding all variance terms.

Quick check
A fund targets 25% shares expected to earn 8% and 75% bonds expected to earn 4%. What annual return follows from these assumptions?

Apply the target weights. Shares contribute 2 percentage points and bonds 3, totaling 5%. Determining risk would also require information about variability and co-movement.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

A fund holds 60% in shares with 10% expected return and 20% volatility, and 40% in bonds with 3% expected return and 10% volatility. Correlation is zero.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Find expected return and portfolio volatility.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Find volatility if correlation rises to one while expected returns stay unchanged. Explain the increase.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Expected return is 0.6 × 10% + 0.4 × 3% = 7.2%. Variance is 0.6² × 0.20² + 0.4² × 0.10² = 0.016, so volatility is about 12.65%.

Part B 3 marks

With perfectly co-moving returns, volatility equals 0.6 × 20% + 0.4 × 10% = 16%. The offset from imperfect co-movement disappears.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why can adding an asset with 20% volatility to one with 10% volatility reduce total risk, and exactly how much should a variance-minimizing investor hold?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would put 12.5% in A under the specified estimates. At the original position, the covariance added with A is below the variance removed from B. At the minimum, another small switch between the assets has zero first-order effect on risk. That conclusion answers a variance-only objective. A spending plan that needs a particular expected return or has inflation-linked liabilities would require additional inputs.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The correlation estimate rises to 0.80. What changes for an investor restricted to long positions?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The covariance becomes 0.016, above B’s variance of 0.01. The unrestricted weight is (0.01 − 0.016)/(0.04 + 0.01 − 0.032) = −1/3. Since shorting is unavailable, the lowest feasible variance is at zero in A and 100% in B. Estimation uncertainty matters when a small portfolio weight motivates a costly trade.
