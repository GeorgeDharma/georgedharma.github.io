Risk-adjusted evaluation

Compare Sharpe ratio, tracking error and information ratio. A short sample and an unsuitable benchmark can make apparently precise ranks unstable.

The starting point

A high-return portfolio may have taken substantial risk. The Sharpe ratio divides average return above a risk-free rate by the volatility of the portfolio's excess returns. It evaluates the reward for total risk relative to that cash alternative.

For a manager hired to track or outperform a benchmark, tracking error is the volatility of portfolio return minus benchmark return. The information ratio divides average active return by tracking error. Use consistent frequencies and comparable mandates, and examine sample uncertainty.

Theory and method

Jensen’s alpha

CAPM regression intercept · Factor-adjusted abnormal return

A fund returns 11% when the market gains 8% and cash earns 2%. With a market beta of 1.3, how much of its result is unexplained by that exposure?

A client can buy a market index and hold or borrow cash. A manager who takes more market exposure than the index should usually gain more when the market rises and lose more when it falls. Jensen’s alpha asks whether returns exceed what the same market exposure would explain after allowing for the cash rate. A single observation gives a realized abnormal return. Estimating a persistent intercept requires many observations, a defensible exposure model and an estimate of statistical uncertainty.

How the result is built

RP,t − Rf,t = α + β(RM,t − Rf,t) + εt

RP,t, Rf,t and RM,t are portfolio, risk-free and market returns in period t using the same frequency. β is the unit-free sensitivity to the market excess return. α is the fitted average unexplained return per period, and εt is that period’s residual around the fitted line.

  1. The market excess return is 8% − 2% = 6%. At beta 1.3 the modeled excess return is 1.3 × 6% = 7.8%, giving a total modeled return of 9.8%.
  2. The realized return above that benchmark is 11% − 9.8% = 1.2 percentage points. The raw 3-point lead over the market includes 1.8 points from additional market exposure.
  3. Suppose the mandate also has a 0.4 loading on a value-style factor earning 3% after market adjustment. That adds 1.2 points of modeled return. In the expanded model this observation has no unexplained gain.

When the reasoning holds

  • Beta and factor exposures must be estimated over comparable periods and treated as stable over the assessment horizon. Market timing or nonlinear exposures can make a single beta a poor description.
  • Include the fee basis and a benchmark the client could identify in advance. Selection after seeing returns or ignoring relevant style exposure can exaggerate a measured intercept.
Terms in this lesson
Benchmark
A reference chosen in advance for comparison, such as a market index or an equal-weight portfolio.
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
Regression
A way of estimating how an outcome varies with one or more observed inputs.
Standard error
An estimate of how much a statistic would vary across repeated samples under the assumed sampling process.
Correlation
A measure of how closely two series move together in a linear pattern.
Percentage point
The difference between two percentages. A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises one percentage point.
How to read the notation
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.

All notation examples

First example

A fund averages 8% against a 2% risk-free rate and has 12% excess-return volatility on a consistent annual basis. Its Sharpe ratio is 0.5. If its benchmark earns 7% and tracking error is 2%, its information ratio is also 0.5, from a different comparison.

Which risk did the client delegate?

A Sharpe ratio measures mean return in excess of a risk-free return divided by volatility of those excess returns. It addresses whether the total risky portfolio delivered attractive excess return for variability. A mandate that closely tracks an index raises a different question about departures from that index.

An information ratio divides mean active return by tracking error, the standard deviation of periodic active returns. A benchmark-hugging fund can have small absolute active returns and a high information ratio if deviations are consistent. That ratio alone cannot tell the client how much total wealth or funding risk it bears.

Alpha, beta and omitted exposures

A one-factor regression relates excess portfolio return to excess market return. Beta describes the portfolio's market sensitivity. Alpha is the intercept, the average return remaining after accounting for that market exposure under the model. A familiar single-factor estimate can miss systematic exposures such as size or duration.

A positive estimated alpha can reflect chance, omitted risk or measurement choices. Inspect its uncertainty, the sample period, style consistency, net fees and whether the chosen benchmark could be held at realistic cost. Testing many strategies can produce a flattering winner by luck.

Sampling and scaling

For an illustrative sample of independent active returns with stable variance, the standard error of mean active return is tracking error divided by the square root of the observation count. Serial correlation, overlapping horizons or smoothed valuations reduce the effective amount of independent information.

Square-root-of-time scaling of volatility and ratios relies on assumptions about dependence and stationarity. Use periods consistent across risk-free rates, benchmark and manager. A short record expressed to three decimal places does not make its ranking precise.

Method

Evaluating a reported track record

  1. Choose the comparator

    Use a risk-free rate for total portfolio reward or an investable policy benchmark for delegated active management, in the client's relevant currency.

  2. Match the units

    Use return differentials, standard deviations and time frequencies consistently. Document gross or net performance and any serial dependence.

  3. Examine uncertainty

    Compare the mean with its sampling error and inspect downside, style changes and observable factor exposures before drawing a skill inference.

Worked case

A precise-looking active record

A manager has twelve nonoverlapping monthly active returns with mean 0.20% and sample tracking error 1.20% per month. Assume independence and stable distribution solely for the illustrative sampling calculation.

Working

  1. Monthly information ratio is 0.20/1.20 = 0.167. Under the stated scaling assumptions the approximate annualized ratio is 0.167 × √12 ≈ 0.58.
  2. Estimated standard error of the mean is 1.20%/√12 ≈ 0.346 percentage points per month. The mean is only about 0.58 standard errors above zero.
  3. The positive estimated ratio and weak statistical precision can coexist. More independent observations and a stable mandate are needed before treating a ranking against another manager as reliable.

Interpretation

If the fund holds illiquid assets valued infrequently, observed monthly volatility may be artificially smooth. Inspect valuation practices before relying on the resulting information ratio.

A common mistake

Interpreting a high Sharpe ratio as proof of beating a benchmark compares return with cash even when the mandate assigned market exposure.

How to correct it

Calculate information ratio or appropriate factor-adjusted alpha as well, then show both the economic size and sampling uncertainty of the result.

Quick check
A portfolio's average annual active return is 1.2% and its annual tracking error is 3%. What is its information ratio?

Divide the active return by tracking error using consistent units. A 0.4 ratio summarizes this sample and benchmark. It does not show whether the record will repeat.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A fund earns 9% on average, cash 3% and its benchmark 8%. Annual excess-return volatility is 12% and annual tracking error 2%. All are comparable figures.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate Sharpe and information ratios and interpret their numerators.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Assume four independent annual active returns with sample mean 1% and tracking error 2%. Estimate standard error and explain its implication.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Sharpe is (9 − 3)/12 = 0.50. Information ratio is (9 − 8)/2 = 0.50. These equal numbers use different rewards, with six points above cash versus one above the benchmark.

Part B 3 marks

Standard error is 2%/√4 = 1 percentage point. The mean is only one estimated standard error above zero, so the short record gives weak grounds for a firm skill judgment.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A fund returns 11% when the market gains 8% and cash earns 2%. With a market beta of 1.3, how much of its result is unexplained by that exposure?

Compare with a spoken answer

The market-only comparison leaves 1.2 percentage points for this observation. A raw comparison with the index gives three points, but the fund took 30% more market exposure and the market paid six points above cash. I would show the exposure-adjusted comparison, then estimate a time-series intercept net of fees across a suitable history. One strong period cannot establish that the manager repeatedly adds value.

Interviewer’s follow-up

An estimated monthly alpha is 0.20% and its standard error is 0.18%. Would you present the point estimate as established skill?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The estimated intercept is about 1.11 standard errors above zero. An approximate two-sided 95% interval using 1.96 standard errors is −0.153% to +0.553% per month. The data permit both a positive and a modest negative alpha under the model. I would also examine benchmark choice, the length of the history and dependence across the residuals.
