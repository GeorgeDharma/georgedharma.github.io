Interest margins

Income on assets and funding costs reprice at different speeds. Higher market rates can help or hurt earnings depending on the bank’s exposures.

The starting point

Banks earn interest on assets and pay interest on deposits and other funding. Net interest income is the difference in currency amounts. Net interest margin divides that income by average interest-earning assets. Compare like periods and decide which assets earn interest before calculating the ratio.

A policy-rate rise can change each side at a different speed. Fixed-rate loans may keep paying the old rate while funding becomes more expensive. Floating-rate assets may reset quickly while deposit rates change slowly. The direction of the effect depends on repricing terms, balances and customer responses.

Theory and method

Deposit beta and repricing-gap analysis

Net interest income sensitivity · Pass-through threshold

A bank carries €50m of immediately floating loans and €80m of administered-rate deposits. At what deposit pass-through would a rate rise cease to improve income?

A policy rise changes loan coupons automatically when their reference rates reset, but the bank decides how much of the change to pass through to deposit customers. Deposit beta measures that pass-through over a specified period. A bank with more deposits than resetting loans can still gain if deposits reprice only partly. Dependence on cheap deposits becomes dangerous when competition raises their pass-through or customers switch to a costlier product.

How the result is built

ΔNII = Δr(AβA − LβL), and βL* = AβA/L

ΔNII is annualized euro change in net interest income, Δr the decimal reference-rate move, A resetting asset balance, L repricing deposit balance, βA and βL their pass-through fractions, and βL* the break-even deposit beta.

  1. For €50m of fully resetting loans and €80m deposits, a two-point rate rise adds €1m to annualized gross loan income.
  2. With deposit beta 0.50, cost rises €80m × 0.02 × 0.50 = €0.8m, leaving €0.2m additional NII.
  3. Set the change to zero. Break-even deposit beta is 50/80 = 0.625. At 0.75 the bank instead loses €0.2m annual income.

When the reasoning holds

  • The first-order arithmetic assumes immediate repricing, stable balances and no rate floors, caps, new hedges or customer mix shifts. A time lag alters first-year income.
  • A low pass-through can cause customers to leave for other products. Replacing funds at market rates can overturn the mechanical gain even if posted deposit rates remain low.
Terms in this lesson
Margin
In business accounts, profit expressed relative to sales. In derivatives trading, margin is collateral required to support a position.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
Coupon
The interest payment specified in a bond’s terms.
Swap
A contract to exchange payment streams, such as fixed interest for floating interest.
How to read the notation
A slope or exposure · β
In a regression a coefficient describes how the fitted outcome changes with one unit of its input, holding the other included inputs fixed. A market beta of 1.2 gives a fitted change of 1.2 percentage points for a one-point change in market return.

All notation examples

First example

A bank earns 5% on €100m of loans and pays 2% on €80m of interest-bearing deposits. If loans are its only earning assets and other funding is costless, annual net interest income is €5m − €1.6m = €3.4m. The margin is 3.4%.

Two measures of rate exposure

Net interest income measures currency interest received on assets less paid on liabilities over an interval. Net interest margin divides it by average interest-earning assets. Economic value of equity instead asks how the present values of assets and liabilities change. Rising rates can improve short-term income while damaging the economic value of longer assets.

A fixed-rate loan pays its old coupon until maturity or reset. A floating-rate loan can reprice at a contractual fixing. Deposits may reprice by a fraction of the market move, called deposit beta in this context. Deposit beta is a funding pass-through parameter, distinct from equity-market beta.

Repricing gaps and deposit behaviour

A simple repricing gap subtracts rate-sensitive liabilities from rate-sensitive assets assigned to a time bucket. Multiplying the gap by the rate movement approximates income change if all bucket members reset promptly by the same amount. Partial pass-through, different reset dates, floors and caps break that shortcut.

A bank may gain initially by keeping deposit rates low, then lose customers to money-market funds and have to replace cheap balances with wholesale funding. Forecast volumes, competitive pricing and funding stability as well as the mechanical rate shock.

Check contracts and hedges

A borrower’s contractual margin can compensate credit and administrative costs. It is distinct from the market reference rate. When reference rates fall below a contractual floor, a floating loan may respond asymmetrically. Swaps can shift repricing exposure but bring collateral, basis and counterparty effects.

Measure over a clearly stated horizon. A six-month repricing lag alters interest for only part of the first year, even if it changes the full following year. Average earning assets in the denominator may themselves change if funding leaves or loans run off.

Method

Forecast a margin bridge

  1. Classify

    For each asset and funding group, record balance, initial rate, fixing interval, pass-through, floors and expected amount outstanding.

  2. Reprice

    Calculate additional cash interest only for the fraction of the evaluation period after each reset. Treat deposits and wholesale liabilities separately.

  3. Stress

    Change both rate pass-through and balances. Compare the resulting NII and margin with the separately modeled present-value effect.

Worked case

Deposits reprice sooner than loans

A bank has €80m fixed loans at 4% and €40m floating loans at 5%, funded by €100m deposits at 2% and €20m non-interest-bearing capital for this simplified case. All €120m of loans are earning assets throughout. A one-percentage-point rate rise occurs at the year start.

Working

  1. Initial annual interest income is €3.2m + €2m = €5.2m and expense €2m. Net interest income €3.2m gives margin 2.667%.
  2. The floating loans reset after six months by the full point, adding €40m × 0.01 × 0.5 = €0.20m in year one. Deposits immediately pass through 60% of the point, raising costs €0.60m.
  3. New first-year NII is €2.80m and margin 2.333%, a decline of about 0.333 percentage point. In a following full reset year, with unchanged balances and rates, NII becomes €3.0m and margin 2.5%.

Interpretation

The difference across years arises from timing. A separate duration analysis might also reveal a fall in the market value of fixed-rate assets even if their coupons are unchanged.

A common mistake

Subtracting 2% deposit cost from 4% loan yield despite unequal funding and asset balances.

How to correct it

Compute currency interest for each balance and period, then divide net interest income by the chosen average earning-asset base.

Quick check
Deposit rates rise to 3% and loan rates stay fixed. What annual margin follows with the same balances?

Interest expense becomes €2.4m. Net income from interest is €5m − €2.4m = €2.6m, or 2.6% of the €100m earning assets.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A bank has €50m loans repricing immediately by a full 2-point market rise and €90m deposits repricing immediately with 40% pass-through. Other balances and rates are fixed.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Compute the annualized change in NII under fixed balances.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Could deposit outflows reverse this result? Give a numerical threshold condition.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Loan interest rises €50m × 0.02 = €1m. Deposit cost rises €90m × 0.008 = €0.72m, leaving NII up €0.28m annually.

Part B 2 marks

Yes. Additional annual wholesale replacement costs above €0.28m erase the gain. For example, replacing €20m deposits with funding more than 1.4 percentage points dearer adds over €0.28m annually.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A bank carries €50m of immediately floating loans and €80m of administered-rate deposits. At what deposit pass-through would a rate rise cease to improve income?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would model each side’s repricing on actual dates and show the pass-through threshold. At 50% deposit beta the €50m of asset resets more than offset the deposit cost, giving €0.2m added annual income. At 62.5% the benefit disappears. I would test both customer retention and wholesale replacement costs, and compare earnings sensitivity with the separate effect on economic value of equity.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If the loans reprice only after six months but deposit rates respond immediately, what beta breaks even in the first year?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Only half of the annual €1m asset increase arrives in year one, giving €0.5m. The deposit change is €1.6m times beta, so first-year break-even beta falls to 0.5/1.6 = 31.25%. The full-run-rate threshold can remain 62.5% afterwards.
