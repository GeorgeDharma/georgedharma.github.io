The bank balance sheet

Loans and cash are assets. Deposits and other borrowing are liabilities. Equity absorbs losses before creditors under the simplified priority model.

The starting point

Start from the bank’s point of view. A loan to a customer is an asset because the bank expects to collect payments. A customer’s deposit is a liability because the bank owes that money. Cash, reserves and other investments belong on the asset side. Equity is the residual remaining for the bank’s owners.

The balance sheet obeys assets = liabilities + equity. A decline in an asset reduces equity if liabilities stay unchanged. Leverage means that a small percentage fall in asset value can represent a large percentage fall in the owners’ cushion. Cash and equity answer different questions about the bank.

Theory and method

Bank DuPont decomposition and leverage

Return on assets · Return on equity · Equity multiplier

A bank reports 8% return on equity while earning only 0.4% on assets. How can both be true, and why might this be fragile?

A bank owner supplies a small share of the money invested in loans and securities. Interest, fees, credit provisions and operating expenses apply across the whole portfolio, while residual earnings accrue to the smaller ownership base. A narrow change in loan losses can therefore move return on equity sharply. The basic DuPont identity separates profitability of assets from the leverage used to finance them. It gives a historical accounting bridge and requires further analysis before judging the quality of earnings.

How the result is built

ROE = ROA × A/E, where ROA = NI/A

NI is net accounting income in euros over the period, A average euro assets, E average euro equity measured on a consistent basis, ROA return on assets and ROE return on equity.

  1. Assume average assets €1,000m, equity €50m, net interest income €28m, fees €4m, costs €20m, provisions €8m and zero tax. Net income equals €4m.
  2. ROA is 4/1,000 = 0.4%. Equity multiplier is 1,000/50 = 20. Multiplying gives ROE 8%, matching 4/50 directly.
  3. Holding average balances fixed for the scenario, provisions of €14m reduce profit to −€2m. ROA becomes −0.2% and ROE becomes −4%.

When the reasoning holds

  • Use matching period and average balance definitions. Changing capital midyear distorts comparisons using ending equity alone.
  • Accounting provisions and market value of credit losses may differ. An apparent high ROE from delayed recognition or a small equity cushion needs a separate solvency review.
Terms in this lesson
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Liability
Something owed to another party, such as a loan or an unpaid supplier bill.
Expected credit loss
For a simple single-period example, default probability multiplied by exposure and the share lost if default occurs.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
First example

A bank has €20m of cash and €80m of loans, funded by €90m of deposits and €10m of equity. A €4m loan loss leaves €96m of assets and €6m of equity. The 4% asset loss consumes 40% of starting equity.

Follow a loss through the balance sheet

The balance sheet is a snapshot of what the bank controls and owes. Loans are assets because customers owe the bank. Deposits are liabilities because the bank promises funds to depositors. Equity is the residual claim left after liabilities. Expenses and credit losses generally reduce income and, other things equal, retained earnings and equity.

A deposit withdrawal reduces cash and a deposit liability together, with no mechanical equity loss if the bank can meet it at recorded value. A discounted forced sale used to fund the withdrawal can generate a separate loss. Post each transaction before combining their effects.

Leverage and asymmetric outcomes

Assets divided by equity is a simple leverage measure. If a bank has €20 of assets per €1 of equity, an unanticipated 1% asset loss consumes 20% of starting equity if liabilities are fixed. Ownership returns respond to the spread earned across a much larger asset base. Debtholders can still be exposed if losses exceed equity.

Book equity uses recognized accounting figures. Economic equity values assets and liabilities at an appropriate valuation convention and can diverge, especially when market yields or expected credit losses change. Label the basis before comparing cushions or calculating a threshold.

Risk weights and regulatory concepts

A raw equity-to-assets ratio gives equal weight to each asset euro. A simplified risk-weighted measure assigns different weights to specified categories and compares eligible capital with weighted assets. The eligible numerator can differ from ordinary book equity and regulations vary by jurisdiction. Use classroom weights only when explicitly supplied.

A bank can shrink a ratio’s denominator without adding much equity, for example by replacing a high-weight asset with a lower-weight one. This improves the quoted ratio under the assumed rules but may still leave interest-rate or liquidity exposure. Inspect both numerator and underlying economic risk.

Method

Run a balance-sheet bridge

  1. Start

    Record cash, other assets, deposit and funding liabilities, and residual equity. Verify the accounting identity.

  2. Post

    Distinguish a write-off, a repayment, a deposit transfer, an asset sale and a capital injection. State both sides of each entry.

  3. Measure

    Compute pre- and post-event equity, liquidity and any defined risk-weighted ratio separately. Explain the valuation basis and the binding constraint.

Worked case

A loss followed by a withdrawal

A bank begins with €20m cash, €100m of loans, €90m deposits, €20m wholesale debt and €10m equity. A €4m loan loss occurs. Then customers transfer €12m of deposits elsewhere, paid from cash. Ignore tax and further funding.

Working

  1. After the loss, loans fall to €96m and equity to €6m. The 3.33% loss on opening total assets has consumed 40% of opening equity.
  2. Withdrawal reduces cash to €8m and deposits to €78m. Closing assets are €104m, liabilities €98m and equity €6m. The withdrawal did not produce an additional €12m accounting loss.
  3. With classroom weights of zero for cash and 100% for loans, closing weighted assets are €96m. If eligible capital equals book equity here, the ratio is 6/96 = 6.25% compared with 10/100 = 10% initially.

Interpretation

A sale at a discount to fund another withdrawal would change equity further, unlike a withdrawal covered entirely by existing cash.

A common mistake

Calling customer deposits part of the bank’s equity because both finance the assets.

How to correct it

Deposits are repayment obligations. They enter liabilities. Equity is the residual buffer after all recorded liabilities and changes as asset values change.

Quick check
An additional €7m loan loss occurs with the same liabilities. What is equity on this simplified balance sheet?

Assets fall from €96m to €89m. With €90m of liabilities, residual equity is −€1m.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A bank has assets €150m and liabilities €138m. It sells a loan carried at €20m for €17m cash and then pays €10m of deposits from cash.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find closing assets, liabilities and equity.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Why does the sale generate a loss but the deposit withdrawal does not here?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

The sale reduces total assets by €3m and equity from €12m to €9m. Withdrawal reduces assets to €137m and liabilities to €128m. Equity remains €9m.

Part B 2 marks

The sale realizes less than the recorded claim. Paying the deposit extinguishes an equal liability while using cash at its recorded value, so there is no residual change from that payment itself.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A bank reports 8% return on equity while earning only 0.4% on assets. How can both be true, and why might this be fragile?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would reconcile net profit before praising the return. Four million euros on €1bn assets is only 0.4% ROA, but the bank has a 20-times equity multiplier, giving 8% ROE. A €6m rise in provisions reverses the whole picture to a 4% equity loss. I would ask whether the credit cost is through-cycle, how much capital absorbs unexpected losses, and whether the funding is stable.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If equity doubles to €100m with the same €1bn assets and €4m income, did the asset business deteriorate?

Compare with the follow-up answer

ROA stays 0.4% and the equity multiplier falls to ten, giving 4% ROE. The lower equity return here reflects more funding from owners and a larger loss buffer. To judge value creation I would also examine the cost and risk of that equity.
