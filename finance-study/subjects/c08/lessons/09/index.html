Supervision and safety nets

Deposit protection, lender-of-last-resort facilities and capital rules can stabilize funding while also changing incentives to take risks.

The starting point

A deposit guarantee promises eligible depositors defined protection under a particular scheme. A central bank may supply emergency liquidity against acceptable collateral on stated terms. Capital requirements place a loss-absorbing cushion behind bank assets. Each tool addresses a different part of a bank’s funding or loss problem.

Protection can also change incentives. Depositors who expect reimbursement may monitor the bank less closely, and owners can benefit from gains while their downside is limited. Supervision, risk-sensitive terms and loss-absorbing capital can address some of that moral hazard. Coverage limits and eligibility depend on the jurisdiction and the account.

Theory and method

Deposit insurance, limited liability and risk shifting

Moral hazard · Lender of last resort

Can a riskier asset portfolio with identical expected proceeds be more attractive to insured bank shareholders, and which safety-net tool addresses an immediate cash shortage?

Owners receive what remains after depositors are paid, with their payoff floored at zero by limited liability. A credible insurer covers an insured depositor’s shortfall if bank assets disappoint. Increasing dispersion around the same average asset payoff can increase both shareholder option value and the insurer’s expected payout. This creates a risk-taking incentive if insurance premiums and supervision ignore risk. Separately, a solvent bank lacking settlement cash may need eligible collateralized liquidity even when insured customers expect eventual reimbursement.

How the result is built

Equity payoff = max(A − D,0); insurance claim = max(D − A,0)

A is the bank’s euro asset payment at liquidation and D the insured euro deposit promise. The illustration ignores discounting, other claims and intervention costs. The two option-like payoffs allocate upside to owners and shortfall to the guarantor.

  1. With €90 deposits and safe assets paying €100 for sure, equity is €10 and insurer payout zero.
  2. An alternative pays €140 or €60 with equal probability, still €100 on average. Equity pays €50 or zero, averaging €25. Insurance pays zero or €30, averaging €15.
  3. With a free guarantee owners gain €15 of expected payoff while asset value is unchanged. A €15 fair expected-loss charge would offset this simple incentive under the stated risk-neutral example.

When the reasoning holds

  • Depositors are assumed fully covered and paid, with owners protected by limited liability. Actual eligibility, coverage limits, creditor hierarchy and recovery rules matter.
  • Expected-loss pricing in the classroom is not necessarily a market actuarially sufficient premium when losses are concentrated in recessions or create systemic costs.
Terms in this lesson
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Moral hazard
A contract can change behaviour after it is signed when the other party cannot fully observe or control those actions.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Settlement
The final transfer of cash or securities needed to complete a transaction.
First example

Consider a hypothetical bank with sound assets, €3m cash and €9m due today. A €6m loan secured on eligible assets covers today’s gap. It adds both €6m of cash and €6m of debt before the payment. It supplies liquidity without increasing equity at origination.

Match the instrument to the failure

Deposit protection changes the payoff to eligible depositors if a bank fails, potentially weakening the incentive to run. It need not make the bank’s assets whole. Emergency liquidity can deliver settlement cash against acceptable collateral. Capital requirements and new capital address capacity to absorb losses.

A resolution process deals with a failing institution, continuity of important functions and allocation of losses under applicable law. Classroom cases should specify which claims are protected and which can bear losses. Coverage limits, central-bank eligibility and lender terms are institutional facts, not universal constants.

Incentives created by a safety net

When depositors believe they will be repaid regardless of bank risk, they may demand less compensation or monitor management less. Shareholders with limited downside can then profit from risky lending at the expense of a guarantee fund if safeguards are weak. This is a moral-hazard channel.

Risk-sensitive premiums, credible supervision, limits on risk exposure, loss-absorbing equity and orderly resolution can address different dimensions of that incentive. An excessively costly or stigmatized facility might discourage a solvent institution from seeking liquidity when early use would help.

Asset haircuts and institutional feasibility

A lender providing emergency cash needs to verify eligible collateral, ownership, legal documentation, valuation and operational readiness. A 20% haircut on collateral worth €10m permits at most €8m borrowing on that asset under the stated simple rule. Amount and date of access matter as much as headline eligibility.

A guarantee can reduce a run without disbursing funds immediately if depositors trust its coverage and payout process. Delays may still matter to households with urgent expenses. A capital injection can increase both cash and equity if paid in cash, though who provides it and at what price affect ownership.

Method

Evaluate an intervention

  1. Diagnose

    Quantify immediate payment gap, hold-value equity and severe-loss scenario before assigning a treatment.

  2. Terms

    Check who qualifies, eligible collateral or claims, haircut, time to cash and who bears loss if performance falls short.

  3. Incentives

    State how the intervention affects creditors, owners and managers before the next crisis, and attach a suitable accountability mechanism.

Worked case

Haircuts and a residual capital hole

A bank has €3m free cash and €15m due today. Its eligible unpledged securities are worth €16m. An approved facility will lend against them with a 25% haircut. The same bank’s hold-value balance sheet has €102m assets and €100m liabilities.

Working

  1. The liquidity gap is €12m. The facility can lend €16m × 0.75 = €12m and fully bridges the immediate gap if documents and settlement are ready.
  2. Borrowing initially increases cash and liabilities equally, leaving hold-value equity €2m. A subsequent verified €5m deterioration in other assets then produces equity of −€3m.
  3. An equity contribution of €3m restores zero residual value in that deteriorated scenario. A positive buffer requires more. A new secured loan alone would leave the negative equity unchanged.

Interpretation

A deposit guarantee might prevent additional withdrawals, but its effects on confidence should not be entered as a fictitious asset that erases the €5m deterioration.

A common mistake

Inferring that protection of depositors guarantees the owners of a failing bank retain their equity.

How to correct it

Identify precisely whose claim is insured and where losses are allocated. A protected customer and an unprotected shareholder can have very different outcomes.

Quick check
A bank has a permanent €4m loss in its assets. What directly replenishes its equity in this example?

A capital contribution increases assets without creating an equal repayment obligation. A new loan increases cash and debt together.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A solvent bank owes €18m in immediate withdrawals and holds €4m cash. Eligible collateral worth €15m attracts a 20% haircut. A guarantee applies only to deposits within the case’s defined scope.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find the cash remaining to be sourced after using the facility.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Explain one confidence benefit and one incentive cost of deposit protection.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Available secured funding is €12m, giving €16m with cash. Against €18m due, €2m remains to be funded by another confirmed route or delayed under applicable terms.

Part B 2 marks

Credible payment protection weakens the incentive for covered depositors to queue for first payment. Reduced creditor discipline can encourage bank risk-taking if pricing, oversight and equity discipline do not offset it.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Can a riskier asset portfolio with identical expected proceeds be more attractive to insured bank shareholders, and which safety-net tool addresses an immediate cash shortage?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would show the payoff asymmetry first. Investors keep upside above the €90 deposit promise and lose no more once their equity is exhausted. More dispersion raises their mean payoff from €10 to €25 and transfers €15 of expected shortfall to the insurer in this example. Deposit protection can stabilize withdrawals. For an immediate cash shortage I would instead quantify timely collateralized funding and check underlying solvency and facility eligibility.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If a central bank lends €20 at par against adequate collateral immediately, does it remove the risky portfolio’s €30 low-state economic shortfall?

Compare with the follow-up answer

At origination the bank receives €20 cash and owes €20 more, leaving net assets unchanged before fees or valuation effects. The loan can settle a payment or avoid a forced sale. It does not by itself recapitalize a portfolio whose ultimate assets fall short of its obligations.
