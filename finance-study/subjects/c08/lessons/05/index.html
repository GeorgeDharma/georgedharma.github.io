Liquidity insurance

Depositors value the ability to withdraw early. A bank can offer this while holding longer-lived assets if withdrawals are sufficiently predictable.

The starting point

A depositor may need money unexpectedly, even when a factory loan will be repaid only next year. By pooling many depositors, a bank can hold some readily available funds for early withdrawals and invest the remainder in longer-lived assets. This service is called liquidity insurance.

Its usefulness depends on how withdrawals behave together. Some individual surprises can average out across a large pool. A simultaneous demand for cash is different. Selling a long-lived asset urgently may bring less than holding it until payment, so reserves, eligible borrowing and the terms offered to depositors matter.

Theory and method

Diamond–Dybvig liquidity insurance

Maturity transformation · Ex ante risk sharing

Why could a household rationally choose a bank deposit before knowing whether it will need its savings early, even if patient households earn less through the bank?

A household knows it will need food or medical spending but cannot predict the timing. Before its type is realized it may value a better early payout even if the patient-state payout falls. A bank can pool households whose liquidity timing is independent and allocate a limited amount to short storage while investing the remainder in a more productive long asset. The contract shifts resources toward urgent states, trading some productivity for insurance where marginal utility is higher.

How the result is built

λc₁ + (1 − λ)c₂/R = 1, and EU = λu(c₁) + (1 − λ)u(c₂)

Each household starts with one unit. λ is the fraction needing early consumption, c₁ its early consumption, c₂ late consumption per patient household, R the long asset’s gross late return, u utility of consumption, and EU expected utility before the household learns its type.

  1. Set λ = 0.25 and R = 1.5. Offering c₁ = 1.10 requires short resources of 0.275 per initial household. Investing the remaining 0.725 long produces 1.0875 late.
  2. Distribute 1.0875 to the 75% late households, giving c₂ = 1.45. Against a benchmark with own-investment outcomes 1 and 1.5, the contract shifts resources toward early need.
  3. With u(c) = −1/c, expected utility rises from −0.25/1 − 0.75/1.5 = −0.75 to −0.25/1.10 − 0.75/1.45 ≈ −0.7445. A less negative score is preferred.

When the reasoning holds

  • The aggregate early fraction is predictable in the first allocation. A common liquidity shock can force inefficient liquidation and alter the promised late payment.
  • This comparison specifies the available own-investment benchmark, early liquidation of one and utility. A welfare ranking requires preferences and feasible alternatives, beyond simply quoting a higher late return.

Terms in this lesson
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Principal
The amount borrowed or invested before interest is added.
How to read the notation
Subscripts · S₀ and S₁
A small index names the time or item. If S₀ = €40 and S₁ = €42, the price moves from €40 at time 0 to €42 at time 1. A subscript i can label a company instead of a date.

All notation examples

First example

A bank holds €25 of cash and assets with €75 of book value. It expects early withdrawals of €20, leaving a €5 cash cushion. If withdrawals instead reach €35, it needs €10 more cash. Selling assets at €0.80 per euro of book value requires €12.50 of assets.

A two-date economy with uncertain needs

Households may discover early that they need cash immediately, while others can wait and benefit from a productive long-term project. Each household initially faces the risk of becoming an early spender. Holding only liquid cash sacrifices some return. Investing everything long risks an expensive premature sale.

A bank can pool such liquidity risks. If the share of early customers is predictable in aggregate, it can hold liquid resources for their withdrawals and invest the rest long. Depositors value a redeemable claim partly because their spending date is uncertain before they invest.

Choose a feasible deposit promise

Suppose n customers each deposit one unit. If a fraction λ needs an early payment c₁, the bank requires nλc₁ liquid units at the early date. Long assets that survive pay R later and can fund c₂ for the remaining customers. Write both resource constraints before judging generosity.

The feasible contract may redistribute across early and late types. Preferences over consumption timing and risk determine whether an allocation is desirable. Liquidity insurance is about pooling timing shocks. Simply maximizing expected asset return can leave early customers unable to consume when needed.

When pooled demand becomes correlated

A common emergency, loss of confidence or the disappearance of an expected funding line can produce more early withdrawals than the designed buffer covers. Liquidating long assets at a discount reduces what remains for later customers. The early promise becomes fragile when others can also choose to withdraw.

A larger liquid reserve reduces forced sales but uses funds that could have produced a higher late return. Committed liquidity, deposit maturity and redemption rules also influence this trade-off. Test whether support remains available under the same common event that increases withdrawals.

Method

Check a deposit contract

  1. Forecast

    Estimate early demand, the contractual payout per early customer and the immediate resources reliably available. Show when assumptions are population averages.

  2. Allocate

    Invest the remainder long and compute the late payout achievable after the early claims. Compare the allocation with solitary saving if preferences are specified.

  3. Stress

    Increase early demand, apply realizable liquidation prices and calculate how much long principal must be sold and what remains per late claimant.

Worked case

A pooled contract and a surprise withdrawal

One hundred households deposit €1 each. A bank holds €30 liquid and invests €70 in a project returning €1.40 per retained initial euro at the late date. It promises €1 per early withdrawal. Early liquidation yields only €0.70 per euro of project principal.

Working

  1. With 30 early households, liquid money covers them exactly. The project pays €98 late, allowing the other 70 households €1.40 each.
  2. With 44 early households, the cash shortfall is €14. Liquidating €20 of project principal raises €14, leaving €50 invested. It produces €70 late for the 56 remaining households, or €1.25 each.
  3. If a credible bridge instead lent €14 repayable at €15 late, keeping the entire project yields €98 − €15 = €83 for later households, about €1.48 each under this changed participation scenario.

Interpretation

The bridge comparison is conditional on timely availability and an ability to repay it. It shows the economic cost of forced liquidation, not that a bank can always borrow in a crisis.

A common mistake

Using the long project’s maturity value as cash available at the early withdrawal date.

How to correct it

Apply the early liquidation value or a confirmed borrowing facility when testing the early constraint. Keep future output in the late-period calculation.

Quick check
If assets can only be sold for €0.50 per euro of book value, how many must be sold to cover the same €10 gap?

Divide the cash needed by the cash received per euro of assets. €10/0.50 = €20. Worse sale prices consume more future repayment capacity.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A bank holds €24 cash for 80 identical depositors and invests €56 long. Early claims pay €1. Twenty-eight customers withdraw. Each euro sold early produces €0.80. Each retained euro pays €1.30 later.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find project principal sold and the late payment per remaining customer.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Why might raising cash balances in advance reduce risk but lower late payouts in a normal year?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

The €4 cash gap needs €5 of principal sold. Remaining €51 pays €66.30 at maturity. Dividing by 52 remaining customers gives €1.275 each.

Part B 2 marks

More cash can serve unexpected withdrawals without forced-sale losses. Under normal lower demand it displaces long investment earning the higher late payoff, creating a resource trade-off.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why could a household rationally choose a bank deposit before knowing whether it will need its savings early, even if patient households earn less through the bank?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would compare the contract when households choose it, before their timing needs are known. This pool raises urgent consumption from 1 to 1.10 and reduces late consumption from 1.50 to 1.45. Under the given utility the ex ante household prefers the insurance. I would then test how reliably the bank can predict aggregate withdrawals and what happens if patient customers imitate early ones.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Suppose 35% genuinely need early payout and long assets can be liquidated early at one unit per invested unit. What late payout is feasible?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Paying 0.35 × 1.10 = 0.385 requires 0.110 beyond the 0.275 liquid reserve. Liquidation leaves 0.615 invested long, producing 0.9225. Dividing among the 65% remaining yields about 1.4192 per patient household, lower than the planned 1.45.
