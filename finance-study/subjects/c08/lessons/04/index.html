Delegated monitoring

Diversification can reduce the cost of delegating borrower oversight. Ask who monitors the intermediary and how the contract gives it incentives.

The starting point

Delegated monitoring asks why savers can rely on an intermediary to examine borrowers for them. Checking every invoice and enforcing every contract is costly. Giving one party the task avoids duplication, but that party must also have an incentive to perform it. Owners with capital at risk can suffer losses if oversight is poor.

In Diamond’s model, diversification across sufficiently independent borrowers helps support delegation. The whole loan portfolio can be more predictable than one loan, making the intermediary’s own promised repayments easier to support. A common industry shock weakens that benefit because many repayments may disappoint together.

Theory and method

Diamond’s delegated monitoring model

Delegation cost · Diversification and intermediary debt

If pooling loans leaves total expected collections unchanged, why can it still make a bank a more effective delegated monitor?

A specialist can inspect borrowers once on behalf of many savers, yet the savers must also trust that the specialist will remit collections. A bank promising fixed payments against a diversified pool can meet those obligations more reliably, reducing states in which creditors must verify failure or enforce costly claims. Diamond’s model connects this reduction in delegation costs with the economies from avoiding duplicate checks on individual borrowers. Concentration in common shocks can undermine both the funding contract and credible monitoring.

How the result is built

Expected creditor shortfall = Σpⱼ max(D − Xⱼ, 0)

D is the aggregate fixed euro claim promised to depositors, Xⱼ euro portfolio collections in state j, pⱼ the probability of that state, and Σ averages the positive contractual shortfall. The shortfall illustrates one consequence of unreliable intermediation, not the complete cost function in Diamond’s paper.

  1. Each of two loans pays €100 with probability 0.9 or zero. With independent outcomes, total collections are €200 with probability 0.81, €100 with probability 0.18, and zero with probability 0.01.
  2. For fixed creditor promise €150, expected shortfall is 0.81 × 0 + 0.18 × €50 + 0.01 × €150 = €10.50. Expected loan collections are €180.
  3. With perfectly common failure, collections are €200 with probability 0.9 or zero with probability 0.1. Their expected value stays €180, but the positive shortfall rises to €15.

When the reasoning holds

  • Independence in the first case concerns loan outcomes, not the bank’s monitoring technology. Shared geographic or customer risks can reintroduce common default.
  • The shortfall calculation uses one fixed claim. The actual delegated-monitor model also specifies incentives, the verification or punishment technology and costs of issuing alternative contracts.

Terms in this lesson
Diversification
Combining exposures that do not always move together to reduce dependence on a single outcome.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Expected credit loss
For a simple single-period example, default probability multiplied by exposure and the share lost if default occurs.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
First example

Two equal loans each have a 10% chance of default. If defaults are independent, the chance both default is 0.10 × 0.10 = 1%. If both depend entirely on one buyer failing with 10% probability, the chance both default is 10%. The individual default chances are unchanged.

Separate monitoring from the promise to savers

A borrower may know project cash flow or effort better than outside funders. If every small saver must verify what happened before being paid, monitoring is duplicated and some may free-ride on others. A bank can act on their behalf and issue savers a simpler claim on its collective assets.

Delegation introduces a second information problem. Savers cannot cheaply observe every monitoring decision the bank makes. The intermediary’s contract, own stake, reputation and institutional oversight help give it incentives. The theory asks whether savings from delegation exceed these extra costs.

Why diversification helps this mechanism

If many monitored borrowers have independent project outcomes, total collections can become more predictable relative to their average. A bank funded by debt may then meet relatively simple promises to savers across most outcomes even when individual borrower outcomes are difficult to verify.

Diversifying a portfolio does not reduce its arithmetic expected credit loss merely by itself. It can reduce the risk of many defaults at once and the expected agency cost associated with the intermediary failing to meet its own obligations. Common shocks slow or eliminate that pooling benefit.

Test whether monitoring is worth the effort

A monitor incurs a cost whether or not a problem is ultimately found. Its benefit can come from preventing misuse, improving recovery, renegotiating promptly or distinguishing a sound firm from a weak one. Some benefit accrues to creditors, some to shareholders and potentially some to new lenders or the borrower.

A bank chooses effort based on the share of improvement it captures, not necessarily the full social benefit. Selling most of a loan while keeping little loss exposure can reduce monitoring incentives unless contracts retain risk, pay for verifiable actions or impose consequences for poor servicing.

Method

Evaluate an intermediary’s incentives

  1. Problem

    Specify what the borrower privately knows or controls and what particular monitoring action would change a repayment outcome.

  2. Value

    Compare discounted expected collection improvements with monitoring and delegation costs. Distinguish the whole loan’s benefit from the monitor’s retained share.

  3. Pool

    Test sensitivity to correlated defaults, concentrated sectors and uncertain enforcement, then identify who checks the intermediary.

Worked case

A lender sells most of its exposure

Specialist monitoring costs €12,000 per loan. An otherwise comparable loan has a 12% default probability without monitoring and 6% with monitoring. Each default costs €300,000 in present-value terms. Other outcomes and costs are unchanged.

Working

  1. The whole loan’s expected loss falls by (0.12 − 0.06) × €300,000 = €18,000. Net economic gain from monitoring is €6,000 before other effects.
  2. If the bank sells 70% of losses and gains pro rata, it retains only 30% of the €18,000 benefit, or €5,400, while still bearing €12,000 effort cost. Its private incentive reverses.
  3. With no other rewards, break-even retained share is €12,000/€18,000 = two thirds. A servicing fee linked to verified work could alter that condition.

Interpretation

The case illustrates a delegated monitoring conflict, independent of whether depositors can withdraw on demand. Cash-flow oversight and the run mechanism answer distinct banking questions.

A common mistake

Claiming a bank with more borrowers must be a better monitor.

How to correct it

Separate staff effort and verification technology from diversification of outcome risk. More borrowers can reduce idiosyncratic concentration and still overwhelm monitoring capacity.

Quick check
Which change most directly addresses the shared-buyer problem in this example?

The source of the cash flows drives dependence. Changing the paperwork leaves exposure to the same buyer.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

Monitoring costs €8,000 and raises expected portfolio recovery on one credit by €20,000. A lender retains 25% of that exposure.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find whole-loan and private monitoring benefits net of cost.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    How much extra monitoring-linked payment is needed at minimum to remove this private loss?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Whole-loan net benefit is €12,000. The bank captures €5,000 and pays €8,000, for private net benefit −€3,000 under the stated allocation.

Part B 2 marks

At least €3,000 makes the bank indifferent at the stated numbers. A contract should specify verifiable activity or outcomes and consider whether it creates incentives to alter reports or take extra risk.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

If pooling loans leaves total expected collections unchanged, why can it still make a bank a more effective delegated monitor?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would separate the loan book’s average cash from the cost of financing an opaque monitor. With independent loans the expected €150-promise shortfall is €10.50 versus €15 under common default, although both books collect €180 on average. More predictable aggregate collections can make a simple fixed claim easier to support and reduce reliance on investors checking each borrower. The bank still needs incentives to monitor well.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If creditors were promised only €90, how does independence compare with common default?

Compare with the follow-up answer

With independent loans, even one successful €100 payment covers the full €90. Only the 1% joint default creates a €90 shortfall, averaging €0.90. Under common default the 10% failed state gives an expected €9 shortfall. Changing the funding promise changes the economic benefit of diversification.
