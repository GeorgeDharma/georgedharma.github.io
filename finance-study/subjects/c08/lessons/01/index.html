Banking and Financial Intermediation
Core finance / Lesson 1 of 13

Why intermediaries exist

A bank can reduce the cost of screening and monitoring many borrowers on behalf of many small savers.

The starting point

Imagine one hundred households each lending a little money to a furniture maker. Every household would like to know whether the firm can repay, yet verifying orders, accounts and inventory separately would repeat expensive work. An intermediary can do the screening once, pool funds and monitor the business on their behalf.

A bank can use specialist staff and information from repeated dealings to detect problems earlier. Intermediation also brings responsibilities and costs. Savers must trust the intermediary, and it needs enough capital, liquidity and sound incentives to keep its promises. An analyst compares these costs with the savings from doing the work collectively.

Theory and method

Akerlof’s adverse-selection mechanism and financial intermediation

Lemons problem · Screening

Why could a bank’s ability to verify individual loans improve financing even if public investors know the average repayment of the loans on offer?

A portfolio owner wants to sell two types of one-year loans. Buyers see an identical application file for each, but sellers know whether repayment is likely to be strong. A public bid based on the average may be unacceptable to holders of good loans. Their withdrawal changes the quality of whatever remains available. An intermediary can create value by obtaining credible borrower information and pricing types separately if the information is cheaper to produce than the market loss it prevents.

How the result is built

Ppool = θVG + (1 − θ)VB, with θ updated for sellers accepting the offer

Ppool is today’s pooled euro value at zero discount in this case, VG and VB are expected euro receipts from good and weak loans, and θ is the good-loan proportion conditional on accepting the sale.

  1. Take equal starting numbers of good loans worth €110 and weak loans worth €70. Initial pooled value is €90. A good owner requiring €100 refuses. Weak owners requiring €65 accept.
  2. After informed selection, accepted loans are weak and the competitive pooled value becomes €70. The initial €90 price cannot sustain the original mix.
  3. If a verifier identifies the type at €4 per accepted sale, a verified good loan can support a €106 net valuation, above the owner’s €100 threshold. The value created depends on what financing the verification enables.

When the reasoning holds

  • Sellers know more than buyers and have type-dependent participation. If all sellers are committed to sell regardless of price, this selection step weakens.
  • Verification must be credible to the eventual funder, with incentives to report bad findings. Cheap unverifiable opinions do not eliminate information asymmetry.

First example

Twenty savers could each pay €60 to review the same loan, a total of €1,200. A bank does one specialist review costing €350 and incurs €250 of additional administration. In this case shared review saves €600 before other costs.

Identify the friction a bank addresses

In a direct financing market, a borrower could issue a claim to savers. That works more readily when public information is reliable, the issue is large enough to justify disclosure, and investors can sell or diversify cheaply. Small opaque businesses may face high costs proving their prospects to many separate lenders.

A bank can screen applications before lending, write a contract that allocates risks, and follow up when outcomes disappoint. Screening concerns information before the contract. Monitoring concerns information and behaviour after it. These functions can be valuable even when the bank also supplies payments and deposits.

Economies of scale and relationship information

One institution can spread the fixed cost of an industry analyst or underwriting system across many loans. Repeated interaction may reveal seasonal customer payments or management quality unavailable in a public report. Relationship-specific information can help a viable borrower obtain credit during a temporary difficulty.

The same informational advantage can give a lender bargaining power if competitors cannot verify the borrower cheaply. An established relationship can also encourage delaying recognition of a bad loan. Ask whether the intermediary produces information, how it uses it and what incentives discipline the decision.

Compare technology, risk bearing and alternatives

Intermediation can also transform maturity, manage liquidity and diversify independent exposures. A transaction platform reducing search costs need not perform borrower monitoring, and a mutual fund holding public bonds need not promise demand deposits. Identify the precise contract behind an institution’s service.

Changing technology can lower direct distribution and screening costs, changing which route is efficient. A firm could move from bank funding toward public bonds as it grows. There is no fixed size threshold independent of information, scale and market access.

Method

Compare two financing arrangements

  1. Map

    List screening, documentation, monitoring, diversification and liquidity services needed for this borrower and these investors.

  2. Cost

    Calculate duplicated costs of direct contracts and the intermediary’s own operating, funding and oversight costs. Keep common costs on both sides.

  3. Incentives

    Specify who owns the residual loss, whether information can be verified, and how financing access changes if the intermediary fails.

Worked case

When shared monitoring becomes worthwhile

A borrower needs many equal-sized lenders. Direct contracting costs €90 per lender for repeated screening and monitoring. A bank incurs €2,400 fixed setup and oversight plus €18 per participating saver. Assume identical loan losses and funding in both routes and no other differences.

Working

  1. With n savers the direct cost is €90n. Bank cost is €2,400 + €18n. The bank is cheaper when €72n exceeds €2,400, or n exceeds 33⅓.
  2. For 50 savers costs are €4,500 directly and €3,300 through the bank, a €1,200 saving. For 20, direct costs €1,800 and the bank costs €2,760.
  3. If coordination among direct lenders cuts their cost to €55 each, the bank needs more than 2,400/(55 − 18) ≈ 64.86 savers to be cheaper. The relevant technology altered the threshold.

Interpretation

The result compares a specified set of services. A bank offering extra liquidity or imposing a costly collateral requirement could change the welfare comparison even at the same computed cost.

A common mistake

Comparing a bank’s interest rate with a bond coupon while excluding issuance, monitoring and liquidity costs.

How to correct it

Compare total financing terms and services for the same risk and horizon. Interest alone can reflect different promises or who bears information costs.

Quick check
If oversight of the bank itself adds €700 to that example, what happens to the cost advantage?

Direct review still costs €1,200. The intermediary route now costs €350 + €250 + €700 = €1,300. The comparison changes when delegation itself is costly.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

Direct oversight costs €42 per investor. An intermediary costs €900 fixed plus €12 per investor, while separate supervision of the intermediary costs €150.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find the smallest integer number of investors for which the intermediary is strictly cheaper.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Would a higher bank fee automatically show that intermediation destroys value?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Require 42n > 1,050 + 12n, giving n > 35. At 35 the costs tie. 36 is the smallest strictly cheaper group.

Part B 2 marks

No. A fee can pay for different monitoring, payment, maturity or liquidity services and transfer risks. Value requires comparing total costs and services on matched assumptions, including any agency costs.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why could a bank’s ability to verify individual loans improve financing even if public investors know the average repayment of the loans on offer?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would ask which owners will accept the calculated average. A €90 pooled bid drives out owners who require €100 for strong loans. The public buyer then faces weak loans worth €70. A bank able to verify type for €4 can support a good-loan valuation of €106. I would also check who verifies the bank and whether direct disclosure or contractual warranties can produce the same information more cheaply.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If verifying each good loan costs €15 and its owner still requires €100, can screening alone unlock the good market at these values?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The verified loan is worth €110 gross but only €95 net of the verification cost. It fails the €100 reservation. A cheaper signal, credible guarantee, lower reservation or additional financing benefit would be needed for this channel to support the sale.
