Deposit creation and settlement

Making a loan can create a matching deposit. Payment to another bank can create a need for reserves or wholesale funding.

The starting point

When a bank approves a loan and credits a customer’s account, it records two entries. The loan is an asset for the bank and the newly credited deposit is a liability. The account holder can spend the deposit. Loan approval still depends on creditworthiness, funding conditions, capital and other constraints.

If the customer pays someone at another bank, the deposit moves to the recipient’s bank. The banks must settle between themselves, commonly using central-bank reserves. The originating bank can therefore face a funding need after creating the deposit. Movements within one bank generally change which customer owns the deposit.

Theory and method

Double-entry money creation and consolidated settlement

Inside money · Central-bank reserves · Balance-sheet counterpart

A central bank purchases a €100 security. Does that always add €100 to the deposits held by the public?

A payment record has to identify the seller. If a commercial bank sells its own security to the central bank, it swaps securities for reserves on its asset side. If a household sells the security and is paid through its commercial bank, that bank receives reserves and credits the household’s deposit. Both operations create reserves under the assumed settlement, yet only the second directly adds a customer deposit. A private bank loan is a third, economically different balance-sheet expansion.

How the result is built

Assets = liabilities + equity, applied separately to the central bank, commercial bank and nonbank holder

Each transaction below changes euro asset, liability or equity balances for the named entity at settlement. A reserve is a commercial-bank asset at the central bank. A household deposit is a commercial-bank liability and the household’s asset.

  1. A bank originating a €100 loan credits a €100 customer deposit. Loan assets and deposit liabilities increase by €100 while system reserves initially stay unchanged.
  2. A central-bank purchase of a €100 security directly from the bank exchanges that asset for €100 reserves. Commercial-bank deposits and equity do not mechanically change.
  3. A purchase from a nonbank through its bank gives that bank €100 reserves and creates a €100 deposit for the nonbank seller. The central bank acquires the security and issues the reserve liability.

When the reasoning holds

  • Ignore subsequent spending, repayments, fees and other simultaneous operations. These follow-on transactions can alter observed aggregate balances.
  • The assumed nonbank seller holds a commercial-bank account and settlement involves reserves. Government or foreign accounts and alternative settlement arrangements require their own ledger.
Terms in this lesson
Settlement
The final transfer of cash or securities needed to complete a transaction.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Liability
Something owed to another party, such as a loan or an unpaid supplier bill.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
Principal
The amount borrowed or invested before interest is added.
First example

Bank A lends €100 and credits its customer €100. The customer sends €70 to a supplier at Bank B. Ignoring other payments, Bank A gives up €70 of reserves and retains €30 of the new deposit; Bank B gains €70 of reserves and owes its supplier €70.

Record loan origination at one institution

A bank accepting a borrower’s signed promise can credit a deposit to that borrower. The bank records a loan asset and an equal deposit liability. The deposit becomes spendable. The transaction expands the bank’s balance sheet, but creates no new equity and no new central-bank reserves on its own.

New bank deposits are a form of money for their holders. Central-bank reserves are an asset of eligible institutions used for settlement. A household’s deposit and a bank’s reserve balance are claims on different entities. Treating the two balances as interchangeable obscures who can pay whom.

Clear and settle a customer’s payment

When a payment goes to another bank, the receiving institution credits its customer and obtains settlement funds, subject to payment-system arrangements. Netting can reduce gross reserve transfers if opposing customer payments occur. A transfer between customers of one bank generally changes the names on its deposit accounts only.

The originating bank may meet outflows using reserves, receiving incoming payments, selling assets or borrowing against collateral. Constraints on funding, profitable lending opportunities, credit risk, capital and regulation affect its ability and willingness to expand. A fixed reserve multiplier misses these decisions.

Reverse the entries at principal repayment

A borrower paying principal from a deposit at the lending bank reduces both the bank’s loan asset and its deposit liability. If the borrower pays from another bank, settlement moves reserves as well. Principal repayment can reduce system-wide deposit money under these simplified assumptions. Interest income and government or central-bank transactions need separate entries.

A default write-off instead reduces the bank’s loan and equity or previously recognized allowances. A deposit created long ago may now be held by a completely different person at another bank. Losing the loan does not automatically reverse that person’s deposit.

Method

Trace a payment chain

  1. Separate

    Create columns for borrower, each commercial bank and deposit holder. Use a separate reserves line for interbank settlement.

  2. Net

    Post loan creation, then the customer transfer, then repayment or default as separate events. Match reserve losses and gains across the banking system.

  3. Reconcile

    Check deposits, loans and total reserves system-wide against the events, and distinguish funding needs at one bank from money creation in aggregate.

Worked case

Two banks exchange payments

Bank A grants €300 to a borrower and credits its deposit. The borrower pays €240 to a supplier at B. Independently a customer of B transfers €70 to another customer of A. Assume same-day net settlement, ample initial balances and no central-bank action.

Working

  1. Origination raises A’s loans and deposits by €300. The two payments imply a net reserve transfer from A to B of €240 − €70 = €170.
  2. Relative to the starting system, A’s customer deposits rise €300 − €240 + €70 = €130 and B’s deposits rise €240 − €70 = €170. The combined new deposits equal €300. Aggregate reserves have simply moved.
  3. If the borrower later receives €60 in its A deposit and uses €60 there to repay principal, that repayment entry removes €60 of deposits and loans. The separate receipt should be recorded according to where it came from.

Interpretation

A bank’s outflow and the system’s unchanged total reserves can coexist. A needs funding locally even when the two-bank aggregate remains balanced.

A common mistake

Treating a default write-off as if it confiscated the supplier’s unrelated deposit.

How to correct it

Record the loan loss or allowance at the lender. Follow the deposit to its current holder and bank. Only specific further transactions affect that balance.

Quick check
What would a €70 transfer to another customer of Bank A do instead, ignoring other flows?

The payment stays on Bank A’s books. Aggregate deposits there and the loan principal are unchanged by this transfer.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

Bank X lends €500 and the borrower pays all of it to Bank Y. Y’s customers send €120 to X customers the same day. There is no other activity.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Compute net reserve transfer and system deposit change.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Would a loan funded and spent entirely between two customers of X necessarily cause the same reserve outflow?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

X sends net €380 reserves to Y. Deposits across both banks rise €500 from loan origination, comprising a €120 net increase at X and €380 at Y under the stated transactions.

Part B 2 marks

No. X would debit one customer and credit another internally. Without external payments the transfer itself needs no interbank reserve movement, though X still has a larger loan and deposit base.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A central bank purchases a €100 security. Does that always add €100 to the deposits held by the public?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would first identify the seller and settlement account. Buying from the commercial bank replaces one bank asset with another and can leave customer deposits unchanged. Buying from a nonbank typically credits its deposit and the receiving bank’s reserves together. In contrast, a commercial loan creates a deposit without creating reserves. I would separately trace any later transfer that redistributes reserves among banks.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The nonbank seller immediately uses the newly credited €100 to repay a €100 loan at the same commercial bank. What remains from these two steps?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The repayment extinguishes the new customer deposit and €100 of loan assets. The commercial bank ends with €100 more reserves and €100 fewer loans relative to the start, with no net deposit change. The central bank still holds its acquired security.
