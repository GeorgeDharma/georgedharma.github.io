Cross-border banking

Branches, subsidiaries, currency mismatches and uneven access to group funding change where a shock is absorbed. Trace the legal entity that owes the cash.

The starting point

A banking group may operate through branches belonging to one legal entity or through separately incorporated subsidiaries. Track which entity owns each asset, which one owes each payment and what currency the contract requires. A consolidated total can conceal a shortage in one location or currency.

A group cannot automatically move funds wherever they are needed. Available collateral, funding markets, contractual commitments and local requirements affect transfers. A currency hedge may reduce a valuation exposure while still leaving a requirement to deliver the right currency on a particular day.

Theory and method

Covered interest parity and synthetic foreign-currency funding

Cross-currency basis · Currency funding ladder

A euro-funded subsidiary can borrow euros at 2% or dollars directly at 5%. How would it establish whether swapping euro funding into dollars is cheaper?

A dollar borrower can obtain dollars either by borrowing them directly or by borrowing euros, selling those euros for dollars now and locking in the future dollar cost of buying back the euros owed. Compare the two paths for the same amount, dates and credit terms. Deviations can reflect currency basis, balance-sheet scarcity, collateral or the borrower’s own credit access. Even a favorable synthetic quote requires operational ability to deliver currency in the relevant legal entity.

How the result is built

USD synthetic growth = (F/S)(1 + r€); FCIP = S(1 + r$)/(1 + r€)

S is spot US dollars per euro, F the contracted one-year forward dollars per euro, r€ the annual simple euro borrowing rate, r$ the comparable dollar rate, and FCIP the covered interest parity forward under matched idealized terms. Dollar growth measures repayment per dollar obtained today.

  1. To raise US$100m at S = 1.20, borrow €83.333m and sell euros spot. At 2% the euro amount due in a year is €85m.
  2. If the forward dealer requires US$1.25 per euro, lock a purchase of €85m for US$106.25m. The synthetic dollar rate is 6.25%, versus 5% direct borrowing.
  3. At parity F would be 1.20 × 1.05/1.02 ≈ 1.235294. Using that forward to buy €85m costs US$105m, exactly matching direct repayment.

When the reasoning holds

  • Rates and quotes must be executable for the same legal borrower, collateral, maturity and size. Comparing a parent’s subsidized euro rate with a subsidiary’s unsecured dollars can conflate distinct risks.
  • Forward performance, settlement limits and collateral calls affect funding. A locked terminal exchange rate does not supply cash on an earlier margin date.
Terms in this lesson
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
Swap
A contract to exchange payment streams, such as fixed interest for floating interest.
Counterparty
The other party to a financial contract or trade.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
First example

A subsidiary must pay US$12m tomorrow. It has US$5m of cash and a confirmed US$4m facility usable today. Its parent holds €10m but has made no enforceable or immediately executable dollar transfer. Under this stress assumption the subsidiary still needs US$3m.

Locate the relevant legal balance sheet

A foreign branch is part of its parent bank’s legal entity. A subsidiary is a separately incorporated institution with its own assets, liabilities and usually its own local obligations. Both can appear in a consolidated report, but the legal identity of the payer affects collateral access, claim priority and the feasibility of intragroup transfers.

A parent guarantee is a specific contingent contract. Common branding or common ownership alone does not establish one. Even when a parent promises support, verify trigger, currency, enforceability and the time at which cash can reach the subsidiary.

A group aggregate can hide currency risk

A subsidiary lending long in local currency and funding short in dollars can be exposed both to exchange-rate losses and to dollar refinancing. A currency swap may reduce valuation sensitivity. Its rollover, counterparty exposure and collateral calls can still generate dollar funding needs at inconvenient dates.

Net open currency position is different from same-day currency liquidity. Dollar assets due next year can match a dollar liability’s amount while providing nothing for a dollar payment due today. Construct separate cash ladders by currency and legal entity.

Examine cross-border transmission

A group treasury under stress might call intercompany balances or refuse to roll a line, transmitting stress to a locally sound business. Conversely, local restrictions can prevent a healthy subsidiary’s cash from supporting the parent. Operating profits consolidated into group reports can be trapped at a particular entity.

Home and host authorities may have different priorities and laws. Use specified transfer assumptions in exercises. Distinguish facts about binding restrictions from simulated stress measures or unconfirmed management intentions before judging the ability to meet foreign obligations.

Method

Follow the payment across borders

  1. Map

    Identify entity, currency, exact due date and legal claim. Mark any confirmation or guarantee and the account from which payment would actually be made.

  2. Translate

    Revalue assets and liabilities consistently, then build the settlement ladder in the payment currency. Apply limits on conversion, transfers and usable collateral.

  3. Stress

    Consider parent line withdrawal, a currency depreciation, later receipts and margin calls together. Avoid offsetting inaccessible group cash.

Worked case

A subsidiary’s open and timed dollar positions

A subsidiary owes US$25m tomorrow and US$5m in three months. It holds US$10m cash, a US$6m receivable collectible tomorrow and a US$16m loan payable in three months. A confirmed parent transfer is capped at US$4m tomorrow. Other group dollars cannot move on time.

Working

  1. Before intragroup financing, total dollar assets are US$32m against US$30m liabilities, a net positive US$2m on the stated nominal values. That net position does not settle tomorrow’s claims.
  2. Available tomorrow is US$10m + US$6m + US$4m = US$20m. The immediate shortfall is US$5m. Selling US$6.25m face of the three-month loan at 80% of face would fill it, with a US$1.25m economic loss relative to face.
  3. If the US$6m receivable is delayed, immediate shortfall becomes US$11m. Its eventual collection may leave final solvency adequate even while the nearer funding problem worsens.

Interpretation

A larger pool of dollars at the parent helps only if it can be transferred in time under the relevant legal and operational requirements. Record the transfer’s repayment terms when assessing the later bucket.

A common mistake

Netting a US$10m maturity at a subsidiary tomorrow against US$10m available next quarter at a different group company.

How to correct it

Match legal owner, currency and date. Then document a timely transfer or a genuinely available bridge before treating the second balance as usable.

Quick check
What new evidence would directly close this modeled dollar gap?

The payment requires dollars at the subsidiary tomorrow. Funding amount, currency, entity and timing must all line up.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A subsidiary owes €12m tomorrow. It has €4m free cash, receivables due in a week worth €6m, and a confirmed immediately drawable €5m parent line. Receivables can be sold today for 90% of face.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find the immediate gap and minimum face value it must sell if no other funding is available.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    How would a same-day suspension of parental transfers change the feasible outcome?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Cash and line supply €9m, leaving €3m. Sale of €3m/0.90 ≈ €3.333m face raises the balance, realizing about €0.333m against face.

Part B 2 marks

Only €4m cash plus at most €5.4m from selling all receivables is available, totaling €9.4m. A €2.6m shortfall remains despite positive group-level resources elsewhere.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A euro-funded subsidiary can borrow euros at 2% or dollars directly at 5%. How would it establish whether swapping euro funding into dollars is cheaper?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would write down which currency is bought at each date. The subsidiary receives US$100m now and owes €85m later. At US$1.25 per euro that costs US$106.25m, so borrowing dollars directly at 5% saves US$1.25m before other costs. I would verify comparable seniority, counterparty and collateral, settlement date, and whether the subsidiary actually has access to both funding channels.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If spot remains 1.20 but the executable forward falls to US$1.22 per euro, what happens?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The euro debt still matures at €85m. Purchasing it costs US$103.70m under the new forward, equivalent to 3.70% dollar funding. That is US$1.30m less than the direct loan under identical assumptions. Forward margin requirements and entity transfer limits still require inspection.
