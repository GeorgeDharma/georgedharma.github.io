Runs and coordination

If customers expect others to withdraw, early withdrawal can become individually attractive. Model an otherwise viable bank facing forced sales.

The starting point

Even a depositor who can wait may withdraw today if many others are expected to take their money first. The reason is strategic. Early withdrawals can force a bank to sell long-term assets cheaply, leaving fewer resources for people who wait. In the Diamond–Dybvig setting this feedback can produce a run.

Distinguish a coordination problem from bad underlying loans. A bank whose assets pay enough at maturity can still fail to meet a large immediate demand. Credible protection or funding can ease the incentive to rush, although confidence in that support and the underlying asset values still matter.

Theory and method

Diamond–Dybvig bank-run equilibrium

Strategic complementarity · Multiple equilibria

Suppose depositors genuinely prefer waiting when others wait. How large a belief in other withdrawals could nevertheless make running rational?

A patient customer can still join a queue if she expects enough other customers to force an early sale of the bank’s productive assets. Her personal preference for late consumption has not changed. What changes is the payoff to waiting when everyone else demands immediate cash. A simple two-person coordination game makes the threshold explicit. When both waiting and running can be best responses to the same action by others, beliefs can select different outcomes for the same underlying bank.

How the result is built

Wait value = 1.30(1 − q) + 0.60q; withdraw value = 1.00(1 − q) + 0.80q

q is the believed probability that the other depositor withdraws. All four listed values are the first depositor’s payoff units in a deliberately simplified game with a random queue when both withdraw.

  1. If the other waits, waiting pays 1.30 rather than 1.00 for withdrawing. If the other withdraws, joining the queue pays expected 0.80 rather than 0.60 for waiting.
  2. Expected wait value is 1.30 − 0.70q. Expected withdrawal value is 1.00 − 0.20q. Set them equal to find the belief threshold q = 0.60.
  3. Above 60% believed run probability the patient depositor prefers to withdraw. Both all-wait and all-withdraw can be mutual best responses at the ends of the belief range.

When the reasoning holds

  • The payoff matrix embodies first-come service and liquidation losses. A different suspension, protection or liquidity arrangement can change the game and equilibrium count.
  • Treat the listed payoffs as comparable expected-utility units or assume risk-neutral evaluation of these amounts. Actual depositors face timing needs and heterogeneous information.

Terms in this lesson
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
How to read the notation
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.

All notation examples

First example

A bank has €20 cash and a project worth €90 if held to maturity. It owes €100 of withdrawals in total. Selling the whole project early raises only €60. Holding can produce €110, whereas an immediate liquidation produces €80. Belief in a rush changes the cost of waiting.

Withdrawal decisions can reinforce each other

Consider a bank that promises early payment to customers on demand and holds long assets that pay well if allowed to mature. A patient saver may value a late claim more than an early one when few others withdraw. The same saver may rationally prefer to move early when a large queue would exhaust liquid resources.

This is strategic complementarity. One customer’s reason to withdraw strengthens when others do. A run can therefore reflect a coordination failure even without newly discovered bad loans. There can also be a run driven by genuine insolvency. The observable queue alone does not distinguish these mechanisms.

Why first-come service matters

If a bank pays early claimants at par until funds run out, position in the queue matters. Selling long assets for less than their maturity value shrinks the resources left for those who wait. An expectation of widespread withdrawals can become self-fulfilling under an otherwise viable contract.

The result depends on rules. Credible deposit protection, payment suspension provisions or reliable collateralized liquidity can change incentives and timing. Their costs and scope matter, including possible hardship for households with genuine early needs. A classroom model can specify them explicitly without assuming identical rules everywhere.

Distinguish fire sales from fundamental losses

Compare going-concern asset value with total obligations, then compare immediate cash and forced-sale value with runnable claims. A bank can pass the first and fail the second. If underlying asset payments themselves are also too small, removing the queue cannot restore long-run solvency.

Depositors and potential emergency lenders often lack reliable information about which situation applies. A fall in collateral market prices can simultaneously reduce emergency funding and affect confidence. Test both a liquidity-only and an underlying-credit-loss scenario.

Method

Diagnose a possible run

  1. Value

    Calculate what a patient holder can expect when normal withdrawals are funded and long assets mature. Identify whose demand for early cash is genuine.

  2. Queue

    Model larger withdrawals, the cash available before liquidations, the sale haircut and the payoff to arriving late.

  3. Intervention

    Specify which promise or facility changes the withdrawal incentive. Check collateral, timing and the solvency basis before assigning an effect.

Worked case

When waiting pays until others run

A bank has 100 deposits of €1, €20 cash and long investments costing €80 that pay €104 at maturity. Immediate sale of all investments brings only €64. Twenty customers genuinely need early cash. Deposits are served in order while available. Ignore all other claims.

Working

  1. Under normal withdrawals the €20 cash suffices. The 80 remaining households share €104 late, or €1.30 each. A patient person would rather wait for €1.30 than take €1 now under this outcome.
  2. If everyone requests €1 immediately, total immediate resources are €84. The queue cannot pay all 100 at par. A patient person believing others will run has a reason to seek an early place.
  3. A binding commitment to provide enough temporary cash against sound collateral could prevent forced sale if credible and repayable. Conversely a revised maturity project value of just €65 leaves total going-concern resources €85, below deposits.

Interpretation

Both scenarios involve fear and possible withdrawals, but only the second alternative contains a fundamental economic shortfall even with patient funding.

A common mistake

Diagnosing every rapid deposit outflow as proof that the loan book is worthless.

How to correct it

Estimate hold and liquidation values separately, verify asset quality, and model the rules governing early versus late claims.

Quick check
Which fact most clearly identifies a coordination problem in this case?

Early action by many depositors changes what remaining depositors can receive, even though the original cash flows could cover everyone over time.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A bank owes €200 of deposits, holds €40 cash and has assets paying €180 late or €130 if sold immediately. Thirty customers each need €1 early.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Compare resources if normal withdrawals occur and if all accounts demand par at once.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    If going-concern asset proceeds fall from €180 to €145, classify the new weakness.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Normal withdrawals leave €10 cash plus €180 late, covering the €170 remaining claims. A full immediate liquidation yields only €170 total for €200 claims, a €30 gap.

Part B 2 marks

Total resources if held are €40 + €145 = €185, short of €200 by €15. Liquidity pressure still exists, but confidence alone cannot erase this underlying loss.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Suppose depositors genuinely prefer waiting when others wait. How large a belief in other withdrawals could nevertheless make running rational?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would calculate the individual incentive in both joint states. If others wait, I prefer 1.30 later. If they run, I prefer 0.80 in the queue to 0.60 as the remaining claimant. In this simplified game a belief above 60% that the other person withdraws makes running rational. That mechanism can coexist with fundamental concerns, so I would separately estimate whether assets cover claims if held.

Interviewer’s follow-up

A credible liquidity service raises a waiting depositor’s payoff against another’s run to 0.90, keeping all other listed payoffs unchanged. Does a run remain a best response?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Waiting now pays 1.30 against waiting and 0.90 against withdrawal, exceeding the respective withdrawal payoffs of 1.00 and 0.80. Waiting is strictly preferred against either action in this modified game. The credibility and timely availability of the service are what change the result.
