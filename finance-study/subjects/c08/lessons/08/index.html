Capital versus liquidity

Insolvency concerns the value of assets relative to liabilities. Illiquidity concerns the cash available to meet obligations when due.

The starting point

A bank is solvent on a chosen valuation basis if its assets cover its liabilities. It is liquid for a particular payment date if it can meet the cash falling due. Assets that are valuable in total may be tied up for years. A lender can also have cash today even though its assets are worth too little overall.

Liquidity analysis therefore examines cash, reliable funding and sales proceeds available by the deadline. Solvency analysis examines the value and loss-bearing capacity of the whole balance sheet. Forced sales can connect the two problems by turning a short-term funding shortage into a permanent loss.

Theory and method

Basel liquidity coverage ratio and capital adequacy

LCR · High-quality liquid assets · CET1 capital ratio

A bank has ample marketable assets and enough forecast receipts to meet outflows. Why could mechanically netting all receipts still overstate its regulatory liquidity measure?

A bank may count on loans paying on time while deposits and funding run off during stress. The Basel liquidity coverage framework normally limits how much expected inflow offsets thirty-day stressed outflow, requiring some buffer of eligible high-quality liquid assets. Capital adequacy asks a different question about losses and weighted exposures. A high liquidity ratio can coexist with a thin capital cushion, and encumbered assets or unreliable inflows can make a casual ratio calculation far too optimistic.

How the result is built

LCR = H/[O − min(I,0.75O)]; CET1 ratio = K/W

H is eligible unencumbered high-quality liquid assets after relevant adjustments, O modeled thirty-day gross stressed outflows, I eligible gross inflows, K regulatory common equity tier one capital, and W risk-weighted assets. Monetary inputs share a currency and ratios are dimensionless.

  1. With H €120m, O €200m and I €180m, the general 75% inflow cap allows €150m to offset outflows, giving net outflow €50m. LCR is 240%.
  2. Using all €180m would instead show 600%, greatly understating resources that must be held independently of expected inflows. Apply the jurisdiction’s actual eligibility rules and relevant exceptions.
  3. If capital K is €24m and W €300m, CET1 is 8%. A €6m loss with unchanged W reduces it to 6% even if the liquidity inputs did not immediately change.

When the reasoning holds

  • The exercise uses the usual general inflow limit with no applicable exemption and treats reported H, I and O as already classified under the selected rules. Actual treatment depends on regime and exposure.
  • A thirty-day ratio may conceal a same-day cash mismatch. Verify that HQLA are operationally available, unencumbered and in a currency usable when obligations fall due.

Terms in this lesson
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
Liability
Something owed to another party, such as a loan or an unpaid supplier bill.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
First example

A bank has €4m cash and loans worth €106m if held, against €100m of liabilities. It is solvent on that valuation by €10m. With €12m falling due today and no immediate funding or sale possible, it has an €8m liquidity gap.

Three questions on one balance sheet

Solvency asks whether asset value covers liabilities under a stated valuation basis. Liquidity asks whether obligations can be settled when due. Regulatory capital compares eligible loss-absorbing resources with a specified measure of exposures. A bank can pass one or two tests and fail another.

Cash is an asset, while equity is a residual source of finance. Selling an asset at its carrying value converts one asset into another, usually improving immediately accessible cash without increasing equity. Selling below carrying value also realizes a loss and can damage capital.

Build a dated liquidity ladder

List opening unencumbered cash, reliable inflows, unavoidable outflows and available actions by time bucket. Exclude collateral already pledged unless genuinely reusable. An asset’s gross market value overstates cash that can be borrowed against it if a haircut applies. The same asset cannot be simultaneously sold and pledged.

A liquidity ratio gives a useful summary only for the time period, outflow assumptions and eligible resources in its definition. A large obligation due in one hour can bind before next-day receipts arrive. Stress both volume and timing under a common scenario.

Fire sales can transmit illiquidity into insolvency

If a bank needs cash G and assets sell for a fraction h of carrying value, it must sell G/h of book assets. Realized loss is G/h − G. Compare that loss with actual equity capacity. An initially solvent bank can lose its capital by meeting a large sudden withdrawal through deep discounts.

Collateralized borrowing can avoid immediate sale losses but creates a new liability and a claim on pledged assets. It can buy time for solvent cash flows to mature. If underlying asset losses remain large, borrowing alone cannot create the equity needed to absorb them.

Method

Run a liquidity and capital stress

  1. Classify

    Mark settlement due dates, unencumbered cash, confirmed lines and asset values under hold, pledge and sale scenarios.

  2. Fund

    Choose one permissible action per asset, apply haircuts and calculate cash by deadline. Post liabilities from borrowing and the loss from any sale.

  3. Test

    Recompute equity and any defined capital measure after the payment. Separate a cash gap from a permanent asset-value shortfall.

Worked case

A sale clears payments but uses capital

A bank has €8m cash and €92m loans against €94m liabilities and €6m equity. It owes €20m of those liabilities today. It can sell loans immediately at 75% of carrying amount. No other authorized funding is available in the first scenario.

Working

  1. The cash shortfall is €12m. Selling €16m book loans supplies €12m cash and realizes a €4m loss. After settlement, loans are €76m, cash zero, liabilities €74m and equity €2m.
  2. If the sale price were 60% of book, €20m loans must be sold, realizing €8m loss and leaving equity −€2m. Payment can clear at the cost of insolvency on the stated valuation basis.
  3. If a €12m collateralized borrowing were available without initial valuation loss, holding the loans and borrowing could fill the immediate gap without reducing the original €6m equity at origination. The new liability still has to be repaid.

Interpretation

A claim that the bank is simply liquid or illiquid is incomplete without explaining which funding arrangements, sale prices and deadlines were used.

A common mistake

Using a pledged asset’s gross carrying value as available cash while also counting the collateralized facility that already uses it.

How to correct it

Mark encumbered assets, apply contractual haircuts and keep sale and borrowing alternatives mutually consistent.

Quick check
Suppose its loans are then revalued at €90m and other amounts stay the same. What is its new equity value?

Total assets now equal €4m + €90m = €94m, below €100m of liabilities by €6m. The €8m payment gap answers a separate timing question.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A bank with €5m equity needs €9m additional cash today. Assets can be sold at 80% of book. Separately consider a secured loan for the entire gap at par.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find book assets sold, realized loss and equity after sale.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Compare immediate equity and future obligations under secured borrowing.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

€9m/0.80 = €11.25m book assets sold. Loss is €2.25m. Equity falls to €2.75m before other income or expense.

Part B 2 marks

At origination €9m cash and €9m debt are added, so equity remains €5m before charges or revaluation. The bank must satisfy collateral terms and repay principal and interest later.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A bank has ample marketable assets and enough forecast receipts to meet outflows. Why could mechanically netting all receipts still overstate its regulatory liquidity measure?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would first check eligibility, timing and the applicable inflow cap. In this example only €150m of €180m anticipated cash may offset €200m of modeled outflows, so €120m HQLA gives 240% LCR. I would examine intraday and currency-specific gaps separately. Then I would assess loss-bearing capital against weighted exposures. A bank with adequate liquidity can still face losses larger than its capital.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if €40m of those liquid assets is pledged and unavailable, and eligible inflows fall to €100m?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Available HQLA become €80m and recognized inflows are €100m, now below the €150m cap. Net outflows are €100m, so LCR falls to 80% under the specified general treatment. The bank should evaluate usable collateral and actual daily settlement needs separately.
