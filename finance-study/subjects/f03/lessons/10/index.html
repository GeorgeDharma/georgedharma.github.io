Replication and arbitrage

If two portfolios promise identical payments in every state, compare their prices. The argument requires that the replicating trades and financing are actually available.

The starting point

Imagine two tickets that pay exactly the same amount on the same date in every possible outcome. If one ticket costs less, someone able to buy the cheap one and sell the expensive one can lock in the difference.

Replication means creating one claim’s payments with other instruments. The law of one price links the costs of identical attainable payments. For an arbitrage argument, check every state, all cash flows and dates, trading costs, and whether both sides of the trade can actually be executed.

Theory and method

Arrow–Debreu state prices

State-contingent claims · Law of one price

How can a trader price a state-specific claim without guessing the real-world probability of that state?

Suppose tomorrow has only two possible outcomes. A claim that pays one euro in exactly one outcome is a basic building block. Its cost today is called a state price. If two traded assets have sufficiently different payouts, their market prices can reveal the cost of each building block. Add the required blocks to value a new payoff. State prices incorporate time and risk compensation. They need not equal the frequencies an analyst expects in the real world.

How the result is built

P = qH × XH + qL × XL.

P is today’s euro price, qH and qL the euro prices today of €1 paid only in the high or low state, and XH and XL the claim’s euro payouts in those states.

  1. A safe asset costs €0.95 and pays €1 either way, so qH + qL = 0.95. A risky asset costs €1.15 and pays €2 high or €0.50 low.
  2. Substitute qL = 0.95 − qH into 2qH + 0.5qL = 1.15. This gives 1.5qH = 0.675, hence qH = 0.45 and qL = 0.50.
  3. A claim paying €30 high and €10 low costs 0.45 × 30 + 0.50 × 10 = €18.50. Reversing those payoffs would cost €19.50.

When the reasoning holds

  • The two traded payoff patterns are linearly independent and can be bought or sold as required. Otherwise they may not identify both state prices or enforce the result.
  • All cash is due at the same time and the assets span the relevant outcomes. Additional default states or restrictions can invalidate a two-state calculation.
Terms in this lesson
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Replicating portfolio
A combination of traded claims that delivers the same future cash flows as a contract in every state covered by the model.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
First example

A claim costed at €98 and a portfolio costing €96 each deliver €100 for certain in one year. With no fees and unrestricted short selling, sell the claim for €98 and buy the portfolio for €96. Keep €2 today. Their future cash flows cancel.

Match state by state

Two claims with the same average payoff can have different values because their payments occur in different states of the world. A claim paying when resources are scarce can be especially valuable. In a replication argument, require identical payments in every relevant state at the same dates, after costs and contract conditions.

A replicating portfolio combines traded instruments to deliver the same state-by-state payoffs as a target claim. If the portfolio and claim can both be bought or sold on the stated terms, persistent price differences allow a trade producing a certain gain. The law of one price compares payment patterns, not average returns or labels.

Solve for the holdings

With two possible future states, two traded assets with sufficiently different payoff patterns can often replicate a two-state claim. Assign weights x and y to the traded instruments. Write one equation per state equating the portfolio’s payoff to the claim’s payoff. Then price the portfolio by applying those weights to today’s traded prices.

A negative weight represents short selling or borrowing. If the instrument cannot be shorted, the computed portfolio may be algebraically correct yet unavailable to the investor. When assets’ payoff patterns move in exactly the same proportion, the equations may not have a unique solution spanning every claim. This is a question about what can be traded, not about solving harder arithmetic.

Construct the complete trade

If the market price of a claim exceeds the cost of replication, sell the claim and buy its replica. If cheaper, reverse both positions. Show cash at inception and net payoffs at every later state. A gain today with zero later obligations, or zero cost today and nonnegative future payoffs with positive payoff in some state, meets common frictionless arbitrage definitions.

Include bid and ask prices if given. Buy at the ask and sell at the bid. Financing haircuts, fees and different borrowing rates can create a range of prices without an executable gain. The existence of uncertainty does not defeat a hedge whose payments genuinely match in every state. Incomplete matching does.

Method

Price by replication

  1. Write payoff columns

    List values for each traded asset and claim in each state and ensure currency, timing and quantities agree.

  2. Solve and value

    Choose holdings matching every state, then multiply by today’s executable purchase or sale prices.

  3. Demonstrate the gain

    Specify the trades and show net cash today, net future cash in every state, and any constraint that blocks execution.

Worked case

A claim paying only in the strong state

A share costs €75 and pays €100 if demand is strong or €60 if weak. A safe claim costs €76 and pays €80 either way. A new claim pays €20 in the strong state and zero otherwise. Assume frictionless short sales.

Working

  1. Subtract the weak-state equation from the strong-state equation to get 40x = 20, so hold 0.5 share. In the weak state this pays €30.
  2. Choose y so €30 + €80y = 0, giving y = −0.375. The negative safe holding borrows against a €30 future repayment.
  3. Replica cost is 0.5 × €75 − 0.375 × €76 = €9. If the new claim sells for €11, short it and purchase the replica to collect €2 immediately.

Interpretation

In the strong state the replica pays €50 − €30 = €20, exactly the short obligation. In the weak state it pays zero, leaving no future exposure.

At the board

Build a forward rate by following two deposits

One euro buys US$1.10 today. Assume one-year simple rates of 2% in euros and 5% in dollars, the same borrowing and lending rates, and no costs. You need the forward price in dollars per euro.

In the idealized market, borrow US$1.10, use it to buy and invest €1, sell €1.02 forward, and repay US$1.155. The difference is US$0.0282. Real executable bid/offer quotes, funding costs and collateral decide whether the trade survives.

A common mistake

A student identifies a price difference but never checks the future short obligation.

How to correct it

Write the short claim with a negative payoff in every state and add the replica. A positive initial receipt alone is a loan if an unhedged future repayment remains.

Quick check
Which change most directly invalidates this specific trade?

Without the ability to sell the expensive claim, this trader cannot lock in the stated €2 using the proposed trades. Comparing the two prices alone is insufficient.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A share costs €50 and pays €70 or €40. A riskless unit costs €19 and pays €20 in either state. A target pays €15 in the high state and zero in the low state.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Solve for a replication and compute its cost.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    The target is offered for €4. Describe a trade and a specific trading restriction that could block it.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

The payoff spread is €30 per share, so x = 15/30 = 0.5. Low-state payout €20 must be offset by shorting one safe unit. Cost 0.5 × €50 − €19 = €6. High payoff is €35 − €20 = €15.

Part B 3 marks

Buy the target for €4. To sell its replica, short half a share for €25 and buy one safe unit for €19. The trades leave €2 in cash today. The future payoffs cancel in both states. A ban on shorting the share can block this direction.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How can a trader price a state-specific claim without guessing the real-world probability of that state?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would infer the prices of state-specific euros from the traded safe and risky assets. The equations here give €0.45 for a euro only in the high state and €0.50 in the low state. A €30-high, €10-low contract therefore costs €18.50 under replication. I need the spanning assets and executable prices, while a forecast of how often the high state occurs is unnecessary for this argument.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Can you express these as probabilities under a pricing measure, and how would you distinguish them from state prices?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Divide each state price by their sum of 0.95. The high pricing probability is about 47.37% and the low is 52.63%. Discounting the expected payoff at the safe discount factor 0.95 reconstructs price. These normalized weights are pricing probabilities, while the unnormalized state prices already include discounting.
