Discounting

Reverse the growth calculation to express a future payment in today’s terms. A higher positive discount rate reduces the present value of a fixed receipt.

The starting point

Someone promises you €242 in two years. To compare that promise with cash today, ask how much money today could grow into €242 by the payment date. This reverse growth calculation is discounting.

For a fixed rate r per period, divide the future amount by (1 + r) once for each period. The rate should reflect the cash flow’s currency, timing and risk. A higher rate reduces the current equivalent of a fixed positive future payment.

Theory and method

Present-value sensitivity and Macaulay duration

Discounted cash-flow weights · Modified duration

Two certain contracts have the same present value. Why can a small rate increase affect one of them more sharply?

A payment further away is discounted over more periods. A contract containing both early and late receipts behaves like a weighted collection of exposures to time. Use the present value of each receipt as its weight, since an enormous distant promise can matter more than a small near one. Macaulay duration turns these weights into an average payment time. It also yields a first-order sensitivity to a small common change in an annual yield.

How the result is built

D_M = Σₜ t × PVₜ / P. For a small yield change, ΔP/P ≈ −[D_M/(1+y)]Δy.

D_M is Macaulay duration in years, t payment time in years, PVₜ each payment’s present euro value, P total present value and y the common effective annual yield. Δ means a small change in the indicated variable.

  1. At a 10% yield, €110 in one year and €121 in two years each have present value €100. Total value is €200 and the weights are one half each.
  2. Weighted time is 0.5 × 1 + 0.5 × 2 = 1.5 years. Differentiating each discounted factor brings down a factor −t/(1+y), which explains the price sensitivity.
  3. Modified duration is 1.5/1.10 ≈ 1.3636. A 0.01 yield increase gives a first-order fall near 1.36%, or €2.73 from €200.

When the reasoning holds

  • The calculation uses one annual yield and fixed cash flows. Curve changes by maturity or cash flows that vary with rates require a different sensitivity model.
  • This is a local linear estimate. A large yield change requires full repricing because the relationship between yield and present value is curved.
Terms in this lesson
Discount rate
Rate used to express a future payment in today’s money, reflecting time and the relevant risk.
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Discount factor
The price today of one unit of currency payable at a particular future date under specified risk assumptions.
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Risk-neutral valuation
A way of pricing by taking a discounted expectation under a probability model consistent with traded prices.
How to read the notation
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.

All notation examples

First example

If a certain payment is appropriately discounted at 10% per year, €242 in two years is worth €242 / 1.10² = €200 today. Check by investing €200 for two years at 10% and recovering the original €242.

Choose a single valuation date

A promised €1,000 in three years and €1,000 today carry the same printed amount but different spending dates. Present value finds the amount at the valuation date that, invested at a suitable rate, produces the promised future payment. This allows a choice between genuinely comparable alternatives.

The valuation date need not be today. If asked for value one year from now, bring earlier cash forward and later cash backward to that particular date. Under consistent rates and assumptions, ranking at a common future date and ranking at today’s date will agree. Mixing amounts from different dates in one sum breaks that consistency.

Rates, risk and promised amounts

A one-period discount factor is the current price of one unit at the future date under the applicable assumptions. Discounting a certain cash amount at a rate reflecting its currency and horizon is straightforward. A risky promise requires a coherent treatment of default or uncertainty. A risky promised bond payoff is different from its expected payoff after default.

For a deliberately simple exercise, if a risk-neutral valuation with a stated risk-free rate is appropriate, average state payments under the given pricing probabilities and discount that expectation. In other problems a risk-adjusted rate is explicitly supplied for expected cash. Avoid deducting the same risk from the cash flow and then applying an arbitrary extra penalty without a consistent model.

What a change in rates does

A distant fixed receipt is divided by the growth factor more times than a near receipt. Raising a positive discount rate lowers both present values, with a larger proportional impact on the later payment under a common proportional change in the discount factor. This relationship underlies the sensitivity of long-dated promises.

The discount rate can in principle be negative. When r is between −1 and zero, the denominator 1+r is below one and a certain future receipt discounted using that rate has a higher present value than its face amount. Familiar inequalities about positive rates must therefore be stated with their assumptions.

Method

Move all cash to the same date

  1. Map the timeline

    Label dates 0, 1 and onward from the requested valuation date. Record cash promised or expected and its sign.

  2. Match the rate

    Use a compatible period, currency, risk treatment and compounding convention for the cash on each date.

  3. Verify by reversal

    Take each present value forward again to see whether it reaches the original amount. Compare the sum with the alternative price today.

Worked case

A choice of sale terms

A shop can receive €3,000 today or receive €1,100 in one year and €2,420 in two years. Assume both future amounts are certain and a 10% effective annual rate is available for this comparison.

Working

  1. The first future receipt is worth €1,100/1.10 = €1,000 today. The second is worth €2,420/1.10² = €2,000.
  2. Both deferred receipts together are worth €3,000 today. At 10% the seller is indifferent before liquidity constraints or fees.
  3. At a 5% rate the value is approximately €1,047.62 + €2,195.01 = €3,242.63, which favours waiting if all else is equal.

Interpretation

If the seller urgently needs cash and cannot borrow at the stated rate, the present-value comparison and practical feasibility may diverge.

A common mistake

All future cash is discounted for the maximum number of periods in the question.

How to correct it

Count the distance from valuation date to each individual payment. A one-year receipt and a three-year receipt require different exponents.

Quick check
At 10%, what is the present value of the same €242 paid after one year?

One year of discounting gives €242 / 1.10 = €220. An earlier positive payment has greater present value with the same positive rate.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A supplier offers €1,800 today in lieu of two certain year-end payments of €945 and €992.25. Use a 5% annual rate.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate the deferred offer’s present value and show the relationship to the immediate offer.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    If the second payment occurs a year later at the same amount, give the new value and explain the direction.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

€945/1.05 = €900 and €992.25/1.05² = €900. Total €1,800 equals immediate cash, so the offers are equivalent on the given assumptions.

Part B 4 marks

The second receipt now has present value €900/1.05 ≈ €857.14. Total is €1,757.14. Delaying the same payment introduces another positive-rate discount period.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Two certain contracts have the same present value. Why can a small rate increase affect one of them more sharply?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would compare where the present-value weights sit. For this contract an equal share of value arrives in each of years one and two, giving a 1.5-year Macaulay duration. At 10% yield its modified duration is about 1.36, so a one-percentage-point increase suggests a price loss near €2.73. A contract paying everything in two years would be more exposed at the same starting value.

Interviewer’s follow-up

At 11%, how close is that approximation to exact repricing?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The exact value is €110/1.11 + €121/1.11² ≈ €197.31, a fall of about €2.69. The linear estimate of €2.73 is a little larger. Reporting the exact number alongside the approximation shows the small curvature effect.
