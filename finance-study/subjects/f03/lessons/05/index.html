Annuities and perpetuities

Derive the value of repeated payments by adding a geometric series. Distinguish payments at the period start from payments at the end.

The starting point

Rent, pension payments and loan instalments often arrive in equal amounts at regular intervals. An annuity values a finite sequence of those payments. Start by drawing their dates because paying today changes the value.

For payments C at each period end, add C/(1+r), C/(1+r)² and so on. For a constant positive rate r and payments continuing forever, the sum is C/r. This perpetuity result assumes the first payment is one period away and the stream remains constant.

Theory and method

Finite growing annuity

Geometric series · Growing cash flow

A contract pays a first fee next year and increases it every year for a fixed term. How do you value it when growth approaches the discount rate?

A maintenance provider promises fees that rise with a stated index for a limited number of years. Each later amount grows, yet also waits another year to be received. Compare the relative size of consecutive discounted payments. If growth and discounting have the same rate, those adjustments cancel and each future fee has equal present value. A finite contract still has a usable valuation at that point by adding the individual discounted amounts.

How the result is built

PV = C₁/(1+r) × (1+q+⋯+qⁿ⁻¹), with q = (1+g)/(1+r). At g=r, PV = nC₁/(1+r).

C₁ is the first year-end euro payment, n is the finite number of payments, g the annual payment growth rate, r the annual discount rate and q the ratio between consecutive present values. PV is value today.

  1. Payment t equals C₁(1+g)ᵗ⁻¹. Divide by (1+r)ᵗ and factor out the first term C₁/(1+r).
  2. For q unequal to one, multiplying the sum by 1−q cancels adjacent terms, leaving 1−qⁿ. This gives the standard finite geometric sum.
  3. If both rates are 5%, q = 1 exactly. Three payments starting at €105 each contribute €100 of present value, for €300 in total.

When the reasoning holds

  • Each payment grows with a known constant rate and falls at the end of a matching annual period. Uneven index resets need a dated cash-flow schedule.
  • A finite stream can be valued even when g equals or exceeds r. For an unending stream of positive cash, growth must be sufficiently slower than discounting for a finite sum.
Terms in this lesson
Perpetuity
A stream of payments assumed to continue indefinitely under the model.
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Liability
Something owed to another party, such as a loan or an unpaid supplier bill.
How to read the notation
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.
Subscripts · S₀ and S₁
A small index names the time or item. If S₀ = €40 and S₁ = €42, the price moves from €40 at time 0 to €42 at time 1. A subscript i can label a company instead of a date.
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.

All notation examples

First example

Two €110 payments at the ends of years one and two, discounted at 10%, are worth €100 + €90.91 = €190.91. Paid at the start of those same years, they are worth €110 + €100 = €210.

Why the annuity formula works

A sequence of n equal payments C at each period end is worth the sum C/(1+r) + C/(1+r)² + … + C/(1+r)ⁿ. Multiplying that sum by 1+r shifts every term one place forward. Subtracting the original sum cancels the internal terms and leaves C − C/(1+r)ⁿ.

Rearranging gives present value = C × [1 − (1+r)^−n]/r for nonzero r. When r is zero the value is n × C. This derivation assumes a common effective rate per payment period. A staircase of different payments can be handled as separate ordinary cash flows or as blocks with matching dates.

Timing, deferral and a final balance

An annuity due pays at the beginning of each period. Moving every payment one period earlier multiplies its present value by 1+r. For a deferred annuity, first value the ordinary sequence one period before its first payment and then discount that intermediate value back to the chosen valuation date.

A final balloon is a separate payment added to the final instalment on its stated date. Mixing the balloon into every instalment would overstate the liability. A common exam technique is to label the valuation date, the date of the first regular instalment, and the date of the final extra balance.

A growing stream and its limit

A perpetuity promises payments forever. With a constant first payment C₁ one period from now and no growth, its value C₁/r follows as the limit of the annuity formula for positive r. The value is finite because later cash flows are progressively discounted to very small present amounts.

If payments grow at a constant rate g from the first future payment, the ratio of consecutive discounted terms is (1+g)/(1+r). When r exceeds g under the usual positive-factor assumptions, the sum converges to C₁/(r−g). Very small differences between r and g make the value highly sensitive. Replacing C₁ with an earlier historical payment misses one period of growth.

Method

Choose an annuity date

  1. Draw the sequence

    List the first two and final payment dates. Record whether the stream is finite, level, growing, or includes an extra amount.

  2. Value at its natural date

    Use the regular formula one period before the first payment. Add any separate balloon at its own discounted value.

  3. Move to the requested date

    Discount a deferred balance or roll an annuity due back one less period. Check a short stream by adding the actual payments individually.

Worked case

Deferred maintenance receipts

A contractor receives €110 at year two and €110 at year three, with nothing in year one. A separate €121 bonus is payable in year three. Discount all cash at 10% annually.

Working

  1. At the end of year one the two regular payments are worth €110/1.10 + €110/1.10² = €190.91.
  2. Bring that intermediate balance back to today by dividing again, giving €190.91/1.10 ≈ €173.55. The bonus today is €121/1.10³ ≈ €90.91.
  3. Total present value is about €264.46. Directly discounting €110 in year two and €231 in year three gives the same sum.

Interpretation

The annuity calculation at year one is an intermediate valuation only. Receiving nothing then does not prevent using that date for the calculation.

A common mistake

A deferred cash stream is delayed by as many extra periods as the date number of its first payment.

How to correct it

The ordinary annuity formula already discounts its first payment one period. With the first payment in year three, the ordinary formula gives value in year two, which then needs two additional discounts.

Quick check
What is the value of €30 each year forever if the first payment is in one year and the annual rate is 5%?

The perpetuity value is €30 / 0.05 = €600. At 5%, €600 can generate the €30 payment each year while keeping the original balance.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A trust will pay €60 at each of the next two year-ends and another €120 at the end of year two. Use 20% annually.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Find today’s value, separating regular and final amounts.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    A different trust promises €60 next year growing forever at 2%. Value it at 8% and explain why changing growth to 8% breaks the formula.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Regular payments are €60/1.2 + €60/1.2² = €50 + €41.67. The extra €120/1.2² = €83.33. Total €175, allowing rounding.

Part B 4 marks

Value is €60/(0.08 − 0.02) = €1,000. At equal discount and growth rates each discounted payment is the same positive amount. Their infinite sum does not converge to a finite value.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A contract pays a first fee next year and increases it every year for a fixed term. How do you value it when growth approaches the discount rate?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would write the first payment’s present value and the ratio between successive discounted terms. If growth equals the discount rate, the ratio is one, so adding the terms is especially straightforward. With €105 first, 5% growth and 5% discounting, each payment is worth €100 today. Three payments are worth €300. A division by zero in a shortcut is a signal to use the underlying sum.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If this same indexed payment were promised forever, could you still quote €300 or another finite value?

Compare with the follow-up answer

No finite value follows from this constant-rate model. Every future discounted term stays at €100, and an infinite sum of positive €100 amounts diverges. I would need a finite contract end, declining long-run growth, or another defensible change to the assumptions to produce a finite valuation.
