Can a reserve repay a bullet loan at maturity?

Calculate the reserve deposit and the combined cash burden on a bullet borrower.

Theory and terminology

The sinking-fund method of debt repayment

accumulated value · bullet repayment

What you should be able to explain

  • Calculate the reserve deposit and the combined cash burden on a bullet borrower.
  • Reconcile gross debt, reserve assets and the final funding shortfall after a reinvestment shock.

Follow two balances, not one

Under an amortizing loan each scheduled principal payment reduces the amount owed to the lender. Under the bullet contract here, principal stays unchanged until maturity and each coupon pays only for the use of that principal. The borrower places additional cash into a separate reserve, often called a sinking fund.

The reserve is an asset. Gross loan principal remains a liability until repayment. Net economic exposure may decline as the reserve grows, but whether assets and liabilities can be offset in financial statements is a separate accounting question. The distinction matters if the reserve is restricted or held with a different counterparty.

Build the reserve from its last date backward

Equal end-of-year deposits accumulate for different lengths of time. The last deposit earns no interest before immediate redemption, the penultimate one earns a single year's interest, and the first earns all but the final year's worth of periods. Add those accumulation factors to find the future value of one unit deposited each year.

Divide the promised redemption amount by that factor for the required deposit. If reserve earnings are zero, each deposit simply funds an equal slice of principal. Beginning-of-year deposits start earning one period earlier. Mistaking deposits in advance for deposits in arrears understates or overstates the required cash.

Compare both financing structures on the same basis

The annual cash commitment is loan interest plus the reserve deposit. If the two rates are equal and dates, costs and credit risk match, this equals the level amortizing installment. If the reserve earns less than the borrowing rate, carrying gross debt while accumulating a lower-yield asset generally requires a larger annual commitment.

A negative spread does not by itself settle the financing decision. The reserve may protect a contractual repayment, while an amortizing loan may have different prepayment rules. A rate labeled expected cannot guarantee redemption. Separate contractual contributions, market value of the reserve and a plan for any shortfall.

Method

Build the result

Fₜ = Fₜ₋₁(1 + j) + D. D = L / [1 + (1 + j) + ⋯ + (1 + j)^(n − 1)]. Annual cash commitment = Li + D.

Fₜ is the reserve after year t's end deposit, F₀ is zero, j is annual reserve yield, D is the annual end deposit, L is gross bullet principal, n the number of deposits and i the effective annual loan coupon rate. Amounts use the same currency.

  1. Set the target final reserve equal to L. Each deposit compounds at j for its remaining periods.
  2. Divide L by the sum of those accumulation factors to find D. Keep the coupon based on L throughout.
  3. Roll the fund forward, compare its value with the redemption target, and use the reserve plus any shortfall funding on the maturity date.
Worked case

A three-year equipment note

A borrower issues a three-year €30,909 bullet at 5% with annual coupons. It can earn a certain 3% on a dedicated reserve. Deposits arrive just after each year's reserve interest and immediately before any maturity payment. Ignore fees and tax.

Working

  1. Every coupon is €30,909 × 5% = €1,545.45. Scheduled principal before maturity stays €30,909.
  2. A €1 end deposit each year builds 1.03² + 1.03 + 1 = 3.0909 at maturity, so D is €30,909/3.0909 = €10,000.
  3. The reserve balances after deposit are €10,000, €20,300 and €30,909. In the final year interest contributes €609 before the last €10,000 deposit.
  4. The combined annual commitment is €11,545.45. An equivalent three-year amortizing loan at 5% needs €30,909 × 0.05/[1 − 1.05⁻³] = €11,350.05 per year.

Decision and interpretation

The two-rate arrangement costs €195.40 more annual cash here. Redeeming principal from the reserve uses the accumulated deposits and should not be counted as an additional unfunded €30,909 contribution.

Quick check
Which event reduces the contractual principal under the bullet terms in the worked case?

The reserve assets and loan balance are separate until redemption. The coupon pays interest on the outstanding gross principal.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

A two-year £41,600 bullet charges 6% annual interest. Deposits occur at each year-end, and the originally assumed reserve yield is 8%. After the first deposit, the available second-year reserve rate falls to 2%. Calculate the revised final funding need.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked solution
  1. Each coupon is £41,600 × 6% = £2,496. The original reserve deposits were £41,600/(1.08 + 1) = £20,000.
  2. At the revised rate the first £20,000 grows to £20,400. Adding the planned final £20,000 yields £40,400.
  3. The reserve is £1,200 short. To redeem in full, make a £21,200 final deposit and pay the separate £2,496 coupon.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

The borrower pays every coupon on time. Why might it still be unable to repay principal?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

The coupon only services interest. If principal is due in one lump, cash must accumulate separately or refinancing must be available. A reserve invested below its assumed rate may fall short.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What changes if the lender is entitled to use each reserve deposit immediately to reduce contractual principal?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The loan terms now provide a form of amortization. Rebuild interest on the reducing contractual balance and trace who earns any interest on cash. The coupon on an unchanged face amount is no longer the correct schedule.
