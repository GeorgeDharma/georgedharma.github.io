Rates and units

A rise from 4% to 5% is one percentage point. Record the currency, sign, period and rate convention before calculating.

The starting point

A news report says a loan rate rose from 4% to 5%. A borrower needs to know what changed in their bill. The difference between these rates is one percentage point, equivalent to 100 basis points. Relative to the old rate, the increase is 25%.

Attach a unit and a period to every number. A rate per month cannot simply be used as a rate per year. Record signs from one party’s perspective so that money received and money paid carry opposite signs throughout the calculation.

Theory and method

Bank discount yield and investment yield

Discount-basis quotation · Money-market yield

A 90-day bill is quoted at a 4% bank discount rate. Why does a buyer earn a different simple annual return on the cash actually invested?

A treasury bill pays a fixed face amount at maturity and is purchased for less. A bank discount quote describes the discount as a fraction of the face amount. The investor’s return compares the same cash gain with the smaller price paid. A perfectly consistent contract can display different percentage rates simply because their denominators differ. Write the face value, price, calendar length and denominator convention before comparing it with a deposit quote.

How the result is built

P = F(1−dN/B); y = [(F−P)/P] × B/N.

F is bill face value, P purchase price, d quoted bank discount rate per year, N actual days, B the annual day-count base and y the simple annualized return on invested cash using the same base. Money quantities are euros here.

  1. A €100,000 face-value bill has N = 90, B = 360 and d = 0.04. The cash discount is €100,000 × 0.04 × 90/360 = €1,000.
  2. The investor pays €99,000 and receives €100,000. The holding-period return is €1,000/€99,000 ≈ 1.0101%.
  3. Annualize this simple holding return by 360/90 = 4 to obtain approximately 4.0404%. No reinvestment is assumed by that simple annualization.

When the reasoning holds

  • Use the same 360-day base for both quotes to isolate the denominator effect. A deposit on an actual/365 basis requires a separate conversion.
  • The bill is held to maturity with certain payment and negligible fees. Selling before maturity can realize a different cash gain.
Terms in this lesson
Percentage point
The difference between two percentages. A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises one percentage point.
Basis point
One hundredth of a percentage point. A rise from 4.00% to 4.01% is one basis point.
Principal
The amount borrowed or invested before interest is added.
How to read the notation
Basis points and percentage points
A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises by one percentage point, or 100 basis points. Its increase relative to the original rate is (5 − 4) ÷ 4 = 25%.

All notation examples

First example

On €10,000 of principal outstanding for a full year, simple annual interest moves from €400 to €500. The €100 change equals principal times the change in rate, €10,000 × 0.01, with no fees or repayment during the year.

A quote needs a clock and a base

An annual interest quote of 6% can mean several different things. It might be a simple rate used to accrue interest over a stated fraction of a year, a nominal rate split into monthly periods, or an effective rate stating actual one-year growth. Before multiplying, state which interpretation the contract or question supplies.

A basis point is 0.01 percentage points, or 0.0001 as a decimal rate. A move of 40 basis points changes a decimal rate by 0.004. A percentage change in the rate itself uses the old rate as the denominator. These calculations answer different questions even when printed as adjacent numbers.

Time fractions in simple interest

For simple interest, charge I = P × r × t, where P is outstanding principal, r is the decimal annual rate and t is the fraction of a year. If part of the principal is repaid during the period, split the interval into pieces with their own balances before calculating charges.

Under an actual/360 convention, 30 actual days count as 30/360 years. Actual/365 fixed instead uses 30/365. This difference can matter on large balances. Other conventions exist. Use the stated convention and the actual accrual dates supplied, and avoid adding an unmentioned day at each end of the interval.

Signs and currency in a timeline

A borrower receives principal now and pays interest and principal later. A lender records the opposite signs at each date. Keeping one perspective prevents a supposed net benefit from appearing when both sides of the same transfer are accidentally added. Within one currency, interest has the same currency unit as principal.

If a term sheet quotes a margin over a floating reference, add percentage points in matching units before multiplying. A 120-basis-point margin means 1.20 percentage points above the reference. A hypothetical change in annual rates does not affect an entire year’s expense when the quoted balance is outstanding for only part of that year.

Method

Turn a rate quote into a bill

  1. Transcribe the quote

    Write currency, principal, decimal annual rate, accrual dates and day-count basis. Identify whose cash flow you are measuring.

  2. Split the schedule

    Create a new time segment whenever principal or the contractual rate changes and calculate each segment separately.

  3. Check scale

    A one-percentage-point change on €100,000 for a year is €1,000 under simple annual interest. Use scale and duration to spot decimal errors.

Worked case

A seasonal revolving line

A retailer owes €240,000 for 45 days, repays €90,000 and owes the remaining balance for 30 further days. Rate is 4.2% simple, actual/360, with no fees. Use the day counts exactly as given.

Working

  1. First segment interest is €240,000 × 0.042 × 45/360 = €1,260.
  2. Second segment principal is €150,000. Its interest is €150,000 × 0.042 × 30/360 = €525. Total charge is €1,785.
  3. A 50-basis-point higher annual rate throughout adds €240,000 × 0.005 × 45/360 + €150,000 × 0.005 × 30/360 = €212.50.

Interpretation

The borrower’s interest is a cash outflow when paid. The €90,000 reduction of principal is a separate financing outflow.

A common mistake

A 0.40-percentage-point move is entered as 0.40 in a formula expecting a decimal rate.

How to correct it

Convert percent to decimal before multiplying. Here the change is 0.004. Use the 100-basis-points-per-percentage-point identity as a second check.

Quick check
A rate moves from 3.50% to 3.75%. How large is the move?

The difference is 0.25 percentage points. Each percentage point contains 100 basis points, so the move is 25 basis points.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A €360,000 facility charges a 3.5% reference plus 150 basis points for exactly 60 days, simple actual/360. Principal is constant.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Find the total rate and interest. What would a 25-basis-point increase in the reference add during the same period?

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    If the quoted reference instead falls from 3.5% to 3.0%, distinguish the change in percentage points from its relative decline.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

The rate is 3.5% + 1.5% = 5%. Interest is €360,000 × 0.05 × 60/360 = €3,000. A 0.0025 increase adds €150.

Part B 3 marks

The reference falls 0.5 percentage points, or 50 basis points. Relative to 3.5%, the decline is 0.5/3.5 ≈ 14.29%. The spread does not change.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A 90-day bill is quoted at a 4% bank discount rate. Why does a buyer earn a different simple annual return on the cash actually invested?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would show the buyer the cash price first. The quote subtracts €1,000 from face value, so the investment is €99,000 and the gain at maturity is €1,000. That is 1.0101% on cash committed for 90 days, or about 4.0404% on the same simple 360-day basis. I would bring any competing deposit quote onto the same basis before ranking it.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If the investor can reinvest the same fraction for four identical 90-day intervals, is 4.0404% the resulting annual growth?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The four interval growth factors multiply. Using 100/99 each time yields (100/99)⁴−1, approximately 4.1020%. The 4.0404% figure is simple annualization. The compounding result also requires the same reinvestment opportunity in all four intervals.
