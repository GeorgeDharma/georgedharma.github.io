Inflation and real values

Separate growth in purchasing power from growth in money amounts. A nominal cash forecast needs a compatible nominal discount rate.

The starting point

A salary can rise in euros while buying fewer groceries. A nominal amount is measured in money at the date stated. A real amount expresses purchasing power relative to a chosen price level.

To find purchasing-power growth, divide one plus the nominal growth rate by one plus inflation, then subtract one. Subtracting inflation from the nominal rate is only an approximation. When valuing cash flows, use real rates with real amounts and nominal rates with nominal amounts on a consistent basis.

Theory and method

Fisher relation and unexpected inflation

Ex ante real rate · Ex post real rate

A borrower and lender set a nominal interest rate using expected inflation. Who gains in purchasing-power terms if inflation turns out much higher?

A lender agrees to accept a fixed number of euros a year from now. At signing, the parties think about how much those euros should buy. If prices later rise more than expected, the promised euros purchase fewer goods while the contractual amount stays fixed. Compare planned and realized purchasing-power growth using the same principal and maturity. This identifies a transfer through an existing fixed nominal promise, even though both parties still follow its payment terms.

How the result is built

1 + real rate = (1 + nominal rate)/(1 + inflation rate).

The real rate measures growth in purchasing-power units. The nominal rate measures growth in money. Use expected inflation for an ex ante comparison and observed inflation for a realized ex post comparison. All rates are effective for one year.

  1. The parties plan a 2% real rate with 3% expected inflation. The compatible nominal factor is 1.02 × 1.03 = 1.0506, or 5.06%.
  2. On €10,000 principal the promised payment is €10,506. Had inflation been 3%, its base-date purchasing power would have been €10,200.
  3. If actual inflation is 8%, the same €10,506 buys €10,506/1.08 ≈ €9,727.78 of base-date goods. The realized real return is about −2.72%.

When the reasoning holds

  • The contractual euro payment is fixed, with no indexation, renegotiation or default. An inflation-linked loan reallocates the exposure.
  • The price index used represents the purchasing power of interest. A borrower whose income fails to keep pace with prices may still face payment stress in cash terms.
Terms in this lesson
Discount rate
Rate used to express a future payment in today’s money, reflecting time and the relevant risk.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
How to read the notation
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.

All notation examples

First example

Savings grow from €100 to €106 while a comparable basket rises from €100 to €104. Real growth is 1.06 / 1.04 − 1 ≈ 1.92%. Purchasing power rose, but by less than the 6% change in the bank statement.

Use a consistent purchasing-power unit

Imagine a university scholarship rising 4% while rent and food rise 6%. The nominal scholarship is larger, yet a fixed basket of goods is harder to afford. To express an amount from date t in base-date purchasing power, divide it by the price index at t relative to the base index.

A single consumer price index represents a specified basket, and different households may face different inflation. If a model assumes one general inflation rate, use it consistently for the relevant cash flows. A contract linked to a particular commodity or wage index can behave differently from a claim to general purchasing power.

Connect nominal and real rates

Let i be the effective nominal return, π inflation and r the realized real return over the same period. One unit of money grows to 1+i in currency and each basket costs 1+π times the previous price. The real growth factor is (1+i)/(1+π), so 1+i = (1+r)(1+π).

The approximation r ≈ i−π omits the cross-product in the exact relation. The discrepancy is small for modest rates but can matter materially at high inflation. If inflation is unknown in advance, distinguish the real rate calculated using forecast inflation from the real return actually delivered after realized inflation is observed.

Two ways to value an inflation-linked flow

Suppose a future payment preserves fixed present purchasing power. Inflate it to its future cash amount and discount with a nominal rate, or leave it in base-date purchasing power and discount using the corresponding real rate. The two present values coincide if inflation and rates are deterministic, internally consistent and use the same timing.

A fixed nominal payment does not promise fixed real value. When inflation changes after a fixed-rate contract is signed, the borrower’s and lender’s purchasing-power outcomes change. To evaluate an indexed contract, read the base date, lag, cap and index. A promise indexed with a time lag can leave some inflation exposure.

Method

Reconcile real and nominal valuations

  1. Select a price base

    State the currency date for each figure. Mark fixed money payments separately from amounts promised in purchasing-power units.

  2. Convert the rates

    Use the exact ratio for the matching period. Distinguish inflation already realized from inflation assumed for a forecast.

  3. Check both routes

    Inflate real cash then discount at the nominal rate, and separately discount real cash at the implied real rate. Agreement checks the model.

Worked case

A guaranteed equipment allowance

A firm promises a payment one year ahead worth €1,000 in today’s purchasing power. Assume 8% inflation, a 2% real discount rate and full indexation. Ignore tax and risk.

Working

  1. The promised nominal payment is €1,000 × 1.08 = €1,080. The compatible nominal growth factor for a year is 1.02 × 1.08 = 1.1016.
  2. Discounting nominal cash gives €1,080/1.1016 ≈ €980.39. Discounting real cash gives €1,000/1.02 ≈ €980.39.
  3. If the promise were fixed at €1,000 nominal instead, its future purchasing power would be €1,000/1.08 ≈ €925.93 in today’s basket units.

Interpretation

A different index or a ceiling on indexation would alter the actual cash payment. The simple equivalence above uses the same stated general inflation.

A common mistake

A forecast already inflated to future euros is discounted using a real rate, producing a high value.

How to correct it

Label every cash-flow column as nominal or as a stated base year’s purchasing power, and apply its matched rate. Reconcile a sample period by both routes.

Quick check
What nominal rate exactly preserves purchasing power when prices rise 3%?

A saver needs 1.03 units tomorrow per unit today to buy the same basket. Dividing 1.03 by 1.03 leaves real growth of zero.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A one-year investment turns €500 into €550. The price index rises from 125 to 137.5. Separately, a planner requires a 3% real return for another one-year project facing the same deterministic inflation.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate the investment’s real return and its final value in starting-date euros.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Determine the planner’s exact nominal required rate and value a cash flow worth €200 today in purchasing power, payable next year.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Inflation is 137.5/125 − 1 = 10%. Nominal return is 10%, so real return = 1.10/1.10 − 1 = 0%. Ending money buys the original €500 basket.

Part B 4 marks

The nominal factor is 1.03 × 1.10 = 1.133, so 13.3%. Nominal payment €220 discounted by 1.133 gives €194.17, also €200/1.03.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A borrower and lender set a nominal interest rate using expected inflation. Who gains in purchasing-power terms if inflation turns out much higher?

Compare with a spoken answer

Relative to the expected real terms, the lender loses and the fixed-rate borrower repays in euros with less purchasing power. Here the promised nominal €10,506 represents roughly €9,728 of starting-date goods at 8% inflation. The realized real return is negative even though the nominal interest rate is positive. That conclusion concerns the existing contract and the chosen inflation index.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If the principal is fully indexed and the contract promises 2% real interest, what cash amount would the borrower pay under 8% inflation?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Full indexation increases the €10,000 base principal by 1.08 and the real interest factor is 1.02. The nominal settlement becomes €11,016, worth €10,200 in starting-date goods. This calculation assumes no lag, cap or alternative index in the contract.
