Quoted and effective rates

A nominal annual quote compounded monthly produces a different effective annual rate. Convert the cash-flow frequency and rate to matching periods.

The starting point

A bank advertises 12% per year with monthly compounding. The printed annual number does not show how much the balance actually grows if every month’s interest stays in the account. First identify how the rate is quoted.

For a nominal annual rate convertible monthly, divide the quote by 12 to get the monthly rate. Multiply the monthly growth factors to find the effective annual rate. An effective annual quote requires the reverse operation to recover its matching monthly rate.

Theory and method

Internal rate of return on net loan proceeds

Effective borrowing cost · Cash-flow yield

Two lenders quote 7% annual interest, but one deducts a fee from the advance. What comparable annual borrowing cost would you show?

A borrower receives money they can actually spend and later must pay the contractual bill. A fee taken from the advance reduces usable funding even if the printed interest rate never changes. Put amounts on the borrower's timeline after every mandatory charge, then solve the rate that equates those proceeds with the later repayments. This produces a cash-flow yield for the stated contract. It also lets a treasury analyst compare loans with different payment dates or fee structures.

How the result is built

Net proceeds = Σₜ paymentₜ/(1+i)ᵗ. For one year and one payment, i = payment/net proceeds − 1.

Net proceeds are the actual euros available to the borrower after the initial fee. Paymentₜ includes required cash at date t in years, and i is the effective annual cash-flow cost under the exercise terms.

  1. A €10,000 one-year loan with 7% coupon repays €10,700 at maturity. A 2% origination fee deducted now leaves €9,800 to spend.
  2. The comparable one-year cash factor is €10,700/€9,800 = 1.0918367. Its effective cost is approximately 9.18%.
  3. A fee-free €10,000 loan at 8% costs 8% on the same annual basis, below the fee-paying quote even though its printed interest rate is higher.

When the reasoning holds

  • The fee is compulsory and withheld at inception, with one final repayment. A fee paid later enters the equation at its own date.
  • Use the same borrowing horizon, currency and payment assumptions. This economic comparison is distinct from any jurisdiction-specific legal disclosure formula.
Terms in this lesson
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
How to read the notation
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.
Natural logarithm · ln
The log return is ln(ending price ÷ starting price). From €100 to €105 it equals ln(1.05) ≈ 0.04879. Log returns add across successive periods. The ordinary percentage gain for this period is 5%.

All notation examples

First example

A nominal 12% annual rate gives 1% each month. Over a year €100 becomes €100 × 1.01¹² ≈ €112.68. The effective annual rate is about 12.68%, assuming reinvestment and no fees.

A stated annual number is not always annual growth

A deposit quotes a nominal annual rate j convertible m times per year. The prescribed periodic rate is j/m, and the account earns it m times. Its effective annual factor is therefore (1+j/m)ᵐ. Write the label alongside the number to differentiate it from an effective annual rate, which directly states the actual one-year growth.

For a given positive nominal rate, more frequent compounding produces higher annual growth if interest is reinvested. Comparing two contracts using their nominal numbers alone can rank them incorrectly. Accounts charging mandatory fees or making different payment schedules warrant a cash-flow comparison, since equal interest conventions do not guarantee equal consumer costs.

Convert without counting interest twice

An effective annual rate i contains its compounding already. The equivalent monthly rate is (1+i)^(1/12) − 1, not i/12. The growth factors should satisfy (1+r_month)¹² = 1+i. When changing from annual to quarterly effective rates, take a fourth root for exactly the same reason.

For a nominal monthly-compounding quote, a quarter spans three successive monthly periods. Its effective growth is (1+j/12)³ − 1. Taking a quarterly rate four times reproduces the effective annual factor. Pick one representation and remain consistent with the payment dates when discounting or calculating instalments.

Continuous compounding as a limiting quote

Some financial models quote a continuously compounded annual rate c. Its one-year growth factor is exp(c), and its equivalent effective rate is exp(c) − 1. Conversely c = ln(1+i), where ln is the natural logarithm. This convention is useful because continuously compounded returns add across consecutive periods when defined on positive wealth ratios.

All these expressions can describe exactly the same cash flows. The change of convention itself creates no extra income. A rise in an effective rate under a fixed nominal quote occurs because the contract actually credits interest more frequently. Converting an existing contract’s quote for comparison changes only the label.

Method

Compare offers on one basis

  1. Read the label

    Separate nominal rates and compounding frequency from effective rates. Identify fees and the term for which money is invested.

  2. Build a common factor

    Convert each offer to a one-year growth factor, or to the same payment-period factor if the horizon is shorter.

  3. Apply cash charges

    Calculate the actual amount received after stated deductions. Verify that equivalent conversions give the same result for a sample balance.

Worked case

Two deposit offers

Offer A pays 8% nominal with quarterly compounding. Offer B pays 8.20% effective annually. The depositor places €5,000 for one year and can reinvest interest. Initially assume no fees.

Working

  1. A credits 2% each quarter. Its annual factor is 1.02⁴ = 1.08243216, producing €5,412.16 and an 8.243216% effective rate.
  2. B produces €5,410.00. A exceeds B by about €2.16 before charges.
  3. A €5 maturity fee unique to A reduces its proceeds to €5,407.16, reversing the comparison. A small rate advantage does not establish a better net offer.

Interpretation

Reporting A as simply 8% would miss the quarterly reinvestment. Reporting B as 8.20%/4 per quarter would improperly convert an effective annual rate.

A common mistake

An effective annual loan rate is divided by twelve and applied monthly, overstating annual growth for a positive rate.

How to correct it

Take the twelfth root of the annual factor and subtract one. Then compound the resulting monthly rate twelve times as a check.

Quick check
A loan quotes a 10% effective annual rate. Which expression gives an equivalent six-month rate?

Two six-month growth factors must multiply to 1.10. Each is the square root of 1.10, giving a six-month rate of about 4.88%.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A deposit quotes 10% nominal compounded semiannually, while another offers 10.20% effective annually. Both accept €2,000 for one year on equal fee-free terms.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate the year-end proceeds on both contracts and rank them.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    An analyst uses an annual effective rate of 10.25% to value a payment six months away. Determine the equivalent half-year rate, then the value today of €1,050 in six months.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

First periodic rate is 5%. Its annual factor 1.05² = 1.1025 gives €2,205. Second gives €2,204. First exceeds second by €1.

Part B 4 marks

The half-year factor is √1.1025 = 1.05, giving 5%. Present value is €1,050/1.05 = €1,000. Dividing 10.25% by two would not reproduce the contractual growth factor.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Two lenders quote 7% annual interest, but one deducts a fee from the advance. What comparable annual borrowing cost would you show?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would compare the actual amount received with every unavoidable payment. Here the client receives €9,800 and returns €10,700, making the one-year cost about 9.18%. The 7% headline describes interest on face value and leaves out the withheld €200. If the client needs exactly €10,000 in usable funding, I would also scale the face amount and calculate the euro payments on that common funding basis.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What face amount is needed to deliver exactly €10,000 usable cash under the 2% fee, and how much is due in one year?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Face amount satisfies 0.98F = €10,000, so F ≈ €10,204.08. Multiplying by 1.07 gives a maturity payment near €10,918.37. The cash-flow cost remains approximately 9.18% because the fee and repayment scaled proportionally.
