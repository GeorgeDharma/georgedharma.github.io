Investment criteria

Net present value subtracts investment cost from discounted benefits. Internal rate of return is the rate that makes that difference zero and can have multiple solutions.

The starting point

Two projects can advertise the same percentage return and still create different amounts of value. Net present value, or NPV, puts the cash flows on today’s footing and subtracts the resources committed. It expresses the gain in currency.

The internal rate of return, or IRR, is a discount rate at which NPV is zero. For an initial outlay followed by positive receipts it can be a useful percentage summary. With later cleanup costs or other sign changes, there can be more than one IRR, or none.

Theory and method

Equivalent annual annuity

Equivalent annual cost · Replacement-chain comparison

Two machines supply the same service but wear out after different intervals. Can you choose by comparing the present cost of buying each once?

A clinic needs equipment continuously. A cheap two-year machine and a more expensive three-year model do different amounts of service over one purchase cycle. If replacements will genuinely repeat on the same terms, a common annual cost makes their costs comparable. Convert each machine’s full discounted lifecycle cost into the level end-year payment with the same present value. A device with a higher purchase price can then be cheaper per year of comparable service.

How the result is built

EAC = PV of lifecycle costs / aₙ(r), where aₙ(r) = Σₜ₌₁ⁿ(1+r)⁻ᵗ.

EAC is equivalent annual cost in euros per year. PV is the present cost of purchase, operation and disposal net of residual proceeds. n is useful life in years and r the annual discount rate.

  1. Machine A costs €1,100 now for two years of service. At 10% its annuity factor is about 1.73554 and its annual cost is €633.81.
  2. Machine B costs €1,500 now for three years. Its annuity factor is about 2.48685 and its annual cost is €603.17, assuming no other costs or residual receipts.
  3. With identical service and repeat purchase conditions, B saves about €30.64 per year on an equivalent basis even though its one-time outlay is higher.

When the reasoning holds

  • The service is comparable and successive replacement cycles can be evaluated on the same economic terms. Obsolescence or changing costs can invalidate a repeated-cycle comparison.
  • Demand persists for the common service horizon. If the clinic closes after two years, B’s resale value and the actual two-year horizon matter.
Terms in this lesson
Net present value (NPV)
Today’s value of incoming cash minus today’s value of outgoing cash.
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Discount rate
Rate used to express a future payment in today’s money, reflecting time and the relevant risk.
Working capital
Money tied up in day-to-day operations. For a deal, agree which receivables, stock and payables count.
Counterfactual
What would probably have happened without the event, decision or policy being studied.
How to read the notation
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.

All notation examples

First example

A project costs €100 now and pays €121 in one year. At a 10% opportunity cost, NPV is €121 / 1.10 − €100 = €10. The rate that makes the investment break even is 21%, because €100 × 1.21 = €121.

Translate the investment decision into cash

A project that reports high accounting earnings may require substantial working capital before customers pay. Net present value uses the incremental cash flows caused by accepting rather than rejecting the project. Include replacement or salvage proceeds and the opportunity cost of resources already owned when they would be sacrificed.

Write the counterfactual explicitly. Prior feasibility spending may be sunk and should not be counted again. A recovery of working capital at the end is a receipt only if that cash is genuinely released. Discount each cash flow at a rate consistent with its risk, dates and currency. A positive NPV means this cash-flow plan exceeds its opportunity cost under those assumptions.

What the internal rate can and cannot do

The internal rate of return is any rate making the NPV equal to zero. For one initial negative cash flow followed only by positive receipts, NPV usually falls as the positive discount rate rises and there is a unique economically relevant root when the sign changes across rates. This makes the IRR a useful threshold for a conventional project.

Unconventional projects can change sign again when cleanup or decommissioning costs arrive. Their NPV can cross zero more than once. For example the cash pattern −100, +230, −132 has roots at 10% and 20% because substituting either rate gives zero. Between those values NPV has a different sign from some rates outside them. Use the NPV at the actual opportunity cost.

Ranking projects that compete

Two independent projects with positive NPVs can both add value if sufficient resources exist. Two mutually exclusive plans require choosing one. A high percentage return on a tiny outlay can produce less total value than a lower percentage return on a large investment. Compare NPVs or analyze the incremental cash flows of choosing the larger plan over the smaller.

Payback period measures when cumulative cash recovers the initial outlay. It can reveal exposure to illiquidity, but ordinary undiscounted payback ignores the time value of money and all later receipts after the cutoff. When capital or capacity is limited, establish the actual feasible combinations rather than mechanically selecting the single highest IRR.

Method

Decide between competing proposals

  1. Forecast incremental cash

    Include today’s outlay, later operating cash, any released working capital and disposal amounts. Remove costs paid regardless of the decision.

  2. Discount and rank

    Calculate NPV at the stated opportunity cost, compare alternatives at the same valuation date, and inspect the incremental cash stream.

  3. Stress the decision

    Find which disputed input could change the sign or ranking. Explain when IRR or payback supports a different ranking and why.

Worked case

Large and small licensing projects

License A costs €50 today and pays €65 in one year. License B costs €500 and pays €590 then. The options are mutually exclusive, equally risky and affordable. The annual required return is 10%.

Working

  1. A has IRR 30% and NPV €65/1.10 − €50 ≈ €9.09.
  2. B has IRR 18% and NPV €590/1.10 − €500 ≈ €36.36. It adds about €27.27 more value than A.
  3. Switching from A to B requires another €450 and yields another €525 next year. Incremental return is 525/450 − 1 ≈ 16.67%, above 10%.

Interpretation

B is preferable at the specified rate and with sufficient funding. A budget cap of €100 would change feasibility even though it does not change either standalone NPV.

A common mistake

An analyst rejects a multi-sign project because one of its two IRRs falls below the hurdle rate.

How to correct it

Evaluate actual NPV at the hurdle and inspect its sign profile. A single inequality comparing an ambiguous root with the hurdle cannot establish the investment decision.

Quick check
Two mutually exclusive projects have comparable risks and no funding constraint. Which measure directly compares the value added today?

NPV measures the currency gain after allowing for timing, the appropriate discount rate and the initial outlay. Scale can make the project with the higher percentage return add less value.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

Two mutually exclusive one-year projects produce certain cash. X costs €200 and pays €250. Y costs €800 and pays €960. Opportunity cost is 10% and both are affordable.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Calculate NPVs and IRRs and choose the project.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Find the one-year incremental return of Y over X and interpret its relation to the opportunity cost.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

X has NPV €27.27 and IRR 25%. Y has NPV €72.73 and IRR 20%. Y adds €45.46 more value, allowing rounding, so choose Y with both feasible.

Part B 3 marks

The incremental investment is €600 and incremental payoff €710. Return is 710/600 − 1 ≈ 18.33%, above the 10% required rate. That explains why the larger project’s extra scale is worthwhile.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Two machines supply the same service but wear out after different intervals. Can you choose by comparing the present cost of buying each once?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would compare annualized lifecycle costs if the clinic needs the same output indefinitely and can replace each machine on similar terms. At 10% the two-year option costs about €634 per equivalent year and the three-year option about €603. I would select B under those assumptions. For a finite closure date I would instead model the specific terminal sale or replacement cash flows.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If B requires €45 more maintenance at each year-end than A, does the initial recommendation survive?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Its annualized cost rises by €45 because that additional stream is already a level cost across its own three-year cycle. B becomes about €648.17 per year, above A’s €633.81. Under unchanged service and replacement assumptions I would switch to A.
