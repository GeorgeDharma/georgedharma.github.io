Term-specific rates

Cash at different maturities may command different rates. Discount each dated payment using a compatible spot rate or discount factor.

The starting point

A payment in one year and a payment in five years need not face the same borrowing rate. Investors may charge different annual rates for each maturity. The set of rates for single future payments is called a spot curve.

A discount factor tells you the present value of one unit paid at a specified date. Multiply each promised payment by the factor for its own date, currency and risk, and add. If annual spot rates are supplied, a two-year factor uses the two-year rate for both years in its exponent.

Theory and method

Cash-flow matching with zero-coupon bonds

Dedication strategy · Discount-factor valuation

A pension plan owes three fixed payments at known dates. What funding amount today locks in those payments using zero-coupon bonds?

A zero-coupon bond delivers a single principal payment at maturity. To fund a future liability, buy enough of the bond maturing on the same date so its payoff equals the liability. Repeat separately at each date. The plan then has matching cash receipts when bills fall due and does not depend on obtaining a favorable reinvestment rate in between. Prices today translate the future payoffs into the initial amount required.

How the result is built

Unitsₜ = liabilityₜ / bond face amountₜ. Initial cost = Σₜ liabilityₜ × Dₜ.

t is a future payment date, liabilities and bond face amounts are in euros and Dₜ is today’s price per euro paid at date t. Units may be fractional in the exercise.

  1. The plan owes €60,000 in year one, €80,000 in year two and €100,000 in year three. Available zeros have €100 face with prices €96, €90 and €84.
  2. Purchase 600, 800 and 1,000 units respectively. Costs are €57,600, €72,000 and €84,000.
  3. Total required funding is €213,600 now. At each maturity the bond position pays exactly that period’s liability, leaving zero residual date-by-date.

When the reasoning holds

  • Zeros and liabilities use the same currency, maturity dates and dependable credit basis. Holding a riskier zero can leave the obligation unfunded on default.
  • The plan can buy the required quantities, segregate them and hold until maturity. Lot sizes, taxes or forced sales introduce residual exposure.
Terms in this lesson
Discount factor
The price today of one unit of currency payable at a particular future date under specified risk assumptions.
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
Interest rate
The price of borrowing money or the return for lending it, quoted as a percentage over a stated period.
Coupon
The interest payment specified in a bond’s terms.
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Settlement
The final transfer of cash or securities needed to complete a transaction.
How to read the notation
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.
Subscripts · S₀ and S₁
A small index names the time or item. If S₀ = €40 and S₁ = €42, the price moves from €40 at time 0 to €42 at time 1. A subscript i can label a company instead of a date.
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.

All notation examples

First example

Suppose annual spot rates are 5% for one year and 10% for two. A claim paying €105 in year one and €121 in year two is worth €105/1.05 + €121/1.10² = €200.

Prices by maturity

Think of a discount factor Dₜ as the current price of receiving one unit at date t under a specified risk and currency basis. A strip of these factors values a known cash schedule without requiring all maturities to share one rate. For annual effective spot rate sₜ, Dₜ = 1/(1+sₜ)ᵗ.

A two-year annual spot rate describes a two-year investment starting now at a single compound rate. It is different from the one-year interest rate that will actually be available next year. A coupon bond’s single yield to maturity is a summary rate reproducing its price and need not equal any particular rate on the spot curve.

Recover an unknown discount factor

Start with a short instrument paying only one known amount. Its price divided by payoff gives the factor to that date. A longer instrument has early coupons that can already be priced with previously inferred factors. Subtract their present values from its price and divide the remainder by the final cash payment.

This stepwise recovery is called bootstrapping. It depends on comparable credit quality, currency, settlement and treatment of coupons. If one bond is riskier or materially less liquid, its price difference may include that effect. Then treating its price as pure evidence about time can produce a misleading curve.

What an implied forward rate means

A one-year forward rate from year one to year two can be inferred by comparing investing to year two directly with investing to year one and entering a contract today that fixes the reinvestment rate. With D₁ and D₂ on the same basis, the implied forward growth factor is D₁/D₂.

That rate follows from current prices and the availability of equivalent trades. It is not a guaranteed prediction of the future spot rate available in a year. Risk compensation and market expectations can contribute to the current term structure. Distinguish locking the forward today from waiting to see what rate will prevail next year.

Method

Build the curve one maturity at a time

  1. Find the first factor

    Use the shortest single promised cash flow and identify the clean price/cash assumptions supplied.

  2. Peel off earlier cash

    For the next instrument subtract each known coupon multiplied by its own discount factor. Solve for the final unknown factor.

  3. Test the curve

    Reprice every instrument and calculate a forward factor as a consistency check. Annotate any credit, liquidity or timing differences.

Worked case

Two notes from the same issuer

A one-year zero-coupon note paying €100 costs €96. A second note costs €99.15 and pays €5 in year one and €105 in year two. Both have the same risk and currency, with no fees or accrued interest.

Working

  1. First factor D₁ = 96/100 = 0.96. The first coupon on the second note is worth €5 × 0.96 = €4.80.
  2. The last payment accounts for €99.15 − €4.80 = €94.35. Divide by €105 to obtain D₂ ≈ 0.8985714.
  3. The forward growth from year one to two is 0.96/0.8985714 ≈ 1.06836, about 6.84%. Repricing gives €4.80 + €94.35 = €99.15.

Interpretation

The spot rate for two years would be D₂^(−1/2) − 1, which describes a different time interval from the inferred one-year forward.

A common mistake

The two-year rate is applied only to the second year of a two-year spot investment.

How to correct it

For a two-year spot rate, compound across both years from today. For a second-year-only rate, use the implied forward or the explicitly supplied forward contract.

Quick check
If only the two-year spot rate rises, which present-value component falls?

The year-one discount factor is unchanged. A higher two-year rate means a smaller factor for the positive year-two payment.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A one-year zero pays €100 and costs €95. A comparable two-year note pays €4 at year one and €104 at year two and costs €97.40.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Recover the two discount factors and value a separate €50 payment at each year-end.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Find the implied one-year forward rate starting in a year. Does it guarantee the spot rate at that future date?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

D₁ = 0.95. Early coupon is €3.80. D₂ = (€97.40 − €3.80)/€104 = 0.90. The two €50 payments are worth €47.50 + €45 = €92.50.

Part B 3 marks

Forward rate is 0.95/0.90 − 1 = 5.56% approximately. It is the break-even locked rate supported by today’s prices under the assumptions. The future spot rate can differ.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A pension plan owes three fixed payments at known dates. What funding amount today locks in those payments using zero-coupon bonds?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would buy matching zeros by maturity and check every future payment, then sum the executable costs. Here €213,600 funds all three nominal obligations. If rates later move, the market value of the bonds can fluctuate, but the promised maturity cash remains aligned while the issuer pays and the plan holds the positions. Funding only aggregate duration would give a different degree of protection.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If the year-two obligation unexpectedly rises by €10,000 today, how much must the plan add at the quoted prices?

Compare with the follow-up answer

At the stated two-year price of €90 per €100 face, buy 100 more zeros for €9,000. They supply €10,000 at year two. The other two maturity positions need no change. If the increase is learned later, the prevailing two-year-date bond price will determine the then-current cost.
