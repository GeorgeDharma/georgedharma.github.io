Loan amortization

Each instalment combines interest on outstanding principal with repayment. Only the principal portion reduces the borrower’s balance.

The starting point

A fixed loan instalment can stay the same even as its composition changes. Interest is calculated on the balance before the payment. The rest of the instalment repays principal and reduces the amount on which future interest accrues.

A simple repayment schedule starts with the opening balance, adds interest, subtracts the payment, and carries the result into the next period. With a positive rate and equal instalments, interest usually declines and the principal repayment grows as the loan is paid down.

Theory and method

Prospective loan balance and payment recasting

Outstanding principal · Prepayment

A borrower makes an extra payment after the first instalment. How do you calculate the new scheduled instalment if the lender keeps the original maturity?

A loan balance just after an instalment is the value, at the contractual rate, of the payments still owed. An extra principal payment immediately removes part of that balance. If the original maturity remains fixed and the lender agrees to recast, choose a new level instalment whose remaining present value exactly covers the reduced principal. Some contracts keep the payment fixed and shorten the term instead. The borrower needs to know which contractual treatment applies.

How the result is built

New payment = (balance after scheduled payment − extra principal) / aₙ(r), where aₙ(r) = Σₜ₌₁ⁿ (1+r)⁻ᵗ.

Balance and extra principal are euros immediately after the regular instalment. n is the number of remaining periods, r the effective loan rate per period and a the annuity factor for remaining end-period payments.

  1. A loan has two remaining €1,210 yearly payments at 10%. Its balance now is €1,210/1.10 + €1,210/1.10² = €2,100.
  2. The borrower prepays €350 immediately. New principal is €1,750. The annuity factor is 1/1.10 + 1/1.10² ≈ 1.735537.
  3. Recast payment is €1,750/1.735537 ≈ €1,008.33. Across two years the reduction in scheduled payments comes to about €403.33 for €350 paid now.

When the reasoning holds

  • The prepayment is applied entirely to principal with no fee or arrears and is made at the stated valuation date. Other treatment changes the recast base.
  • The lender allows recasting at unchanged interest and maturity. A contract permitting only term reduction requires a new final-payment date calculation.

How to read the notation
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.

All notation examples

First example

A €1,000 loan at 10% annually receives a €550 year-end payment. The first €100 is interest and €450 reduces principal, leaving €550. Interest in the second year is now €55. A final payment of €605 would clear it entirely.

Derive the level instalment

A fully amortizing loan ends with zero outstanding principal after the last scheduled payment. At origination, the amount borrowed equals the discounted value of its promised payments when the contractual rate is used and fees are absent. The relation makes a fixed instalment a direct application of the ordinary annuity formula.

If principal is P, rate per period is r, and n equal payments A fall at period-end, then P = A × [1 − (1+r)^−n]/r. Rearrange to get A = P × r/[1 − (1+r)^−n]. A zero-interest loan simply divides principal evenly across the scheduled payments.

Two ways to find the balance

The retrospective approach starts at origination, accumulates interest, subtracts payments and carries the outstanding balance forward. Immediately after payment k, the prospective approach values the remaining n−k promised payments at the contractual rate. With correct timing, both procedures give identical balances.

This equivalence helps detect errors in loan models. A balance immediately before a payment includes accrued interest and that payment is still due. A balance immediately afterward excludes it. An early settlement amount can differ from either scheduled balance if fees, contractual penalties or interest through a later settlement date apply.

Unequal principal paths

Level instalments differ from equal-principal repayment. With equal principal, each period retires P/n and pays interest on what remains, so total instalments usually decline at a positive rate. An interest-only period retires no principal, and a final balloon can remain even after many interest payments.

Compare affordability as well as lifetime interest. A long maturity can reduce near-term instalments yet keep the balance outstanding longer. Calculate the period’s total charge and do not assume a low first payment implies a low overall cost. Rounding the payment to cents can leave a small last balance that requires a final adjustment.

Method

Construct and verify a schedule

  1. Size the payment

    Match the rate to payment frequency, draw each due date, then derive the annuity instalment or use the given contractual figure.

  2. Carry the balance

    For each period compute interest on opening principal, principal reduction as payment less interest, and closing principal.

  3. Value the remaining payments

    At a chosen payment date, discount the remaining cash flows at the loan rate to verify the scheduled balance independently.

Worked case

Two payouts for a delivery truck

A business borrows €2,100 today, repaying with two equal annual instalments at 10%. There are no fees, and all payments occur at year-end.

Working

  1. The two-period annuity factor is 1/1.1 + 1/1.1² ≈ 1.735537. Dividing €2,100 by this factor gives a payment of €1,210.
  2. Year one interest is €210. Of €1,210 paid, €1,000 retires principal. The closing balance is €1,100, also €1,210/1.10 when valued prospectively.
  3. Year two interest €110 plus the remaining €1,100 principal exactly equals the last €1,210 payment. Total interest is €320.

Interpretation

An equal-principal schedule on the same loan would retire €1,050 principal each year, creating payments of €1,260 and €1,155. Faster first-year reduction saves €5 in total interest.

A common mistake

The full instalment is treated as principal repaid each year.

How to correct it

Allocate its interest portion first. The balance falls by payment less interest, and that reduction should reconcile to the prospective value of the remaining contract.

Quick check
If the borrower instead pays €550 again at the end of year two, how much principal remains?

The second year begins at €550 and adds €55 interest. Subtract the €550 payment from €605 to leave €55 owing.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A two-year loan of €2,200 charges 20% annually and sets two equal end-year instalments. Ignore fees.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Derive the instalment and show the first year’s principal and interest.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Verify the final balance two ways and explain the consequence of paying only €240 in year two.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

The factor is 1/1.2 + 1/1.2² = 55/36. Instalment = €2,200 × 36/55 = €1,440. First interest €440, principal €1,000, close €1,200.

Part B 3 marks

The final €1,440 consists of €240 interest plus €1,200 principal. Its value just after year one is €1,440/1.2 = €1,200. Paying €240 in year two covers interest only and leaves €1,200 owing.
