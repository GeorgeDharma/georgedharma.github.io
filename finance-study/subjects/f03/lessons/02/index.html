Compounding

Interest earns interest when it stays invested. Derive future value by multiplying by one plus the period rate for every period.

The starting point

Money left in an account can earn interest on earlier interest. The second year’s growth is calculated on a different starting amount from the first year’s. That is compounding.

For a constant rate r per period and no withdrawals, multiply by 1 + r once per period. An opening amount P becomes P × (1 + r) raised to the number of periods. If the period rates differ, use their individual growth factors in order.

Theory and method

Time-weighted rate of return

Geometric linking · External cash flows

A customer funds an account immediately before a market drop. How can you report both the investment strategy’s performance and the customer’s euro result accurately?

A manager controls the investments but generally does not control when a client adds cash. Break the holding period at each outside deposit or withdrawal and measure how each euro invested during that subperiod grew. Link those growth factors to describe the investment strategy. In parallel, roll the client’s actual euros through the same path to explain personal gains and losses. A large deposit before a market fall can make these two pictures look very different.

How the result is built

1 + R_TW = ∏ₖ(1+rₖ); rₖ = ending value before next flow / beginning value after previous flow − 1.

R_TW is the whole-period time-weighted return. The product multiplies subperiod factors indexed by k. External flows are deposits or withdrawals by the client. Values are euros measured immediately before and after them.

  1. Start with €100. A 50% gain brings value to €150. A client then deposits €850, making the next subperiod’s starting balance €1,000.
  2. A 20% drop leaves €800. Time-weighted growth is 1.50 × 0.80 = 1.20, or +20% over both subperiods.
  3. The client put in €950 and ends with €800, a €150 euro loss. The strategy gained on early euros but the drop applied to a much larger balance.

When the reasoning holds

  • Account valuations are available at cash-flow boundaries. If cash moves intraday without such a valuation, the exact return may require a time-weighted approximation and a timing assumption.
  • Internal dividends retained in the account are included in ending wealth. Treating them as external client deposits would understate strategy performance.
Terms in this lesson
Principal
The amount borrowed or invested before interest is added.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
How to read the notation
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.

All notation examples

First example

Put €200 in an account earning 5% per year for two years. After one year it holds €210. The second year adds €10.50, taking it to €220.50. Simple interest on the original €200 alone would have added only €10 each year.

From repeated growth to a product

A savings balance consists of the initial deposit plus all interest left inside the account. After the first period the entire new balance earns the second period’s rate. Multiplication captures this reinvestment. Under simple interest, by contrast, each interest payment is calculated on the originally specified principal unless the contract sets a different base.

Write each period as a growth factor 1 + r. This works for losses as well as gains as long as the price-return model remains meaningful. Rates of +20% and −20% have factors 1.20 and 0.80. Their product is 0.96, so an account can lose money even when the arithmetic average return is zero.

A constant equivalent growth rate

If a balance grows from P to F over n equal periods without deposits or withdrawals, a constant rate g satisfying P(1+g)ⁿ = F summarizes its realized path. Solving gives g = (F/P)^(1/n) − 1. This geometric average reproduces the actual endpoints when reinvested for n periods.

An arithmetic average answers the average single-period rate among observations. It is useful for some forecasting questions but generally will not reconstruct a volatile realized path. The gap is economically important in comparing investment performance. A fund that receives external deposits presents an additional issue because those deposits can increase value without being an investment gain.

When money enters halfway through

Cash deposited between return periods earns only the returns after its deposit date. Draw a timeline and compound each contribution for its own number of periods. Do not compound a mid-year deposit for a full two years simply because the report covers two years. The order of returns affects the investor’s ending wealth when contributions or withdrawals occur.

For the return on the investment strategy itself, one can break the timeline at external cash flows and combine the subperiod growth factors, if values are available around each flow. An investor-specific rate can instead reflect the timing and amount of that investor’s funds. Identify which question the performance comparison asks.

Method

Roll a balance through time

  1. Set the sequence

    For each period write opening balance, cash flows before growth, return factor and cash flows after growth.

  2. Multiply and roll

    Apply only that period’s factor, then take the resulting balance to the next date. Keep deposits and gains in separate columns.

  3. Check the summary

    With no external cash, compare the product of factors with ending wealth divided by opening wealth. For a constant equivalent rate take the appropriate root.

Worked case

A deposit after a difficult year

An investor starts with €1,000. The portfolio loses 20% in year one. Immediately after that loss she adds €500. It gains 25% during year two. There are no fees or withdrawals.

Working

  1. After year one the original money is worth €800. The new contribution brings the opening second-year balance to €1,300.
  2. The end balance is €1,300 × 1.25 = €1,625. Aggregate euro gain over €1,500 deposited is €125.
  3. The original €1,000 alone grew by 0.80 × 1.25 = 1.00, a two-year return of zero. The €125 gain is attributable to the second contribution earning 25%.

Interpretation

The portfolio’s compounded percentage performance and this investor’s euro gain answer different questions. Timing supplies the explanation for the difference.

A common mistake

The end balance is divided by the first deposit even though another deposit was made, and the result is called investment performance.

How to correct it

Remove the external cash contribution from each relevant subperiod calculation or reconstruct separate growth paths for each deposit.

Quick check
What happens to €100 after a year gaining 10% and another losing 10%?

€100 × 1.10 × 0.90 = €99. The second percentage applies to €110, so its euro loss exceeds the first gain.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A savings plan starts at €2,000 and earns 10% in year one. Immediately after year one, €400 is withdrawn. The remaining amount earns −5% in year two.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Find the final balance and total euro investment gain net of external flows.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Find the two-year compounded return and constant equivalent annual rate on continuously invested money.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

First-year end €2,200, after withdrawal €1,800, final €1,710. Final balance plus €400 withdrawn less €2,000 deposited gives €110 investment gain.

Part B 4 marks

The factor is 1.10 × 0.95 = 1.045, for 4.5% over two years. The annual equivalent is √1.045 − 1 ≈ 2.225%. The withdrawal explains why this differs from dividing euro gains by initial money alone.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A customer funds an account immediately before a market drop. How can you report both the investment strategy’s performance and the customer’s euro result accurately?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would report the time-weighted performance as plus 20%, and explain that the customer lost €150 on €950 of total deposits. Both refer to the same two market returns. The first gives equal treatment to the strategy’s return path per continuously invested euro. The second recognizes that €1,000, considerably more than the opening €100, was exposed to the loss.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Would reversing the order of +50% and −20% change either result if the same €850 deposit is still added between them?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The time-weighted factor remains 0.80 × 1.50 = 1.20. The customer’s €100 becomes €80, then €850 lifts the balance to €930 and the 50% gain takes it to €1,395. Euro gain becomes €445 on €950 deposited. The client’s cash timing makes the order matter.
