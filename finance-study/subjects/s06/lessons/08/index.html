Receivables and supply-chain finance

Compare selling invoices, borrowing against them and financing a supplier. Identify who bears customer default and who provides cash.

The starting point

An invoice can provide cash before the customer pays. Under factoring the supplier sells a receivable. A loan secured by receivables supplies borrowed cash while the receivables support repayment. The labels alone are insufficient. Read who must absorb a customer’s default and who bears disputes or returns.

In supplier finance, a financier may pay an approved supplier invoice early and collect from the buyer later. Trace cash at each date for the buyer, supplier and financier. A larger working-capital benefit for one party can come from a longer payment wait for another.

Theory and method

Receivables borrowing base and availability reserve

Eligible receivables · Advance rate · Concentration limit

A borrower has €10m of receivables and an 80% advance rate. Why might less than €4m of additional borrowing be available?

A lender advances against invoices it can expect to collect and control. It can exclude disputed or old invoices, impose a concentration cap on one customer and retain a separate reserve. The resulting borrowing base is a limit on total permitted drawings under the case agreement. The borrower has to subtract the amount already drawn to find fresh money it can actually use. High sales or a large balance sheet alone do not create equivalent borrowing headroom.

How the result is built

New availability = max[0, min(C, aE − R) − D]

C is contractual commitment, E eligible invoices after stipulated exclusions and concentration caps, a advance fraction, R separate euro reserve and D current drawings. All monetary balances use the same date.

  1. Of €10m gross receivables, €1m is overdue and €0.5m disputed, with no overlap. One customer has €2.5m of the surviving pool but a fixed €1.5m eligible cap, eliminating another €1m. E is €7.5m.
  2. At 80% advance, the gross base is €6m. Deduct a €0.4m reserve to reach €5.6m. A €7m commitment exceeds that amount, so collateral eligibility binds.
  3. The firm already owes €2.2m. New availability is €5.6m − €2.2m = €3.4m. If permitted borrowings fall below existing drawings, the zero floor masks a deficiency that must be separately reported.

When the reasoning holds

  • The concentration cap is explicitly an absolute amount here. Some agreements define it as a percentage of an eligible pool, requiring the stated calculation sequence.
  • Excluded balances are disjoint in the example. An overdue and disputed invoice must not be deducted twice when assembling the base.
Terms in this lesson
Receivable
Money a customer owes for a sale already made.
Borrowing base
A lending limit calculated from eligible collateral after applying agreed advance rates and deductions.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
First example

A supplier sells a €100,000 invoice for €98,000 cash under a non-recourse agreement covering customer insolvency. It receives funds now and pays a €2,000 price discount. If the invoice proves invalid, a separate contractual obligation may still make the supplier repay the financier.

Three ways to raise cash against invoices

A supplier can sell a receivable to a factor, borrow against invoices, or participate in a buyer-arranged programme once the buyer approves an invoice. Each structure advances cash earlier but creates different claims. Draw who pays cash today, who pays at maturity, and who bears insolvency, late payment or disputes.

In recourse factoring, the seller may have to repay the financier if the buyer fails under stated circumstances. In non-recourse factoring the financier can assume specified credit risks, while the seller can still bear invalid invoices, returns, fraud or contractual disputes. The label alone does not settle every risk or accounting treatment.

How early cash is calculated

A factor may advance a proportion of face, hold a reserve against adjustments, charge a service fee and charge interest on the advance for the actual period. At collection it can release the reserve after fees. A reserve is not automatically a permanent loss. Determine when it becomes cash.

A secured borrowing typically leaves the supplier with the receivable and a separate obligation to the bank. A borrowing base applies agreed eligibility and advance fractions to accepted invoices. Concentrated, disputed or old invoices can be excluded, reducing available liquidity even when reported sales are growing.

Look through a buyer-led arrangement

In supply-chain finance a financier may buy a supplier’s approved invoice and receive repayment from the buyer at an agreed date. The supplier gains earlier money at a discount, often linked to the buyer’s credit standing. The buyer may seek longer terms but must monitor funding concentration and supplier dependence.

Consider what happens if the financier withdraws. Suppliers suddenly paid later may demand shorter terms, creating a cash need for the buyer. Accounting or disclosure classification depends on the terms and applicable standards. Assess the economic cash timings before assigning a label in the accounts.

Method

Trace the funds and risk

  1. Document the legs

    Draw advance, reserve release, collection and final repayment by party and date. Read recourse, eligibility and any obligation triggered by dispute or credit deterioration.

  2. Compute comparable cost

    Calculate fees and interest on their specified bases, then compare with a bank borrowing for the same usable cash and period. Keep any retained default exposure separate.

  3. Test a withdrawal

    Recalculate if some invoices lose eligibility, collections slip or the financier stops buying new invoices. Identify which party must fund the newly delayed cash.

Worked case

A €500,000 invoice pool

A factor advances 80% of eligible invoices immediately and holds the remainder as a reserve. It charges a 1% service fee on face and 6% simple annual interest on the advance for 60 days on a 360-day basis. All €500,000 is eligible and collected. Fees are deducted from the reserve at collection.

Working

  1. Immediate money is €400,000. The €100,000 reserve is initially withheld. Service cost is €5,000 and interest is €400,000 × 6% × 60/360 = €4,000.
  2. At collection the factor releases €91,000, so the supplier receives €491,000 total across the two dates. The €9,000 financing and service cost is different from the €100,000 temporary withholding.
  3. If €100,000 face is ineligible, the initial advance on the remaining €400,000 is €320,000. The supplier must fund the €80,000 shortfall relative to its earlier forecast, despite unchanged invoice face.

Interpretation

For a price comparison ask whether the bank loan provides equivalent collection services or assumes the same credit losses. Comparing only a quoted annual interest rate can miss the main difference.

A common mistake

Calling the full withheld reserve the price of factoring, or assuming non-recourse covers disputed or fictitious sales.

How to correct it

Separate permanent fees from later reserve release. Read exactly which credit event the financier covers and what the seller must repay.

Quick check
A supplier receives €95,000 on a €100,000 invoice and must repay the financier if the customer defaults. Under that clause, who still bears the customer default risk?

Recourse requires the supplier to make the financier whole under the specified default. Receiving cash early changes liquidity even when credit risk ultimately stays with the supplier.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A factor advances 75% on €240,000 invoices. The service fee is 0.8% of face. Interest is 8% annually on the advance for 45 days on a 360-day basis. Collections are complete and fees come out of the reserve.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find the upfront payment, costs and final reserve release.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    If the contract has full recourse for customer insolvency, what benefit and risk remain?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Advance is €180,000. Service fee is €1,920 and interest is €1,800. The €60,000 reserve releases €56,280 at collection, making €236,280 received in total.

Part B 3 marks

The supplier has earlier cash and may receive collection services, but must still reimburse covered default under the recourse clause. Compare costs and liquidity with a secured loan while retaining customer-credit exposure in the downside.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A borrower has €10m of receivables and an 80% advance rate. Why might less than €4m of additional borrowing be available?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would ask for the lender’s borrowing-base certificate rather than multiply total receivables by 80%. After age, dispute and concentration exclusions only €7.5m qualifies. The reserve reduces allowed drawings to €5.6m, and €2.2m is already used. That leaves €3.4m. I would stress large customers becoming ineligible during a liquidity event and check cure deadlines for a deficiency.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Another €2m of previously eligible invoices becomes disputed and the borrower had drawn the former €5.6m maximum. What immediate issue arises?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Eligible collateral falls to €5.5m. At 80% less €0.4m reserve, permissible drawings fall to €4m. The €5.6m existing balance exceeds this by €1.6m. Report a borrowing-base deficiency and check the contract for repayment, substitution or waiver timing.
