Hedging policy

Identify exposure, risk limit, decision owner and permitted instruments. Assess how much cash a hedge protects under a delay or cancellation.

The starting point

Begin a hedge policy with the cash flow at risk. An exporter expecting dollars to pay a euro payroll has a reason to fix a euro conversion rate. Specify which exposures count, who may trade, permitted instruments, dates and what fraction may be covered.

Hedging an uncertain sale in full can create a new exposure if shipment is delayed or cancelled. A forward obliges a future exchange. An option gives its buyer a choice in exchange for a premium. The right mix depends on the certainty of the underlying cash and the protection needed.

Theory and method

Minimum-variance hedge ratio

Optimal hedge ratio · Basis risk

An exporter receives a price linked to one commodity grade but can hedge only with another grade’s futures. How much should it hedge to minimize price variance?

A producer sells a regional grade whose price usually moves with a liquid benchmark, but their changes are not identical. Selling one benchmark futures unit per physical unit can overhedge when the benchmark price is more volatile. The variance-minimizing ratio measures how strongly the physical price moves with that hedge relative to the hedge’s own variation. A statistical optimum can still be unsuitable when volumes are uncertain or the treasury needs a worst-case cash floor.

How the result is built

h* = Cov(ΔS,ΔF)/Var(ΔF) = ρ σₛ/σF

h* is equivalent short benchmark units per physical unit, ΔS and ΔF physical and futures price changes in comparable euro units over the same horizon, Cov their covariance, Var variance, ρ correlation, and σₛ and σF their respective standard deviations.

  1. The hedged change per unit is ΔS − hΔF. Its variance is Var(ΔS) + h²Var(ΔF) − 2hCov(ΔS,ΔF).
  2. Differentiate that variance with respect to h. Setting 2hVar(ΔF) − 2Cov(ΔS,ΔF) to zero yields the ratio. A positive futures variance makes it a minimum.
  3. At physical volatility €6 per unit, futures volatility €7.50 and correlation 0.80, h* = 0.80 × 6/7.50 = 0.64. Hedge 6,400 equivalent units of a 10,000-unit exposure and convert to whole contract sizes.

When the reasoning holds

  • Price changes must use the same unit, delivery period and observation horizon. Mixing percentage changes with price changes without matching notionals distorts the ratio.
  • Historical relationships must remain informative. Physical volume uncertainty, liquidity limits and margin calls are outside this variance-only objective.

Terms in this lesson
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Receivable
Money a customer owes for a sale already made.
Margin
In business accounts, profit expressed relative to sales. In derivatives trading, margin is collateral required to support a position.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Derivative
A contract whose value depends on something else, such as a currency, an interest rate or a share price.
First example

A firm expects US$1m and sells US$700,000 forward at €0.90 per dollar. If the remaining US$300,000 converts at €0.80, total proceeds are €630,000 + €240,000 = €870,000. If no sale occurs the forward still obliges it to deliver US$700,000 under the assumed terms.

Choose the financial exposure before the instrument

A euro exporter with a contractual dollar receivable is effectively long dollars because fewer euros per dollar reduce the cash available for its euro expenses. A dollar payable creates the opposite position. First put receipts and payments due in the same currency and period together to identify any natural offset.

Separate committed contracts from forecasts of probable sales. A forward can fix a rate on a definite amount and date but remains binding if the forecast sale disappears. A purchased option can protect an adverse exchange rate while allowing the buyer to decline exercise, at the cost of its premium.

Hedging protects a specified objective

A firm might want a minimum profit margin on a contract, a cash floor for debt service or less variability in a planning budget. Define which objective matters before selecting a hedge percentage. Maximizing the derivative’s standalone profit is incompatible with evaluating protection over the combined exposure.

A natural hedge may already exist if euro costs are insensitive to dollars but purchases and sales in dollars offset each other. Hedging gross dollar receipts without allowing for dollar supplier bills can reverse the sign of the economic exposure. Consider amount, date, currency and settlement conventions together.

What can still go wrong?

Basis risk means the hedge price or dates do not move exactly with the commercial exposure. Forecast volumes can change, the exchange-rate fixing date can shift, or the company can receive dollars later than the forward’s delivery date. The hedge can be economically sensible while causing interim liquidity needs.

A policy assigns authorized instruments, limits, decision owners and a review procedure for changed exposures. Specify how much of committed and forecast flows may be covered and what happens if an order is cancelled. Avoid increasing an unmatched derivative position merely to prevent a previously reported loss.

Method

Set an auditable hedge

  1. Measure the net risk

    List receipts and payables by currency and date. Identify contracted versus forecast flows and the base-currency objective each could impair.

  2. Map the instrument

    Forwards fix a rate for an obligation. Options offer protection on an agreed amount and date at a premium. Calculate combined cash under at least two spot rates and a volume change.

  3. Monitor mismatches

    Compare remaining commercial exposure with hedge commitments after every cancellation or delay. Assign responsibility for rebalancing, liquidity planning and policy approval.

Worked case

A customer changes its order size

A euro exporter expects US$1.2m receipts and US$0.4m supplier payments on the same date. It sells US$0.6m forward at €0.92 per dollar. Spot at payment is €0.85. Assume all flows settle as described and ignore fees.

Working

  1. Net dollar receipt is US$0.8m. Of this, US$0.6m converts for €0.552m under the forward and US$0.2m converts at spot for €0.170m. Combined net euro receipt is €0.722m.
  2. Without the hedge the net receipt would be €0.680m. Forward protection adds €42,000 in this state. At a stronger dollar it would reduce proceeds relative to an unhedged position while still protecting the chosen amount.
  3. If sales fall to US$0.8m and supplier payments stay at US$0.4m, net receipts are only US$0.4m against the US$0.6m forward. The exporter must source the US$0.2m difference or close the unmatched portion.

Interpretation

The original hedge ratio was 75% of net receipts. After the order reduction it becomes 150%, so a policy based on forecast net exposure needs an updated decision.

A common mistake

Saying a hedge failed because it made a loss when the foreign-currency cash being protected became more valuable.

How to correct it

Calculate combined euro receipts and compare with the stated budget or risk limit. Report any volume or date mismatch separately from the effect the hedge was designed to offset.

Quick check
An exporter’s order falls from US$1m to US$600,000 after it has sold US$800,000 forward. What is the unmatched exposure at settlement?

The forward and the sale are separate contracts. Delivering US$800,000 with a US$600,000 receipt leaves US$200,000 to source or an equivalent forward adjustment.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A company will collect US$900k and pay US$300k on one date. It sells US$450k forward at €0.90 per dollar. Spot is €0.80 at settlement.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Compute the net exposure, hedge percentage and combined euro cash.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Suppose the receipt drops to US$650k, with the supplier bill unchanged. What exposure remains after the forward?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Net exposure is US$600k. The hedge covers 75%. Euro cash is US$450k × €0.90 + US$150k × €0.80 = €525k.

Part B 3 marks

Commercial net is US$350k and the forward requires US$450k, leaving a US$100k dollar shortfall. Reassess or source that amount. The original 75% label no longer describes the position.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

An exporter receives a price linked to one commodity grade but can hedge only with another grade’s futures. How much should it hedge to minimize price variance?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would estimate the covariance of matched-horizon price changes and divide it by benchmark futures variance. Here that gives a 0.64 hedge ratio and retains basis risk even with the optimal hedge. I would translate the answer into contracts, check the stability of regional price relationships and stress cash collateral. If the mandate concerns minimum budget cash rather than variance, I would evaluate that objective separately.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Correlation falls to 0.30 while both price volatilities stay unchanged. What happens to the optimal hedge and remaining risk?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The ratio falls to 0.30 × 6/7.50 = 0.24. Minimum residual variance becomes 36 × (1 − 0.30²) = 32.76. Only 9% of physical variance is eliminated under this linear model, so the old 0.64 hedge would use too much of the poorly aligned benchmark.
