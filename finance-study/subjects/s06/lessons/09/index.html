Liquidity under stress

A derivatives hedge can create margin calls before a shipment produces cash. Combine collateral, credit lines and operating payments on one timeline.

The starting point

A hedge can offset an eventual price loss and still create an urgent cash need. Some derivatives require collateral when their market value moves against the holder. The matching benefit on a future sale or purchase may arrive weeks later and cannot fund today’s margin call.

Build a dated liquidity timeline. Put cash, drawable credit, required collateral, operating payments and the underlying settlement on the dates they actually move. Run shocks that affect several lines at once, such as a shipment delay coinciding with higher collateral requirements.

Theory and method

Margin liquidity and wrong-way funding risk

Variation margin · Collateral eligibility stress

Why can the same market shock increase a derivatives margin call and reduce the company’s ability to fund it?

An exporter hedges future commodity receipts by selling futures. A price increase causes an immediate margin payment while the higher physical sale proceeds arrive later. The exporter may rely on receivable-backed borrowing to finance this timing gap. If the same disruption makes customer invoices disputed or late, the lender can reduce available borrowing exactly when the margin call grows. This adverse dependence between exposure and funding capacity calls for a joint stress rather than two isolated averages.

How the result is built

Gapₛ = max(0, Mₛ + Oₛ + B − C − Lₛ)

In state s, Mₛ is margin due, Oₛ other dated cash obligations, B required free cash buffer, C current accessible cash and Lₛ additional credit genuinely drawable in that state. All are euros due by the same deadline.

  1. In routine conditions free cash is €0.4m, eligible additional line €0.6m, operating payments €0.35m and target cash €0.10m. A €0.15m margin call leaves €0.4m of unused available resources.
  2. In a joint shock variation margin reaches €0.75m while eligible credit falls to €0.35m. Required resources are €1.20m, while usable resources are only €0.75m.
  3. The immediate gap is €0.45m. Holding the original line at €0.6m would have reported only €0.20m short, understating the real stress by €0.25m.

When the reasoning holds

  • The lower limit must reflect facts affecting actual drawability. A committed headline limit can still depend on collateral eligibility, existing draws or timely notices.
  • Future physical-price benefits come into liquidity only when collected or independently financeable by the deadline. A mark on unsold inventory cannot also be counted as free bank cash.
Terms in this lesson
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Derivative
A contract whose value depends on something else, such as a currency, an interest rate or a share price.
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
Margin
In business accounts, profit expressed relative to sales. In derivatives trading, margin is collateral required to support a position.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
Settlement
The final transfer of cash or securities needed to complete a transaction.
First example

A commodity exporter has €1.0m free cash, a €0.3m drawable facility and €0.4m payroll due today. Its hedge requires €1.1m cash collateral. Available liquidity is €1.3m against €1.5m due, leaving a €0.2m shortfall. Higher eventual sale proceeds next month do not fill today’s gap.

An economic offset need not fund a margin call

A commodity exporter can sell futures to stabilize the selling price for inventory. If commodity prices rise, the inventory becomes more valuable and the short futures lose value. The gains and losses can offset economically over the sale horizon, but futures variation margin can require cash well before goods are sold.

A margin call is a demand to provide cash or eligible collateral under a derivatives agreement. It can happen even if the hedger is solvent and the underlying commercial transaction remains valuable. A mark-to-market gain on inventory cannot settle the call until it can be financed or sold.

Build a combined timeline

List unrestricted cash, immediately drawable committed credit, ordinary operating payments, collateral already posted and new margin due on their actual dates. A restricted collateral balance should not also be counted as free cash. A facility can be counted only if available by the margin deadline and acceptable under its conditions.

Then model when margin is returned or when the eventual sale result offsets the derivative position. Avoid counting both daily futures settlements and the full final derivative gain or loss again. Keep a cumulative cash ledger and separate the timing question from the ultimate hedged selling price.

Stress more than one exposure

The same disruption can delay shipment, increase freight costs, change commodity basis and raise margin requirements. Testing one event while assuming all other sources remain available may understate the peak need. Include scenarios where lenders reduce borrowing-base eligibility or a customer postpones payment.

Options can limit certain adverse derivative cash paths for a buyer after paying a premium, but cost, liquidity and contract terms differ. Changing the hedge ratio can also change commercial risk. Compare alternatives against both the price-protection objective and the ability to fund interim cash obligations.

Method

Measure peak liquidity

  1. Compute economic exposure

    Use the hedge direction and notional to determine how an adverse derivative price move changes the underlying and derivative values. Check basis and volume assumptions.

  2. Date each cash movement

    Insert margin, payroll, pledged cash, facilities and sale proceeds on specific days. Compute free cash and remaining borrowing headroom after each movement.

  3. Size support and test recovery

    Find the largest shortfall relative to the operating floor. Show how and when the facility could be repaid, allowing for delayed shipments or additional calls.

Worked case

A grain business before shipment

An exporter expects to sell 10,000 tonnes and shorts matching futures. The futures price rises €30 per tonne before shipment, creating €300,000 immediate variation margin. It has €220,000 cash, €150,000 drawable credit, €90,000 wages due the same day and a €50,000 operating floor. Assume no previously posted amounts affect these figures.

Working

  1. Required liquidity is €300,000 margin + €90,000 wages + €50,000 floor = €440,000. Free cash and credit total €370,000. The additional same-day gap is €70,000.
  2. If cash and futures prices move together, the physical crop might command €300,000 more at shipment. That future benefit does not pay the current margin unless a financier will advance funds against it now.
  3. If the physical selling price rises only €25 per tonne, the crop gains €250,000 while the futures lose €300,000, an economic basis shortfall of €50,000 as well as the earlier liquidity gap.

Interpretation

A €70,000 line could resolve the immediate gap in the first stress but would not guarantee protection against further calls, a delayed shipment or the separate €50,000 economic mismatch.

A common mistake

Declaring that a perfect price hedge eliminates the need for cash before the commercial settlement.

How to correct it

Match values for price risk and match actual settlement dates for liquidity risk. Calculate both even if the final underlying and derivative values nearly cancel.

Quick check
Today a firm has €600,000 cash and €250,000 immediately drawable credit. It owes payroll of €300,000 and a margin call of €700,000. What is today’s funding shortfall?

Available cash and borrowing total €850,000, while same-day obligations total €1m. Counting next month’s expected hedge offset as present cash would hide the €150,000 shortfall.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A metals exporter is short futures on 5,000 tonnes. Futures prices rise €40 per tonne today. Free cash is €160,000, drawable credit €80,000, payroll €60,000 and required cash floor €30,000.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Compute the margin call, total same-day need and funding gap.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    The physical metal rises €35 per tonne and sells next month. Explain both remaining risks.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Margin is €200,000. Need including payroll and floor is €290,000. Available resources are €240,000, leaving a €50,000 same-day gap.

Part B 3 marks

Physical value rises €175,000 against €200,000 derivative loss, leaving €25,000 basis loss. The physical cash arrives next month, so the full same-day €50,000 liquidity gap still requires funding today.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why can the same market shock increase a derivatives margin call and reduce the company’s ability to fund it?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would aggregate cash due and credit actually usable in each scenario. A €0.75m margin call plus operating payments and buffer needs €1.20m, but the same shock leaves only €0.75m of cash and drawable collateral-backed credit. Our deficit is €0.45m. I would secure an independent liquidity source, examine collateral eligibility and confirm when physical cash can actually settle.

Interviewer’s follow-up

An independent unsecured line provides €0.30m by the deadline, while the physical price gain is €0.50m next month. What remains to solve today?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The new line reduces the immediate gap from €0.45m to €0.15m. The €0.50m physical gain due next month cannot fill the balance today without a separate binding financing arrangement. Also test repayment of both lines if shipment or collection moves further out.
