Trade payment terms

Compare advance payment, open account, documentary collection and a letter of credit. Each arrangement assigns financing needs and non-payment risk differently.

The starting point

In advance payment the buyer sends cash before receiving goods. In an open-account sale, the seller ships before payment is due and is effectively lending to the buyer. The choice changes who must finance production or delivery and who risks non-performance by the other side.

In a documentary collection, banks handle specified documents and instructions, but ordinarily do not promise to pay if the buyer refuses. A letter of credit can add a bank undertaking to pay against a complying presentation under its terms. Compare counterparty risk, fee and document requirements for the shipment.

Theory and method

Implicit interest rate in trade credit

Cash discount · 2/10, net 60

A supplier offers 2% off if paid in ten days and otherwise demands the full invoice on day sixty. What annual rate is embedded in waiting?

A buyer owing €100 can pay €98 on day ten or keep that €98 for fifty more days and pay €100 on day sixty. Choosing later payment is equivalent to borrowing €98 for fifty days and paying €2 interest. Comparing the discount percentage with a yearly bank rate would mix periods and use the wrong borrowing base. The same logic helps an exporter decide how much of its selling price it gives up to bring a receivable forward.

How the result is built

Period cost = d/(1 − d). Effective annual cost = [1 + d/(1 − d)]^(y/n) − 1

d is the cash discount expressed as a decimal, n days between early and final payment, and y days in the annualization year. The period cost is interest divided by the discounted amount retained.

  1. On a €500,000 invoice, early payment is €490,000. Waiting fifty days costs €10,000 on the €490,000 retained, or 2.0408% for the period.
  2. Using 365 days gives simple annualized cost of 2.0408% × 365/50 = 14.90%. Compounding repeated equal periods gives about 15.89% effective annually.
  3. If a line is available at 8% simple yearly, financing €490,000 for fifty days costs about €5,370. Taking the discount saves approximately €4,630 before fees or liquidity concerns.

When the reasoning holds

  • Both payment options must be available and the amount must qualify for the discount. Invoice disputes or unavailable bank capacity can change the practical choice.
  • The effective annual figure assumes repeating comparable transactions and compounding. For one decision compare the actual euro saving with actual financing fees over fifty days.
Terms in this lesson
Counterparty
The other party to a financial contract or trade.
Probability of default (PD)
The chance that the borrower meets the specified default definition over the stated horizon.
Expected credit loss
For a simple single-period example, default probability multiplied by exposure and the share lost if default occurs.
First example

An exporter sells €100,000 of goods on a 60-day open account. At 6% annual financing cost on a 360-day basis, carrying the full invoice for 60 days costs €1,000 before credit losses. A smaller margin or late payment can materially change the economics.

Goods, documents and money travel separately

A sale contract decides when the seller ships, when the buyer pays and which evidence triggers release of documents. Under advance payment the buyer supplies cash before delivery and bears risk that the seller fails to perform. Under open account the seller ships first and finances the buyer until payment.

A documentary collection lets banks transmit documents under instructions. Documents against payment requires payment for their release under those terms. Documents against acceptance may release them after a buyer accepts a future payment obligation. In an ordinary collection the collecting bank does not itself promise to cover the buyer’s default.

What a bank undertaking changes

A documentary letter of credit can substitute an issuing bank’s undertaking against a complying presentation for reliance solely on the importer’s willingness to pay. Its effectiveness depends on the issuing bank, applicable country risk, document compliance, maturity and terms. An advising bank is not automatically adding its own payment promise.

These arrangements allocate different risks instead of making them disappear. The obligations concerning goods quality, shipment or insurance under the sale contract remain relevant. Shipment terms allocating transport responsibilities do not by themselves guarantee the customer’s payment or make trade credit risk vanish.

Compare terms by expected net proceeds

A seller can calculate the funding cost of bridging production and customer payment, bank and insurance fees, possible default loss and the value of any price difference. Keep the exposure base consistent. Funding an entire invoice for ninety days is a stipulated teaching approximation. Actual financing could follow an earlier cost outflow.

Expected loss is exposure multiplied by probability of default and the share lost conditional on default, under the simplified assumptions. Risk concentration, timing of recovery and the ability to survive a large loss also matter. A low expected loss is not equivalent to capacity to bear the full downside.

Method

Choose a payment structure

  1. Draw the timeline

    Mark production cost, shipment, document release, acceptance and cash settlement for both buyer and seller. Specify the party providing finance during each gap.

  2. Identify the actual promise

    Separate buyer promises, collecting banks’ document handling, issuing-bank commitments and any protection provided by confirmation or credit insurance under the terms.

  3. Compare net value and risk

    Calculate financing, fees and expected credit losses on comparable amounts and dates. Explain the largest remaining legal, documentation or operational exposure.

Worked case

An exporter negotiates its first delivery

An exporter invoices €400,000. Under ninety-day open account its simple funding rate is 8% on the full invoice, 360-day basis, and expected customer default loss is 2% probability × 60% loss severity. A competing sight credit costs €9,000 in bank fees and is assumed to pay at shipment with no material residual loss for the numerical case.

Working

  1. Open-account funding cost is €400,000 × 8% × 90/360 = €8,000. Expected credit loss is €400,000 × 2% × 60% = €4,800.
  2. Expected net proceeds before production costs are €387,200 under open account, versus €391,000 for the sight credit at the same sale price. The credit leads by €3,800 under these assumptions.
  3. If the buyer pays €5,000 more for open-account terms, deduct the extra funding and loss on the extra invoice value. At €405,000 the expected deductions become €8,100 and €4,860, giving €392,040.

Interpretation

The comparison now favours open account by €1,040 in expected value, while the exporter still bears a much larger loss if the buyer fails. A board can rationally limit that exposure.

A common mistake

Treating documentary collection as the collecting bank’s unconditional guarantee of the foreign customer.

How to correct it

Read the actual undertaking, release conditions and recourse. Operational control of documents does not create a payment promise absent the relevant bank commitment.

Quick check
A seller ships first and lets its buyer pay 45 days later, with no bank payment undertaking. Which arrangement is described?

The seller is extending trade credit directly to the buyer. A future payment date alone does not create a bank guarantee or require documentary collection.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A €200,000 open-account invoice is payable after 60 days. Use 6% simple funding on a 360-day basis, 3% default probability and 50% loss if default occurs. A sight credit costs a flat €4,500 with other numerical risks assumed zero.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Compare simplified expected net proceeds.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Name two assumptions whose failure changes the recommendation.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Funding costs €2,000 and expected loss €3,000, producing €195,000. The sight credit nets €195,500, €500 higher under the specified assumptions.

Part B 3 marks

Document discrepancies or weak issuing-bank credit can delay or reduce sight-credit payment. Better customer recovery or a premium price for open-account terms can improve open-account proceeds. Test cash capacity for an actual default as well.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A supplier offers 2% off if paid in ten days and otherwise demands the full invoice on day sixty. What annual rate is embedded in waiting?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would put the two cash dates side by side. Waiting lets us keep €490,000 for fifty days and costs €10,000, about 14.9% simple annualized. At an 8% simple bank rate, taking the discount saves about €4,630 before other fees. I would confirm that the line is drawable and that using it leaves enough operating capacity for payroll and other commitments.

Interviewer’s follow-up

A new facility needs a €6,000 fixed setup fee solely to fund this early payment. Does early payment still win on the given invoice?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Bank interest of about €5,370 plus €6,000 setup costs totals approximately €11,370, exceeding the €10,000 discount. Waiting saves about €1,370 on this standalone transaction. If the facility serves other commitments, allocate only the incremental cost appropriate to this decision.
