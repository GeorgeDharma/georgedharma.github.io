Inventory models

Dealers facing accumulated positions may change quotes to attract offsetting trades. The risk and cost of warehousing inventory affect spreads.

The starting point

A dealer standing ready to buy and sell may unexpectedly acquire many shares. Until someone buys them back, a price fall reduces the dealer's wealth. Inventory models link this exposure and the cost of carrying stock to the prices the dealer is willing to quote.

A long dealer can lower its bid to discourage additional sellers and lower its offer to attract buyers. This shifts the centre of the quote. A separate increase in uncertainty can also make the dealer widen the distance between bid and offer.

Theory and method

Avellaneda–Stoikov inventory reservation price

Market-maker inventory skew · Exponential utility

Why would two dealers observing the same midpoint quote different centres when one already owns the stock?

A dealer who already owns a share position becomes poorer if the price falls while waiting for an offsetting customer. Another share adds to an existing exposure. Selling one reduces it. Under a simple Brownian price model with exponential utility, that asymmetry shifts the dealer's reservation price away from the public midpoint. More inventory, more volatility or a longer expected holding horizon amplifies the shift. Arrival rates of customer orders and costs are additional inputs to the actual bid and offer.

How the result is built

r = S − qγσ²τ

r and S are the dealer's reservation and current midprice in euros per share, q inventory in shares, γ absolute risk aversion in inverse euros, σ² price variance in squared euros per share squared per time unit, and τ remaining time in matching units.

  1. A position of q shares held over τ has terminal variance q²σ²τ. Under the normal/exponential setup the certainty-equivalent inventory penalty is one half times γ times this variance.
  2. The marginal risk penalty for an extra share at inventory q is qγσ²τ. Subtract it from S for a long dealer and the reservation centre falls.
  3. At S = €100, q = 10, γ = 0.02 per euro and σ²τ = €0.50 squared per share squared, the centre is €99.90.

When the reasoning holds

  • The benchmark formula assumes a locally driftless Brownian midprice and exponential utility over the stated horizon. Jumps or directional information add different effects.
  • q must reflect relevant economic exposure. Opposite inventory in a close hedge can reduce directional risk but may leave basis exposure.
Terms in this lesson
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
How to read the notation
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.

All notation examples

First example

A dealer quotes €49.90 to buy and €50.10 to sell, then accumulates 10,000 shares. Moving both quotes down €0.05 creates €49.85 and €50.05. The spread stays €0.20 while the new prices encourage the inventory to fall.

A dealer's reservation price

An unhedged long position gains when price rises and loses when it falls. A risk-averse dealer already holding a large long book may value one additional share less than an otherwise identical dealer with no inventory. Its personal indifference price is called a reservation price.

Quoting a lower bid can discourage more selling to it, and a lower offer can attract buyers. Both changes move the quote centre downward. That effect differs from widening the spread around the old midpoint to compensate for uncertainty or inventory holding costs.

Time and volatility increase inventory exposure

A simple mean–variance approximation subtracts a penalty proportional to inventory squared times price-change variance from expected wealth. The incremental risk of one extra share grows with the inventory already held, volatility and time expected before the position can be offset.

A dealer able to hedge with a closely related future can reduce market-direction risk. Basis risk remains if the hedge and inventory move differently. Borrowing, funding limits and the cost of crossing another spread also affect how quickly an inventory correction is worthwhile.

What evidence supports an inventory explanation?

Compare changes in quote centre with the dealer's recorded positions, while controlling for market-wide price moves and publicly available information. A lower quote immediately after taking inventory may be inventory management or a rational response to information implicit in the sale.

If the dealer gradually returns toward its old quote as the position unwinds and other venues do not follow, that is consistent with an inventory motive. It is still evidence about a mechanism, not a conclusive identification from one trade.

Method

Reading a quote adjustment

  1. Reconstruct inventory

    Assign a positive sign to shares the dealer buys and a negative sign to those it sells. Include offsetting hedges when measuring risk.

  2. Split location and width

    Calculate midpoint and spread before and after the change to determine whether the centre shifted, the spread widened, or both.

  3. Test the economic story

    Compare direction with existing inventory and observe whether prices on independent venues changed at the same time.

Worked case

Inventory skew with a changing market

A dealer begins flat, buys 6,000 shares from a client, then sells 2,000 to another. Its original quote was €24.90/€25.10. It now quotes €24.82/€25.02 while the broader market's valuation has not moved.

Working

  1. Net inventory is long 4,000 shares. Old and new spreads are €0.20. Midpoint moves from €25.00 to €24.92, a downward eight-cent skew.
  2. A buyer now pays a lower offer while a seller receives a lower bid. Both changes can reduce long inventory if client behaviour responds to prices.
  3. If the dealer sells a hedge equivalent to 3,000 shares, approximate net directional exposure falls to 1,000 shares. A €0.40 market decline then costs about €400 before basis changes and fees, rather than €1,600.

Interpretation

The dealer still owns the cash shares. A hedge changes the economic sensitivity without changing gross inventory or the need to fund it.

A common mistake

Claiming every wider spread proves a dealer has accumulated inventory confuses quote width with quote direction and ignores other information.

How to correct it

Compare both quotes and independent price changes, then check the actual position and timing of hedges.

Quick check
A dealer is already short stock and wants to buy some back. Holding uncertainty fixed, which move is consistent with that inventory objective?

A higher bid attracts sellers and a higher offer discourages additional buyers. Moving the quote centre upward can help the dealer replenish stock.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A dealer short 2,500 shares quotes €39.90/€40.10. It changes to €40.00/€40.24 and buys a hedge with 1,500 equivalent shares.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Find the midpoint and spread changes and assess the direction of the adjustment.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Estimate its residual directional P&L for a €0.50 price increase, assuming a perfect linear hedge.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Midpoint rises from €40.00 to €40.12 and spread increases from €0.20 to €0.24. Raising quotations encourages sellers and discourages additional buying from the dealer, consistent with covering the short.

Part B 2 marks

Net exposure is −2,500 + 1,500 = −1,000 shares. A €0.50 rise produces an approximate €500 loss.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why would two dealers observing the same midpoint quote different centres when one already owns the stock?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

The long dealer values the next share less because it adds to the risk already carried. Here ten shares long produce a ten-cent downward reservation adjustment, whereas a flat dealer stays centred at €100. A lower centre can attract buyers and discourage further client sales. This explains inventory skew. Actual quote width also depends on order arrivals and execution economics.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Remaining time doubles and the dealer sells half its inventory at the same instant. Under this model what happens to the reservation adjustment?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The inventory term depends on q times remaining variance. With unchanged variance per unit time, halving q while doubling τ leaves their product unchanged. The ten-cent adjustment persists, even though the position and the holding horizon changed in opposite directions.
