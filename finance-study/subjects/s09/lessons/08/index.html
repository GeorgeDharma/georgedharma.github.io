Execution scheduling

Faster execution reduces exposure to later price moves but can increase market impact. Model the trade-off and distinguish a decision benchmark from an execution benchmark.

The starting point

Selling everything at once can consume available buying interest and worsen the execution price. Selling gradually spreads that pressure across time, but leaves more shares exposed if the market moves before later slices trade. Execution scheduling weighs expected cost against uncertainty.

Define when the investment decision was made and when the trader received the order. If the market moves in between, the decision and arrival benchmarks differ. A schedule can look effective against the arrival price while the overall investment implementation still loses value.

Theory and method

Almgren–Chriss optimal execution

Mean–variance execution · Temporary impact and timing risk

Why does a more risk-averse seller optimally front-load a large order even when the expected price drift is zero?

An investor who spreads sales across two intervals can avoid consuming too much depth at once. Each share left for the second interval remains exposed to a random market move. In a simplified Almgren–Chriss setting, the first effect makes fast trading expensive, while the second makes slow trading risky. The decision balances expected execution cost and the variance of the outcome. Greater concern about timing risk shifts volume earlier even without a forecast of falling prices.

How the result is built

J(x) = η[x² + (Q−x)²] + λσ²(Q−x)²; x* = Q(η + λσ²)/(2η + λσ²)

Q is total shares, x first-interval shares, η expected temporary impact cost in euros per squared share, σ² one-interval price-change variance in squared euros per share squared, λ the euro inverse risk-aversion weight, and J the euro objective.

  1. The two trades contain x and Q−x shares, so add their quadratic impact costs. Only Q−x shares bear random price movement before interval two.
  2. Differentiate to get 2ηx − 2(η + λσ²)(Q−x) = 0. Solving for the interior minimum gives x*.
  3. For Q = 1,000 and λσ² = η, x* = 2Q/3 ≈ 667 in the first interval. At zero risk weight it is Q/2 = 500.

When the reasoning holds

  • The teaching model assumes two equally liquid intervals, linear temporary price impact per share, independent price change and no drift. Different liquidity or a signal changes the split.
  • There are no interval capacity limits or nonstandard fees, and shares are treated as divisible. Round and re-evaluate feasible whole-share schedules before trading.
Terms in this lesson
Benchmark
A reference chosen in advance for comparison, such as a market index or an equal-weight portfolio.
How to read the notation
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.

All notation examples

First example

A manager decides to buy at €40.00. The order reaches the desk at €40.20 and fills on average at €40.26. For 1,000 shares, €200 of excess cost arose before arrival and €60 after arrival, before fees.

The timing–impact trade-off

Trading an entire order now secures exposure immediately but may use expensive depth and reveal demand. Trading slowly leaves part of the intended position unexecuted and exposed to adverse price moves. A schedule can minimize expected cost plus an explicit penalty for uncertainty about the result.

Expected impact may increase faster than quantity traded per interval. If impact cost is proportional to the square of each interval's volume, dividing a fixed order across identical intervals lowers the sum of those squared costs.

Comparing scheduling rules

An equal-time schedule trades equal lots in each time interval. A volume-weighted schedule aims to trade more when expected market volume is high. A participation rule trades a specified fraction of observed activity, so cumulative completion depends on actual market volume.

Each requires a completion plan. A quiet session may leave a participation order far behind schedule. A market-wide volume benchmark can be inappropriate for a client with a narrow deadline, an information-sensitive order or a mandate to trade only below a limit.

Decision price, arrival price and urgency

A portfolio manager worried about an impending announcement may prefer faster execution even at higher expected impact. An expected information advantage that decays with time is another reason to accelerate. Without such information, faster execution still reduces variance by shrinking shares left untraded.

A late arrival at the desk is distinct from a slow desk execution schedule. Attribute costs to the phase where control existed and examine whether the manager's information or objective changed between phases.

Method

Comparing feasible schedules

  1. Specify exposure over time

    Record shares left open after each interval and the deadline by which the order must complete.

  2. Estimate impact and timing risk

    Compare costs by interval using comparable quantities and prices. Stress adverse moves on shares still outstanding.

  3. Match the investor's objective

    Choose among feasible schedules using the stated urgency, risk tolerance and price constraints. State what changes the choice.

Worked case

A two-period sale

A fund must sell 1,000 shares over two intervals. A simple impact estimate is €0.0002 times the squared number sold in each interval. All resulting costs are euro amounts. The baseline has no expected price drift and ignores fees.

Working

  1. Selling all now costs €0.0002 × 1,000² = €200. Selling 500 in each interval costs €0.0002 × (500² + 500²) = €100, a €100 expected impact saving.
  2. The split leaves 500 shares exposed until the second interval. A €0.30 fall before their sale adds €150 of timing loss. In that path the split has €250 cost against €200 for immediate execution.
  3. An adverse move of €0.20 on the outstanding shares exactly uses the saving. Whether speed is preferable depends on the assumed distribution and the client's tolerance for larger losses, not only this break-even move.

Interpretation

If later liquidity is predictably deeper, the expected impact estimates should differ by interval. Recompute the preferred split under those assumptions.

A common mistake

Judging a schedule by its best realized outcome rewards luck and can encourage unapproved timing bets.

How to correct it

Compare the decision-time distribution of feasible costs and deviations, then review the realized execution against the same mandate.

Quick check
A seller faces a credible risk of a large price drop before noon. Trading immediately would move prices somewhat more than a slow schedule. What evidence best informs the speed choice?

Speed can buy protection against adverse later moves at an immediate execution cost. Estimating both consequences makes the choice consistent with the seller's risk tolerance.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A buyer can complete 2,000 shares now for €160 expected impact or split equally for €70 impact. The second half then faces the later price.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    What adverse price rise on the second half exactly exhausts the impact saving?

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    If the later price rises €0.15, calculate split cost versus immediate and identify a condition that could still justify the split in advance.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

The expected impact saving is €90. Dividing by 1,000 outstanding shares gives €0.09 per share.

Part B 3 marks

Split cost is €70 + €150 = €220 versus €160 immediately. In advance a low probability of such a move, expected deeper later liquidity or higher immediate impact could support splitting within the client's risk tolerance.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why does a more risk-averse seller optimally front-load a large order even when the expected price drift is zero?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

The cost-minimizing seller with no penalty for price uncertainty splits equally because quadratic impact rewards spreading. Risk aversion attaches a cost to the variance on the unsold shares. With the risk coefficient equal to the impact coefficient, the first slice rises from 500 to about 667 shares. The seller is purchasing a more predictable outcome even with zero expected future price change.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If only 600 shares can trade in the first interval when the unconstrained solution is 667, what schedule follows in this two-interval case?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The objective is convex and decreases up to 667 shares, so the feasible minimum occurs at the upper bound of 600 now. The remaining 400 trade in interval two if that interval can accept them. Re-estimate impact if the cap indicates a different liquidity regime.
