Quotes and depth

The bid is the best stated buying price and the offer the best stated selling price. Displayed quantity limits how much trades at either price.

The starting point

The bid is the highest displayed price available to a seller. The offer, also called the ask, is the lowest displayed price available to a buyer. The difference is the quoted spread. Each price comes with a limited displayed quantity, known as depth.

A large instruction can use up the best price and move on to the next. Multiply shares filled at each price by that price, add the amounts, then divide by total shares to find the average fill. A moving order book can change while the trade is being routed.

Theory and method

Roll's effective spread estimator

Bid–ask bounce · Serial covariance of price changes

How could you estimate a spread from trade prices when historical quotes are missing?

An asset's underlying value may stay steady while successive trades occur alternately at the ask and bid. A purchase makes the recorded transaction price relatively high, and a subsequent sale makes the next price change relatively negative. Roll's model links this mechanical bounce to negative covariance between adjacent price changes. It provides an indirect spread estimate when quote records are missing, with strong assumptions about order direction, the underlying value process and the constancy of trading costs.

How the result is built

s = 2√[−Cov(ΔPₜ, ΔPₜ₋₁)]

s is the estimated full spread in euros per share, P a transaction price, Δ its change across consecutive trades, t a trade index, and Cov their lag-one covariance in squared euros per share.

  1. Write Pₜ = Mₜ + (s/2)qₜ, where M is efficient midpoint and q is independently +1 for a buy or −1 for a sell with equal probability.
  2. Consecutive changes share one trade-sign term with opposite signs. Independent price innovations and signs leave covariance equal to −s²/4.
  3. A measured lag-one covariance of −0.0025 in squared euros implies a full spread 2 × √0.0025 = €0.10 per share.

When the reasoning holds

  • The standard derivation assumes independent balanced trade directions and an efficient value with uncorrelated increments independent of the trades. Order splitting can violate it.
  • The spread should be fairly stable across the sample. A positive sample covariance gives no real-valued estimate under the basic expression.
Terms in this lesson
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
First example

An offer shows 100 shares at €20.00 and another 200 at €20.10. An immediate purchase of 250 shares would cost €2,000 + €3,015 = €5,015 if the book stands still. Average fill is €20.06.

Price, quantity and resilience

A quote has at least three properties to record, including its price, the quantity available there and the time it was observed. The smallest permitted price change is the tick size. A one-tick spread can be large in percentage terms for a cheap share and tiny for a costly one.

Depth measures available quantity at one or more price levels. Resilience describes how quickly quotes replenish after a trade. A market can show substantial size at this instant yet take a long time to recover after that size trades.

Comparing spread measures

The midpoint is halfway between the best bid and offer. The quoted spread is offer minus bid, often expressed relative to the midpoint. The effective spread measures how far the actual trade price lay from the pretrade midpoint, typically doubling the signed one-way difference for comparability.

For a buyer, execution above the midpoint is a cost. For a seller, execution below the midpoint is a cost. Price improvement, hidden liquidity and orders large enough to consume several levels can make the effective spread differ from the displayed spread.

When the screen misleads

A displayed book is conditional on the orders remaining available. Other traders can take or cancel them first. A routed instruction may encounter a different depth profile by the time it reaches the venue, particularly during announcements.

For an intended quantity, compute the volume-weighted average execution price by multiplying each fill by its price. Examine whether the next trade would face depleted liquidity and whether the fee schedule changes the ranking between venues.

Method

Reading a two-sided book

  1. Identify the side

    Buys consume available offers from the cheapest upward. Sales consume bids from the highest downward.

  2. Accumulate actual size

    Record the quantity required at each price, stopping when the order is filled or the limit is reached.

  3. Measure the cost

    Compare weighted price with the correct contemporaneous benchmark, including fees separately and stating one-way versus doubled spread.

Worked case

The same top price with different depth

The best bid is €49.90. Offers are 100 shares at €50.00 and 300 at €50.20. Assume the book stays fixed while a 250-share market buy executes. Ignore fees.

Working

  1. Midpoint is (€49.90 + €50.00)/2 = €49.95 and quoted spread is €0.10. At the best offer only 100 shares can execute.
  2. Cash paid is 100 × €50.00 + 150 × €50.20 = €12,530. The average is €50.12. Compared with the starting midpoint, one-way cost is €0.17 per share or €42.50 in total.
  3. Effective spread on the whole execution using the doubled convention is €0.34 per share, more than the displayed €0.10. This follows from consuming deeper prices in the book.

Interpretation

Another venue advertising exactly the same best offer might deliver a lower average fill if it has sufficient depth there. Fees and accessible quantity belong in that comparison.

A common mistake

Multiplying the best offer by the intended number of shares assumes that much liquidity is available at the top of the book.

How to correct it

Walk through stated quantities and record any part left unfilled when liquidity or the price limit runs out.

Quick check
A buyer sees 50 shares offered at €10 and 100 at €10.20. Ignoring fees and other orders, what average price does an immediate purchase of 100 shares get?

The first 50 cost €500 and the next 50 cost €510. The total €1,010 divided by 100 shares gives €10.10.

Independent exam problem

A share has a best offer of €30.10 and bids for 100 at €30.00 and 200 at €29.80. A seller wishes to sell 250 immediately under an unchanged book.

    Find proceeds and the weighted average fill.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Compare the one-way and doubled costs per share with the starting midpoint.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How could you estimate a spread from trade prices when historical quotes are missing?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I could apply Roll's estimator and report its assumptions. A covariance of minus 0.0025 gives about ten cents per share. That is evidence about a typical transaction-cost component, separate from the depth available for a block order. I would check sample size, serially correlated trades and price staleness, and compare any overlapping observed quotes to the inferred spread.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The measured covariance is positive. Should you report a spread of zero?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The basic formula has no real-valued solution for positive covariance. I would flag an unidentified estimate under these assumptions, inspect order-sign persistence and sampling noise, and use quote or alternative transaction information where available. Setting it to zero would be an additional convention.
