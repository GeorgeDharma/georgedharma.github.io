Premiums and bargaining

The unaffected share price, standalone fundamental value and negotiated offer can differ. Explain which reference price measures a market premium and which one supports a reservation price.

The starting point

A newspaper’s offer premium compares an offer with a market price. Analysts often use a price from before takeover rumours moved it. A bidder’s private estimate of the target’s standalone worth can differ because it uses different assumptions about future cash.

Bargaining determines who keeps the combined gain. The seller compares the offer with realistic alternatives. The bidder compares the price with its own ceiling, including gains unique to the combination. A large percentage premium alone cannot reveal which side got the better deal.

Theory and method

Nash bargaining solution with outside options

Reservation price · Generalized Nash bargaining

The seller improved its alternative by €4m while the buyer’s synergies stayed fixed. How should that affect the possible price?

A seller with another binding bid can reject an offer and still receive cash. The buyer might be able to grow independently. Start bargaining from those two alternatives. Any agreement must allocate the additional value available when these particular parties deal. In a transferable-money model, the generalized Nash solution gives each party a stated share of that additional surplus. An improved outside offer increases the seller’s floor while reducing the amount of fresh surplus still available to split.

How the result is built

P = dₛ + w(V − dₛ − dᵦ), if V ≥ dₛ + dᵦ

P is cash paid to the seller, V is combined equity before payment, dₛ and dᵦ are seller and buyer values if talks fail, all in euros. w is the seller’s bargaining weight between zero and one.

  1. If the seller takes P, its gain over disagreement is P − dₛ. Buyer owners retain V − P and therefore gain V − P − dᵦ over their outside option.
  2. These two gains sum to V − dₛ − dᵦ. With linear money payoffs, maximizing the weighted product of those gains allocates fraction w of that surplus to the seller.
  3. With V = €130m, dₛ = €48m, dᵦ = €70m and w = 25%, the extra surplus is €12m. Seller gets €48m + €3m = €51m. Buyer retains €79m, €9m above disagreement.

When the reasoning holds

  • The alternatives must actually be available if talks fail. A rival’s conditional expression of interest is different from a financed offer.
  • The bargaining weight is an explicit modeling assumption. With asymmetric risk aversion or indivisible terms, a fixed percentage of euro surplus need not follow.

Terms in this lesson
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
Synergy
Extra value expected from combining businesses, such as a saving attainable only after a merger.
First example

A company traded at €20 per share before rumours. An offer of €25 is a 25% market premium. If the bidder estimates standalone value at €22 and net benefits at €5 per share, its ceiling is €27 and its estimated gain is €2 per share.

Choose the right starting price

The market premium answers how much the offer exceeds a chosen traded price. A price observed before credible takeover rumours is often useful because trading after the leak may already reflect the expected deal. Record the date used, changes in the broader market and other news affecting comparisons.

Standalone fundamental value answers a different question. It estimates future cash if the company remains independent. It can differ from the historic share price or from what a seller expects to receive by waiting. A premium over market price does not by itself measure the total value created.

Identify each party’s alternative

A reservation price is the worst transaction a party would accept given its next best alternative. A seller may have a competing bid, or may prefer to remain independent. A buyer might build a plant itself. Each outside option narrows the range in which both parties gain.

If a buyer can produce a gain of €9m through acquisition but can earn €4m on an equally feasible mutually exclusive alternative, it can give up at most €5m beyond standalone value before preferring the alternative. Other constraints, including financing or approval, can narrow the feasible range further.

Who captures the surplus?

In a simple cash acquisition, seller gain equals price minus standalone target value. Incremental buyer gain from the combination equals net synergy less that premium. One can also compare each party with its outside option. State which comparison is being made before labelling a surplus or loss.

Negotiating strength relates to credible alternatives and time pressure. If several bidders can generate the same improvement, competition can transfer much of it to the seller. An auction result can reflect optimistic projections too. A winner should examine the possibility that it made the most generous error.

Method

Derive a negotiating interval

  1. Audit the reference

    Calculate the quoted premium from the unaffected market price. Describe any rumours or independent business news that might make that price a poor benchmark.

  2. Set each limit

    Start from comparable equity values. Add feasible net gains to the buyer’s value and subtract the value of its mutually exclusive alternative. Set the seller’s minimum from its best credible alternative.

  3. Allocate and check

    At an illustrative price, calculate each party’s gain against its stated benchmark. The parts must reconcile to the total surplus when a common baseline is used.

Worked case

A competing offer for a software publisher

A publisher traded at €16 per share before rumours, and the bidder estimates €18 standalone. Combining gives €7 net synergy per share. A rival offers €21 with certain execution. The bidder has a separate project worth €2 per comparable share if it walks away.

Working

  1. The seller can require at least €21. The buyer’s maximum versus its alternative is €18 + €7 − €2 = €23. A feasible bargaining range is €21–€23.
  2. At €22, the quoted market premium is (€22 − €16)/€16 = 37.5%. The seller gains €4 against standalone value and €1 against the rival.
  3. The buyer gains €3 relative to doing nothing and €1 relative to its other project. At €22 each party gains €1 over its stated alternative. The implied €2 bargaining surplus fits the width of the interval.

Interpretation

An uncertain rival offer belongs in an expected-value or scenario comparison. Calling an unfinanced indication certain can exaggerate the seller’s outside option.

At the board

Price the timing of a merger’s savings

A target is worth €80m on its own. A buyer offers €87m cash. The buyer spends €3m on integration today. For three years it expects the operating savings and losses below. Tax is 25% on the net operating benefit, paid in the same year. The cash discount rate is 10%. There are no later benefits.

End of yearGross savings less lost contribution, €mAfter-tax incremental cash, €mPresent value, €m
14 − 1 = 32.252.25 / 1.10 = 2.045
28 − 2 = 64.504.50 / 1.10² = 3.719
310 − 2 = 86.006.00 / 1.10³ = 4.508

Operating gains have a present value of about €10.272m. After the immediate €3m integration cost, surplus is €7.272m. The seller takes €7m above standalone value, leaving the buyer €0.272m. If all benefits arrive one year later but the integration cost is still paid today, the buyer’s gain is 10.272 / 1.10 − 3 − 7 = −€0.662m. The deal can keep the same total savings and still destroy buyer value on timing alone.

A common mistake

Using the quoted announcement premium as if it were the seller’s gain over every possible alternative.

How to correct it

Give the market benchmark, economic standalone value and alternative bid their own lines. Only compare amounts measured at the same date and probability of payment.

Quick check
A target traded at €30 before rumours. A cash offer is €36. The bidder values it alone at €32 and expects €3 of net benefits per share. Which pair is correct?

The market premium is (€36 − €30)/€30. The buyer gives up €36 for a claim it values at €32 + €3, a €1 loss per share.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A target traded at €12 and is worth €14 standalone to its bidder. Net synergy is €5 per share. The bidder has a mutually exclusive €2 alternative gain. The seller has an executable €16.50 offer.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate the reasonable bargaining interval and the quoted premium at €17.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Explain who gains at €16.75 over their alternatives.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

The buyer’s ceiling is €14 + €5 − €2 = €17 and the seller’s floor is €16.50. At €17 the market premium is €5/€12 = 41.67%.

Part B 3 marks

The buyer keeps €14 + €5 − €16.75 = €2.25, or €0.25 beyond its €2 alternative. The seller earns €0.25 above €16.50. Their additional gains sum to €0.50.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

The seller improved its alternative by €4m while the buyer’s synergies stayed fixed. How should that affect the possible price?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would change the threat point before changing the synergy estimate. At the original inputs the seller gets €51m. A new €52m outside option leaves €8m to split, so the modeled price becomes €54m. Both sides still benefit relative to their credible alternatives. The €3m price increase reflects bargaining over a smaller surplus; it does not imply the factories suddenly became more productive.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The best seller alternative rises to €63m and the buyer can earn €70m independently. Should the bargaining formula still generate a deal price?

Compare with the follow-up answer

No joint cash payment can satisfy both alternatives because €63m + €70m exceeds €130m. The parties would need at least €3m more combined value just to reach indifference. A mathematical price calculated by allocating a negative surplus would conceal that they can walk away.
