Cross-border execution

Currency, regulatory review, tax structure and ownership approvals affect timing and completion risk. Model delay and failure separately from operating synergy.

The starting point

An overseas acquisition can be affordable in the target’s currency and exceed the buyer’s home-currency budget at closing. Write down the home currency per unit of target currency or its inverse. Then calculate what the agreed local amount costs under several exchange rates.

A forward contract can fix the exchange rate but the acquisition may be delayed or rejected. The bidder could then owe currency it no longer needs. Combine the currency scenarios with approval, financing and termination terms before deciding how much exposure to hedge.

Theory and method

Deal-contingent foreign-exchange exposure

Completion risk · Conditional hedge mismatch

How would you distinguish the FX risk of a cross-border bid before approval from the FX risk after purchasing the business?

A euro buyer promises US$30m only if a regulator approves a purchase. A dollar forward agreed today can survive even when approval fails. The payable and the hedge therefore exist in different states of the world. An exchange-rate move can line up with rejection if both respond to a political shock. Starting with the amount times the headline approval probability misses the difference between a single unavoidable settlement obligation and a contingent commercial payment.

How the result is built

Net home-currency cost = I × A × x + H × (k − x) + F

I equals one if the deal closes and zero if it fails, A is target consideration in dollars, x the spot euros per dollar at decision, H dollars bought forward, k contracted euros per dollar, and F any euro fee or break payment in that state. The hedge is cash-settled or offset at spot.

  1. Translate the contingent purchase at spot, giving I × A × x. Model the forward as buying H dollars at k and selling or marking them at x, a euro cost H × (k − x).
  2. At closing with A = US$30m, H = US$20m and k = €0.90, the buyer pays €18m on the forward plus spot value of the unhedged US$10m.
  3. Upon failure there is no acquisition bill. Set I to zero, keep the forward and add any applicable break fee. Use the joint deal and currency states for budgets and stress testing.

When the reasoning holds

  • The amounts and maturities match the stated decision date. A regulatory delay beyond forward maturity introduces funding and roll risk.
  • The stated hedge belongs to the bidder and remains binding when the purchase fails. A genuinely deal-contingent product may instead charge a different premium for cancellation protection.
Terms in this lesson
Synergy
Extra value expected from combining businesses, such as a saving attainable only after a merger.
Forward
A contract to exchange an agreed amount at a set price on a future date.
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
Revenue
Amount earned by selling goods or services. The customer may pay later.
Counterparty
The other party to a financial contract or trade.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
First example

A French bidder promises 180bn rupiah. At 18,000 rupiah per euro this costs €10m. At 16,000 the same promise costs €11.25m. If approval fails after it commits to buy all the rupiah forward, closing the unused hedge can produce a gain or loss.

The offer and the funding use different currencies

A target’s owners can demand a fixed amount in their currency while the bidder manages a home-currency cash budget. Specify the quotation convention before converting. At 20 local units per euro, divide the amount of local currency by 20 to get euros. Strengthening of the local currency makes the purchase more expensive in euros.

Price exposure between signing and closing is different from the operating currency exposure after an acquisition. A hedge against the purchase price may protect a funding limit while doing nothing to protect future foreign revenues. Evaluate the two separately so that cash needed at close remains visible.

Treat completion and delay as different states

Regulatory approval, counterparty financing or contract conditions can determine whether and when the purchase completes. Under a delay, the asset may still arrive while a forward contract matures earlier. Under failure the company needs no target currency but may still owe a settlement on the hedge.

A binding forward fixes an exchange rate. A purchased currency option can give the bidder the choice to exchange while charging a premium. The right response depends on likelihood of completion, size of the exposure, funding flexibility and the value of certainty. A forward’s zero initial premium is not zero economic risk.

Keep tax and regulation tied to the case

Group cash cannot always be transferred freely. A locally earned cash stream and cash accessible to the buyer may differ after stipulated withholding or repatriation restrictions. Record the date, currency, tax base and who bears the tax before deducting it. Real transactions need the applicable local rules.

Transactions can face approval conditions that weaken the very source of synergy. A requirement to sell part of the combined operation might reduce the benefit even when the deal closes. Model a conditional close with modified economics separately from outright failure.

Method

Construct a state table at signing

  1. Translate the bill

    Fix the target-currency amount and the bidder’s base currency. Convert at each relevant spot or contracted rate. Match the intended closing date to the hedge maturity.

  2. Price completion states

    Describe on-time close, delayed close and failure. For each, list operating value actually received, cash paid and the effect of closing or unwinding any remaining hedge.

  3. Test authority

    Compare cash needed and worst-case hedge settlements with committed funding and internal limits. Identify whether a condition or earlier decision can reduce any mismatch.

Worked case

An option against an overseas acquisition

A euro bidder owes 90m local units only if approval arrives. It buys an option for €0.20m giving the right to buy all 90m at 15 units per euro on the closing date. If the deal closes, possible spot rates are 12 or 18 units per euro.

Working

  1. At 12 the unhedged bill is €7.50m. Exercise the option for €6m and add the €0.20m premium, giving €6.20m total cash cost.
  2. At 18 the spot bill is €5m. Let the option expire and buy spot. Total cost is €5.20m. The premium bought a ceiling while retaining favourable currency movements.
  3. If approval fails and no currency is needed, letting the option expire costs at most the premium in this simplified scenario. An in-the-money option could be worth cash if transferable or settleable.

Interpretation

The maximum cost assumes the exposure amount and date match the option. Delayed approvals can put the ultimate close outside the period actually protected.

A common mistake

Treating a fall in foreign operating value and a rise in the euro cost of the fixed purchase price as the same risk.

How to correct it

List asset value, promised price and hedge cash flows separately in each state. Only then translate them consistently to the bidder’s currency.

Quick check
A euro bidder has promised exactly US$24m. At signing one euro buys US$1.20. At closing it buys US$1.00. What happens to the unhedged euro purchase price?

Divide dollars owed by dollars per euro. Each euro now buys fewer dollars, so the bidder needs more euros even though the target receives the same dollars.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A bidder promises 120m foreign units and forward-buys 60m at 20 units per euro. The remaining 60m will be bought at spot. It closes with spot at 15 units per euro.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate the hedged and unhedged euro outlays at closing.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    If approval fails at the same rate, what exposure remains?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

The hedged amount costs €3m and the other 60m cost €4m, for €7m total. Buying all at spot would cost €8m, so the partial hedge saves €1m before fees.

Part B 3 marks

The bidder still contracts to buy 60m for €3m. Selling those units at spot would produce €4m, for a €1m benefit here before fees. A weaker foreign currency could instead create a loss even without an acquisition.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How would you distinguish the FX risk of a cross-border bid before approval from the FX risk after purchasing the business?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would construct separate rows for a close, a failure and a delayed close. The forward stabilizes spending on US$20m of a successful US$30m deal, but after failure it becomes an independent exposure. At €0.80 per dollar the unmatched forward costs €2m. After closing the business brings its own currency revenues, wages and suppliers. I would measure those cash flows afresh before extending the purchase hedge.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The contract charges €1m on failure, and the dollar instead strengthens to €1.00. What cash outcome does a failed bid produce?

Compare with the follow-up answer

There is no purchase payment. The US$20m forward gains €2m because the buyer committed at €0.90. After the €1m failure fee, the combined cash cost is negative €1m, a €1m cash gain. Successful speculation on that state would not establish that the original hedge size was appropriate.
