Empirical merger research

Estimate abnormal returns around an announcement and combine gains using pre-event equity values. Discuss anticipation, competing news and selection of completed deals.

The starting point

An event study asks how prices changed when news arrived relative to an estimated ordinary return. Subtract the return predicted by a market or factor model from the actual return. The result is an abnormal return under that model. It measures the market’s immediate reassessment.

Translate each firm’s abnormal return into money using its equity value just before the news. Add those amounts to measure the combined change. Interpret carefully if rumours preceded the date, other news arrived at the same time or the sample excludes announced deals that later failed.

Theory and method

Market-model event study and cumulative abnormal-return variance

Abnormal return · Event-window standardization

A target’s three-day abnormal return is positive. What must you know before describing the reaction as statistically unusual?

A target normally moves with the broader market, and it also has company-specific daily volatility. An event study first removes the predictable market component, then asks whether the remaining event-window movement is large relative to ordinary unexplained movements. Three small positive surprises can add up, but their dependence matters. If sector news moves the company for several consecutive days, simply treating each day's residual as independent can give a misleadingly narrow uncertainty range.

How the result is built

ARₜ = Rₜ − (α + βRₘ,ₜ), CAR = Σₜ ARₜ, SE(CAR) = σ√L under independent daily residuals

Rₜ is the share’s daily return, Rₘ,ₜ the market return that day, α the pre-event intercept and β the market sensitivity. AR and CAR are abnormal and cumulative abnormal returns. σ is daily residual standard deviation, L the number of event days and SE the standard error under the stated simplifications.

  1. Estimate the normal relation during a clean pre-event period. Use the event date’s market return to predict ordinary return and subtract it from the observed share return.
  2. Add the short-window abnormal returns. With residual standard deviation of 1% daily and three independent event sessions, the variance of the sum is three times the daily variance.
  3. The standard error is 1% × √3 = 1.732%. A CAR of 4.5% is about 2.60 such standard errors from zero in this simplified calculation. This alone does not attribute all of the movement to the merger.

When the reasoning holds

  • The calculation assumes stable residual variance, negligible coefficient-estimation uncertainty and independent daily residuals. Serial correlation or sharp event-induced volatility changes the standard error.
  • The information timing and normal-return model must be credible. An earnings warning released simultaneously can affect the CAR even if its statistical precision is high.

Terms in this lesson
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Benchmark
A reference chosen in advance for comparison, such as a market index or an equal-weight portfolio.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
How to read the notation
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.

All notation examples

First example

A €500m bidder has a −2% abnormal return, losing €10m. A €100m target has a +15% abnormal return, gaining €15m. Together they gain €5m, or about 0.83% of €600m. A simple average would give the smaller target far too much weight.

Measure the surprise in a share price

Prices reflect information investors already expect. An event study asks whether a share’s return around new information differed from a reasonable estimate of its normal return. With a simple market model, expected return is an intercept plus sensitivity to the market times the market return, estimated before the event.

An abnormal return is actual return minus that benchmark. A cumulative abnormal return adds abnormal returns across a short event window. Define trading dates in each market and the timing of the announcement. News published after an exchange closes may be reflected in its next trading session.

Weight gains by the money exposed

A 10% target increase and a 2% bidder loss cannot be compared until their original equity values are known. Multiply each abnormal return by its own pre-event value, add those amounts and divide by their combined initial equity if a combined percentage is needed.

The combined reaction is an estimate of investors’ revisions to shareholder value around the announcement. It can include valuation news about the bidder, changing completion expectations or a revelation that the target had other interested buyers. It cannot establish realized operating cash flows several years later.

Design choices that can reverse a conclusion

A short window reduces unrelated news but may miss rumours or delayed trading. A longer window catches more deal-related anticipation and more unrelated earnings or policy shocks. Prepare a timeline and inspect alternative reasonable windows. Select the primary specification before seeing which produces the largest result.

A sample limited to deals that eventually close describes a selected population. Abandoned deals also appeared in investors’ information at announcement. A historical average with statistical uncertainty does not tell what every firm gained or whether one country’s takeover institutions generalize elsewhere.

Method

Construct an announcement-return bridge

  1. Set the clock

    Identify the first public release, any earlier credible leak and local exchange trading hours. Specify the estimation period, event window and any potentially confounding disclosure.

  2. Calculate the counterfactual

    Use the given market or factor model to estimate normal returns for each share. Subtract from observed returns, retain signs and aggregate the stated window.

  3. Interpret the money

    Multiply by each issuer’s initial equity value. Compare total and split gains, then state at least one alternative explanation and a way to investigate it.

Worked case

A bidder announces late in the day

A €900m bidder rises 0.5% and its €150m target rises 11% in the same measured trading window. Their estimated normal returns in that window are +1% and +2%. Assume no change in shares and correctly aligned market hours.

Working

  1. The bidder’s abnormal return is −0.5%, worth −€4.5m. The target’s +9% abnormal return is worth +€13.5m.
  2. Combined abnormal change is +€9m, about +0.857% of €1,050m initial combined equity. The target gained more than the aggregate gain because bidder owners lost value.
  3. The bidder also published a profit warning that day. Some or all of its negative reaction may belong to that news. Timing the two releases and examining uncontaminated comparable events could narrow the inference.

Interpretation

An event study estimates a reaction even when clean attribution fails. The calculation can be correct while the causal statement needs to remain limited.

A common mistake

Averaging the two abnormal percentages when the question asks how many euros changed hands across both equities.

How to correct it

Weight each return by its own pre-news equity. Use pre-event weights to avoid weighting a winner more heavily because its price already rose.

Quick check
A bidder worth €800m loses 1% relative to its benchmark. A target worth €100m gains 6% relative to its benchmark. What is their combined abnormal change in euros?

The bidder loses €8m and the target gains €6m. A large target percentage gain need not outweigh a small percentage decline for a much larger bidder.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A €600m bidder returns −1% against an expected +1%. An €80m target returns +20% against an expected +2%. A takeover rumour circulated three trading days before the primary window.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Compute the combined abnormal euro gain and percentage.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    How could the rumour affect the interpretation, and what would you test?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Bidder change is −2% × €600m = −€12m. Target change is +18% × €80m = €14.4m. Combined gain is €2.4m, or approximately 0.35% of €680m.

Part B 3 marks

Expectation may already have entered prices before the formal announcement, understating the total news reaction in the primary window. Report a pre-specified wider window around the rumour, check competing news and compare signs and money gains.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A target’s three-day abnormal return is positive. What must you know before describing the reaction as statistically unusual?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would describe the benchmark, window and historical residual volatility before using the word unusual. Here the model produces a 4.5% three-day CAR against a 1.73% standard error, roughly 2.6 standard errors. I would then verify timing, inspect concurrent news and test a sensible alternative benchmark. Precision about an announcement reaction and credible identification of a takeover effect are separate parts of the argument.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if every pair of daily residuals has correlation 0.5 while their daily standard deviation remains 1%?

Compare with the follow-up answer

There are three daily variances and three distinct covariance pairs counted twice. Variance of the sum becomes 3 × (1%)² + 2 × 3 × 0.5 × (1%)² = 6 × (1%)². Standard error rises to about 2.45% and the standardized 4.5% reaction falls to about 1.84.
