The market for corporate control

Study horizontal, vertical and diversifying acquisitions. Ownership changes can improve control of assets, but the existence of a bidder does not establish an efficiency gain.

The starting point

Imagine two bakeries serving the same city. If one bakery buys the other, the acquisition is horizontal. The buyer could share ovens and delivery routes. Buying the flour mill would be vertical instead. It might secure a reliable supply or reduce the cost of negotiating deliveries.

Control means being able to change how people and assets are managed. A deal can transfer that power without making the combined businesses more productive. Compare the expected cash from the businesses together with what the same assets could earn separately.

Theory and method

Grossman–Hart free-rider problem in takeovers

Market for corporate control · Tender offer

Why might a shareholder reject a €13 tender for a €10 share even though the bidder would improve the company?

Imagine a company owned by thousands of small shareholders. A successful change of control would lift each share from €10 to €15. A shareholder with a tiny holding expects the same outcome whether she tenders or holds. If the bid succeeds, keeping the share yields €15. If it fails, her share stays worth €10. A tender conditional on success gives her an incentive to wait for others to sell. This can prevent a valuable change of control.

How the result is built

Conditional tender threshold P ≥ V₁. Bidder gain on acquired shares = n × (V₁ − P) − C

P is the cash offer per tendered share, V₁ the post-improvement value per share, n the number acquired, and C the bidder’s fixed transaction cost in euros.

  1. Write the success-state alternatives. A non-pivotal investor receives P by tendering and can hold a share worth V₁ if other holders accept.
  2. Under a conditional bid, failure returns the investor to the original position whether she tendered or held. The success-state comparison therefore gives the tender threshold.
  3. At P = V₁ a bidder with no existing holding earns zero gross trading gain on shares purchased. Any positive investigation cost makes initiation uneconomic under these assumptions.

When the reasoning holds

  • Each holder treats her decision as unable to change the outcome. A large pivotal owner may compare successful sale with a failed bid instead.
  • Holdouts retain the entire improved share value. A pre-existing bidder stake, private control benefits or a valid mechanism buying out remaining shares can change incentives.

Terms in this lesson
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
First example

A bakery pays €12m for a mill worth €11m on its own. Reliable deliveries add €2m of present value after all costs. The businesses create €2m together. The seller receives €1m above standalone value, leaving €1m for the buyer.

Who controls an asset?

A contract can buy a service without buying the company that supplies it. Owning the supplier gives the buyer authority over decisions the contract did not anticipate. A roaster and a coffee grower, for example, can agree on a price today without agreeing exactly how to respond to a poor harvest next year.

The value of ownership depends on what that additional authority makes possible. One owner can coordinate an investment both parties previously feared the other might exploit. It can also weaken incentives if the grower now bears less responsibility for quality. Examine both the proposed improvement and what independent ownership did well.

Why a takeover market exists

If shares can be bought and control transferred, an outsider can profit by replacing weak management or redeploying idle assets. The threat of replacement can itself influence managers. Yet control is expensive to acquire. Costs of identifying a change, paying advisers and carrying it out reduce the prize.

Horizontal deals combine rivals, vertical deals connect stages of production, and diversifying deals join different businesses. These are descriptions of the relationship, not predictions of gain. Two rivals might consolidate empty production lines or gain bargaining power to raise prices. These produce very different outcomes for customers.

How much of the change benefits society?

A cost saving releases resources that can serve other uses. A rise in profit paid for entirely by customers through higher prices can transfer wealth even if the same quantities of labour, materials and capital are used. Positive shareholder gains alone cannot settle whether customers, suppliers or employees benefited from the transaction.

Spell out the comparison. The relevant alternative may be a long-term supply contract or a joint venture, rather than business as usual. If a contract can produce the same coordination at lower cost, willingness to pay for full control should reflect the smaller incremental advantage.

Method

Compare three ownership arrangements

  1. Locate authority

    Name the contested decision and the party controlling it before and after the transaction. Identify who bears the cost if that decision goes wrong.

  2. Price the alternative

    Estimate achievable cash flows under independent trading, a negotiated contract and ownership. Use one date, currency and risk basis. Include costs of drafting, monitoring or integrating.

  3. Separate the claims

    Compare the operating surplus before payment to the seller. Then divide that surplus between buyer and seller using the actual offer, keeping customer effects visible.

Worked case

The glass supply decision

A drinks company values an independent supplier at €30m. Relative to today's arrangement, a delivery contract gives the drinks company €3m of present value net of contracting costs. Ownership produces €8m of additional operating value before €2m integration cost. The seller asks €34m.

Working

  1. Ownership adds €8m − €2m = €6m across both firms. The €34m offer gives the seller a €4m premium over standalone value.
  2. The buyer keeps €6m − €4m = €2m if it buys. The contract would give it €3m without buying shares.
  3. The contract is €1m better for this buyer. At a €33m offer the buyer keeps €3m from acquisition and is indifferent on these estimates.

Interpretation

The €6m combination surplus does not alone justify buying. The buyer can capture the contract benefit without sharing a takeover premium, assuming the contract is acceptable to the supplier.

A common mistake

Treating any positive synergy as sufficient evidence to acquire.

How to correct it

Compare the buyer’s residual gain at the negotiated price with its best feasible alternative. Identify a practical reason the alternative might fail.

Quick check
A furniture maker buys its only timber supplier to coordinate deliveries. Which description best fits?

Follow the production chain. Timber is an input into furniture, so the deal connects successive stages. Whether the operational benefit covers the price is a separate calculation.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A rail operator can improve a terminal’s scheduling through a contract worth €2m to the operator. It values the terminal at €18m standalone. Acquiring control yields €5m net synergy and the seller asks €22m.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Compare the buyer’s acquisition gain with its contract gain.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Find an acquisition price that makes the operator indifferent and identify one fact worth testing.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Ownership gives €18m + €5m − €22m = €1m. The contract gives €2m. The operator prefers the contract by €1m if both are executable.

Part B 3 marks

The ceiling relative to the contract is €18m + €5m − €2m = €21m. Test whether the scheduling changes can be specified and enforced when train volumes unexpectedly change.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why might a shareholder reject a €13 tender for a €10 share even though the bidder would improve the company?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would look at whether my share is pivotal. At €13, a small owner expecting the takeover to succeed can keep a €15 claim. With a conditional bid, a failed tender returns the original share anyway. If all owners use that reasoning, the bidder may need to offer almost the full improved value and cannot cover its costs. This is the Grossman–Hart free-rider problem.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The bidder already owns 20,000 shares and spends €60,000 investigating. Can tendering the remainder at €15 now be worthwhile?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Its old stake gains 20,000 × (€15 − €10) = €100,000 on success. The tendered shares contribute zero trading gain at €15. The €40,000 residual after investigation supports a bid if success is certain in this calculation. With uncertain completion, compare expected gains with the unavoidable upfront cost.
