Financial due diligence

Challenge earnings quality, net debt and normal working capital. Identify which conclusion changes price, which changes contractual protection and which makes the buyer walk away.

The starting point

Due diligence tests the facts behind a bid. Earnings can rise because a repeatable operation improved, or because a customer settled an old claim once. Trace an unusual amount through contracts, invoices and bank records before treating it as future income.

The remedy depends on what the evidence changes. An enduring reduction in earnings can lower value. A past obligation might require a price adjustment or specific contractual protection. If the missing information calls the business itself into question, a lower price might not solve it.

Theory and method

Expected value of a capped indemnity

Deductible basket · Seller credit risk

How should a bidder value a contractual indemnity when the seller promising reimbursement might default at the same time as the claim arises?

A buyer discovers an unresolved environmental complaint. Repricing the whole company assumes an expected loss. An indemnity instead creates a separate reimbursement claim against the seller. Its protection depends on the loss threshold, maximum reimbursement, eligibility and seller credit precisely when money is due. A promise from a seller whose own cash deteriorates in the bad state can be worth far less than its printed cap. First model each state’s gross loss and cash actually recovered.

How the result is built

Net loss in state s = Lₛ − cₛ min[K, max(0, Lₛ − D)]

Lₛ is the buyer’s gross qualifying euro loss in state s, D is the true deductible borne by the buyer, K the seller reimbursement cap, and cₛ the expected fraction collected in that state. min takes the smaller and max the larger value.

  1. Deduct D from the qualifying loss and floor the result at zero. This is a true deductible. A tipping basket with reimbursement from the first euro after a threshold has different terms.
  2. Cap the eligible reimbursement at K. Multiply by state-specific collectibility and subtract the resulting recovery from the gross loss.
  3. Weight each net exposure by its probability and discount on the actual payment dates. Check the pricing bridge so the same claim has not been fully deducted already.

When the reasoning holds

  • The clause covers the particular historic loss and has not expired. An ordinary future customer departure may fall completely outside its scope.
  • Collection risk can depend on loss severity. Multiplying an average claim by an independent average collection rate can overstate effective protection.
Terms in this lesson
Financial due diligence
Checking a business’s earnings, cash needs and debt before a transaction.
Working capital
Money tied up in day-to-day operations. For a deal, agree which receivables, stock and payables count.
Settlement
The final transfer of cash or securities needed to complete a transaction.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
First example

A price of eight times recurring operating earnings assumes €5m of earnings. A verified €1m settlement is included this year and will never recur. Using the same multiple, the operating value falls from €40m to €32m. Any cash retained at closing is assessed separately under the deal terms.

Follow a deal assumption back to evidence

A diligence team does more than collect statements. It tests assertions that make a bid work. A forecast may assume a customer will renew for three years. A contract can establish cancellation rights. Payment records can establish past behaviour. Neither alone proves that future demand will continue.

Classify a finding by its economic effect. Recurring operating weakness reduces projected earnings. An existing unpaid obligation may reduce the payment for shares at closing. A contingent dispute may call for a defined contractual remedy, scenario valuation or withdrawal. Different categories require different evidence and arithmetic.

Build the price bridge

Operating value gives the price of the business before allocating claims between lenders and shareholders. In a simple deal, equity proceeds equal operating value plus eligible cash less agreed net debt items, plus or minus working-capital departures from an agreed normal balance. The signed definitions govern the calculation.

Keep a single issue register to prevent double counting. A pre-closing claim could already have reduced both current profit and the value forecast. Reviewing historic profit, assessing future profitability and counting a legal obligation are distinct questions. State which cash effect each adjustment represents.

Decide whether a contract actually protects the buyer

An indemnity can assign the cost of a specified event to the seller if it occurs. Its economic value depends on scope, limits, time limits and whether the seller can still pay. It does not cure a permanently declining customer base. Evidence of lost repeat business generally requires a forecast revision.

The most important finding need not be the largest booked figure. A missing operating licence or unreliable sales records may make valuation impossible over a wide range. Compare the price change that credible scenarios imply with the time and cost needed to resolve the issue.

Method

Translate evidence into an action

  1. Write the assertion

    Identify the exact price, forecast or contractual assumption being tested. Request evidence aligned with that assertion and the period that matters.

  2. Classify the effect

    Separate sustainable profit, cash required at closing and genuinely uncertain future exposure. Prepare a trace showing where the same item already entered other calculations.

  3. Select a response

    Estimate an amount or range, state the uncertainty and choose a repricing, defined contractual protection, additional evidence or withdrawal. Make clear what would reverse the recommendation.

Worked case

A logistics company’s three findings

The seller proposes 6 times €10m of recurring operating earnings. Diligence verifies €1m of profit came from a one-off reimbursement. A €2m unpaid loan is agreed debt-like. Closing working capital is €4m against a €5m target. There is no eligible cash or other debt.

Working

  1. Sustainable earnings are €9m and operating value is €54m at the same multiple. The €6m reduction reflects lower expected recurring earning power.
  2. Deduct the €2m loan and €1m working-capital shortfall to obtain €51m equity proceeds. Check that the loan is outside both measured working capital and its target.
  3. A threatened future cancellation by a large customer is still unquantified. Review renewal terms and test a downside margin forecast before assuming €51m remains a safe offer.

Interpretation

The one-time reimbursement may have produced real historical cash even though it is absent from future recurring income. Any cash actually delivered belongs in the separate closing calculation.

A common mistake

Subtracting the full lost revenue of a threatened contract from the share price as soon as a customer expresses concern.

How to correct it

Estimate probability, duration and lost contribution after avoidable costs. Translate that cash effect into value before deciding whether to adjust the price or request more evidence.

Quick check
The buyer discovers that the target’s main customer may leave next year. Which first step best tests the recurring earnings forecast?

A possible future loss changes a forecast. The contract and customer evidence help establish when it could happen and how much margin would disappear.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A buyer values a company at 7 times €6m recurring operating earnings. A €0.4m insurance gain is one-off. At close eligible cash is €1m, debt €4m and working capital is €0.5m above target.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Derive the revised equity proceeds.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    A separate unresolved tax claim could cost €0–€2m. Explain a defensible next decision.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Underlying earnings are €5.6m, giving €39.2m operating value. Equity is €39.2m + €1m − €4m + €0.5m = €36.7m under the stated definitions.

Part B 3 marks

Verify assessments, correspondence and legal analysis. Model both outcomes and compare repricing with a defined, collectible indemnity. Keep it separate from net debt unless the terms expressly classify the same exposure there.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How should a bidder value a contractual indemnity when the seller promising reimbursement might default at the same time as the claim arises?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would start with the loss the contract actually covers, apply the deductible and cap, and then ask if the seller can pay in that specific scenario. For the €8m severe loss only €4m qualifies after the cap, and at 50% collectibility the buyer recovers €2m. The buyer still bears €6m in that state. I would compare this residual with a price reduction or a funded escrow.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If a reliably funded escrow ensures collection of the first €4m of qualifying reimbursement, what improvement does it produce in this example?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The moderate and no-claim states stay the same. Severe-state reimbursement rises from €2m to €4m and the buyer’s loss there falls to €4m. At a 10% severe-state probability, expected loss declines by €0.2m to €0.7m. Compare that protection with the cost of tying up escrow cash.
