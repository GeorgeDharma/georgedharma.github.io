Cash, shares and exchange ratios

A cash offer fixes nominal consideration. A share offer gives sellers a stake in the combined firm, so the exchange ratio distributes future gains and losses.

The starting point

Cash fixes what the seller receives if the deal closes on those terms. In a share offer the seller receives units of ownership. A fixed exchange ratio specifies the number of buyer shares delivered for each target share, so their euro value moves with the share price.

To see who owns the future gains, divide the newly issued shares by the total after closing. Each former group then owns its fraction of the combined value. A promised headline price calculated on the announcement date can differ from the final value.

Theory and method

Exchange-ratio bargaining bounds

Stock-for-stock merger · Post-merger ownership

How can you determine which fixed share-exchange ratios leave both original shareholder groups at least as well off?

Two businesses can negotiate a share ratio without settling a fixed euro price. The old buyer owners need enough of the combined equity to retain the value they held before. Target owners require enough new shares to cover theirs. As the number of new shares increases, the sellers’ fraction rises and the buyer owners’ fraction falls. Solving each participation condition gives a range of ratios supported by the same total economic pie.

How the result is built

T/(B + S) ≤ q ≤ (T + S)/B, where q = r × Nₜ/Nᵦ

B and T are standalone buyer and target equity values, S is net synergy in the same currency, Nᵦ and Nₜ are their original share counts, r is new buyer shares per target share, and q is new shares divided by original buyer shares.

  1. With combined value B + T + S, the seller group receives the fraction q/(1 + q). Requiring this claim to be at least T gives q(B + S) ≥ T.
  2. Buyer owners retain 1/(1 + q) of combined value. Requiring this stake to be at least B gives T + S ≥ qB.
  3. With B = €100m, T = €40m, S = €20m, 10m buyer shares and 4m target shares, q ranges from one-third to 0.6. Multiply by 10/4 for r between 0.8333 and 1.50.

When the reasoning holds

  • Identical ordinary shares share one equity value after completion. Preferences, a cash component or a price collar require separate allocation.
  • Both groups accept standalone values as their alternatives. A higher cash bid available to the seller raises its participation floor.

Terms in this lesson
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Synergy
Extra value expected from combining businesses, such as a saving attainable only after a merger.
First example

A buyer has 6m shares outstanding. It issues 0.5 share per target share for a target with 4m shares. The seller group receives 2m of 8m shares and owns 25%. If the combined equity is worth €80m, its stake is €20m.

Cash changes who bears the risk

With an all-cash offer, sellers know the agreed nominal amount if the deal completes. The remaining buyers bear changes in combined value after that point. Financing the cash may expose the buyer to interest, refinancing or leverage risk. A signed agreement can still have completion conditions.

In a fixed-ratio share exchange, sellers receive a specified number of buyer shares per target share. This puts them on both sides of the merger’s future economics. If integration goes badly their shares can lose value even though the agreed exchange ratio never changes.

From exchange ratio to economic ownership

Multiply the number of target shares by the ratio to find new shares. Then divide the new shares by all shares after the issue. An exchange ratio of 0.5 describes a transfer per target share. It says nothing about sellers’ ownership without both firms’ original share counts.

After combining, value per share equals combined equity value divided by all shares, assuming identical ordinary shares and no other changes. The seller’s realized consideration is new shares times that price. Multiplying new shares by the old bidder price instead gives an announcement reference amount, which may differ.

What happens at the boundaries?

A fixed-value share offer aims to deliver a set value and may calculate the share count using a specified pricing period. A collar limits the protection or share adjustment outside agreed boundaries. Read exactly whose downside is limited, when the reference is measured and what happens when the limit binds.

For a simple fixed-ratio offer, calculate seller value under multiple combined values. Compare buyer owners’ retained stake with their original equity. Sellers may accept a lower cash equivalent today if they expect greater upside than the buyer, or may demand cash to avoid concentration in one company.

Method

Reconstruct the post-deal register

  1. Count the shares

    Calculate newly issued shares and total shares after closing. Identify any other securities, options or cash components the case says to include.

  2. Divide the company

    Value combined equity on a consistent basis, including net synergy once. Divide by the enlarged share count, using the same unit of millions or individual shares throughout.

  3. Compare both owners

    Multiply each group’s retained shares by the new price. Subtract each original equity value. If the deal only creates synergy, both owner gains together should equal it.

Worked case

A fixed exchange between two distributors

A bidder has 12m shares worth €5 each. A target has 3m shares worth €8 each. The bidder offers two new shares per target share. Net synergy is €6m and there is no cash payment, debt change or other adjustment.

Working

  1. The bidder issues 6m shares, making 18m in total. Former sellers own one-third. Combined equity equals €60m + €24m + €6m = €90m.
  2. Each post-deal share is worth €5. Sellers receive €30m, a €6m gain. Original bidder owners retain €60m, unchanged. All expected synergy accrues to sellers.
  3. If synergy is only €3m, equity is €87m. Sellers’ one-third is €29m, and existing bidder owners’ two-thirds is €58m. Sellers still gain €5m while buyer owners lose €2m.

Interpretation

The announced ratio stays fixed in both scenarios. Value changes because the owners share the risks of the single combined enterprise.

A common mistake

Treating twice as many shares per target share as a guarantee that the seller receives twice the old bidder price in euros.

How to correct it

Recalculate the share price using the post-deal company. If the contract has a value guarantee or cash top-up, add it explicitly under the terms.

Quick check
A buyer with 8m shares issues 1 new share for every 2 of the target’s 4m shares. What percentage will the former target shareholders own?

They receive 2m shares. The combined company has 10m, so their ownership is 2/10. The exchange ratio of one-half is a shares-per-share term, not an ownership fraction.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A buyer has 10m shares and €100m standalone equity. A target has 2m shares and €20m equity. The offer is 1.5 buyer shares per target share, and net synergy is €10m.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate post-deal ownership, share price and economic consideration.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    What is the original buyers’ gain if synergy turns out to be zero?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Issue 3m shares, leaving 13m in all. Sellers own 3/13. Combined value is €130m, or €10 per share. The seller’s consideration is €30m.

Part B 3 marks

Without synergy total value is €120m. Buyers retain 10/13 × €120m = €92.31m. Their loss is €7.69m versus €100m and is matched by the sellers’ gain over €20m.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How can you determine which fixed share-exchange ratios leave both original shareholder groups at least as well off?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would calculate ownership after issuing the new shares and compare each group’s stake with its own standalone value. In this example any ratio between about 0.83 and 1.50 is feasible. At 1.25, sellers receive five of fifteen million shares. Their one-third stake is worth €53.33m, while existing buyer owners keep €106.67m. The €20m total gain has been divided between them.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The integration benefit falls to zero. Where do the bounds meet and why?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Both bounds for q become T/B = 0.4. With 10m original buyer shares and 4m target shares, r equals 1.00. At combined value €140m, issuing 4m shares gives target holders €40m and buyer holders €100m. Any other ratio transfers value from one group to the other.
