Who really pays a tax on each sale?

Solve a market with a specific tax collected on each unit traded.

  4. Rebuild the calculation
  5. Keep the two price movements and the change in volume visible
  6. A ticket platform introduces a levy

Theory and terminology

Tax incidence and deadweight loss

tax wedge · economic incidence · elasticity

What you should be able to explain

  • Solve a market with a specific tax collected on each unit traded.
  • Distinguish tax receipts, economic incidence and deadweight loss.

Two prices for the same trade

Before tax, buyers' willingness to pay and sellers' willingness to accept meet at one price. A tax per unit creates a wedge. For any surviving sale, what the buyer pays exceeds what the seller keeps by exactly that tax. The government can collect from either party in this competitive model with full compliance and no collection costs.

To find economic incidence, compare both new prices with the old one. Dividing the per-unit tax between them answers who bears the cost on surviving trades. It does not count the cost of transactions that disappear. A firm making a forecast needs both prices and the resulting change in sales volume.

Why a steeper curve matters

The side with fewer alternatives adjusts its quantity less for a given percentage change in price. Around the original equilibrium it bears more of a small tax. Compare absolute price elasticities measured at the same equilibrium. Slopes alone can mislead when axes or units change.

A commuter who must travel today may respond weakly to price, yet have more substitutes after changing jobs or routes. The distribution of a new tax can therefore change with the time horizon. The numbers below assume the demand and supply relationships stay fixed over the adjustment.

Value the trades that disappear

With no spillovers or existing distortions, the original competitive quantity maximizes gains from exchange. After tax, the last trades forgone had a positive gap between what a buyer would have paid and what a seller needed. With straight demand and supply curves, those gaps form a triangle.

Government receipts are a transfer in this calculation. Deadweight loss is the lost surplus after counting those receipts. If trading creates an unpriced external cost, the private curves omit social harm. A reduced quantity can then improve welfare and the unadjusted triangle is an incomplete measure.

Method

Build the result

Pb − Ps = t, Qd(Pb) = Qs(Ps). Revenue = tQ₁. For straight curves, DWL = t(Q₀ − Q₁)/2.

Pb is price paid, Ps price received, and t the unit tax, all in euros per ticket. Qd and Qs are demanded and supplied tickets per day. Q₀ and Q₁ denote equilibrium quantities before and after tax. DWL is lost surplus in euros per day.

  1. With t set to zero, use one price on both sides and solve the initial equilibrium.
  2. Replace the buyer price with seller price plus tax, equate quantities and solve the two new prices.
  3. Compare each price to the pre-tax price. Multiply the wedge by trades completed for receipts, and half the wedge by lost trades for deadweight loss.
Worked case

A ticket platform introduces a levy

Daily demand is 120 − 2Pb; supply is 15 + Ps. The market is competitive and there are no spillovers. A €9 levy applies to every ticket.

Working

  1. At one price, 120 − 2P = 15 + P gives P = €35. Both sides trade 50 tickets.
  2. After the levy, 120 − 2(Ps + 9) = 15 + Ps gives Ps = €29 and Pb = €38.
  3. Volume is 44 tickets. Buyers pay €3 more and sellers keep €6 less per continuing sale. Before tax, absolute demand elasticity was 2 × 35/50 = 1.4 versus supply elasticity 1 × 35/50 = 0.7.
  4. Receipts are 44 × €9 = €396 per day. Six forgone trades generate €9 × 6/2 = €27 of deadweight loss.

Decision and interpretation

Use €29 as seller revenue per ticket and 44 as volume. A seller with fixed commitments needs a separate profit calculation.

Quick check
Which observation identifies economic incidence on sellers?

Economic incidence measures the change in the price sellers keep. Filing responsibility and government receipts measure different quantities.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

In another market the inverse curves are buyer willingness to pay Pb = 50 − Q/2 and seller willingness to accept Ps = 5 + Q/4, in pounds. Work out prices, quantity, receipts and deadweight loss after a £6 tax.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked solution
  1. Equate the curves before tax. The £45 intercept gap equals 0.75Q, giving Q = 60 and P = £20.
  2. After tax the gap must be £6. Solve 45 − 0.75Q = 6 to get Q = 52, Pb = £24 and Ps = £18.
  3. Buyers bear £4 and sellers £2 on each trade. Government collects £312 and deadweight loss is £6 × 8/2 = £24.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A government collects €9 from sellers on every ticket. How much worse off is a seller on each ticket still traded?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Compare the seller's new price after tax with the old market price. Buyers may pay more, so the seller's price need not fall by €9. Equating demand and supply gives the split.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if supply were perfectly fixed at 60 units over the relevant period?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Given unchanged demand, buyers still value the sixtieth unit at £20. A £6 tax would reduce the net seller price to £14. Quantity and the lost-trades triangle would remain unchanged while trade stays feasible.
