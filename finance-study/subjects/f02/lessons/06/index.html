Risk and expected utility

Risk-averse people value an extra euro more when resources are scarce. They may prefer a certain payment to a gamble with the same average payout.

The starting point

An unpredictable year-end payment can be less attractive than a smaller guaranteed sum. A euro can matter more when income is low and essentials are difficult to cover. Economists express this using a utility function, a numerical representation of preferences.

For a risk-averse person the utility of wealth rises at a declining rate. Compute utility for each possible final wealth level, then average using the probabilities. A certainty equivalent is the guaranteed wealth giving that same average utility.

Theory and method

Jensen’s inequality and the certainty equivalent

Expected utility · Risk premium

How can a risk-averse founder willingly pay an insurer more than the expected cash loss?

A founder with a fixed household budget may value an additional euro more in a bad sales year than in a good year. Translate final wealth in each outcome into utility, a numerical representation of the founder’s preferences. For concave utility the average of the utilities lies below the utility of the average wealth. Full insurance shifts wealth from a good state to a bad one and can be valuable even after allowing for an insurer’s administration charge.

How the result is built

For concave u, E[u(W)] ≤ u(E[W]). CE = u⁻¹(E[u(W)]). Risk premium = E[W] − CE.

W is final wealth in euros, E is a probability-weighted average, u is utility and CE is the certain amount with identical utility. The inequality is strict for strictly concave utility and genuinely varying wealth.

  1. Let final wealth be €49 or €121 with equal probabilities. Average wealth is €85. Using square-root utility, the state utilities are 7 and 11.
  2. Average utility is 9, while utility at the mean is √85 ≈ 9.22. A guaranteed €81 gives utility 9, so the risk premium is €4.
  3. From a no-loss wealth of €121, expected loss is €36. Full insurance priced at up to €40 leaves at least €81 certain, making room for a €4 loading above expected loss.

When the reasoning holds

  • Use probabilities and terminal wealth from the same decision. Ignoring the premium in final wealth would make insurance look artificially attractive.
  • The numerical willingness to pay depends on the assumed utility curve. A diversified or wealthier owner may place a different value on the same euro risk.
Terms in this lesson
Expected utility
The probability-weighted average satisfaction from possible outcomes. It permits comparing choices when money has different value in different states.
Risk premium
Extra expected return for bearing risk, or the amount a risk-averse person would give up to replace a gamble with certainty, depending on context.
First example

Suppose utility is the square root of final wealth. A 50–50 gamble ends at €36 or €100. Average wealth is €68. Expected utility is (6 + 10)/2 = 8, which equals the utility of a guaranteed €64. The risk premium is €68 − €64 = €4.

Expected payoff and preferences

A shop owner choosing insurance wants to know what resources remain after a loss. Compare possible final wealth, including initial savings, premium and losses in each state. Probability-weighted average wealth describes the average of many comparable outcomes. It is not necessarily the amount this individual would accept for surrendering a risky prospect.

Expected utility assigns a number to each possible wealth level using the person’s preferences and then averages these utility numbers, weighted by probabilities. With a concave utility function, a euro gained when poor helps more than an extra euro gained when rich. Risk aversion in this model concerns the curvature of utility in final wealth.

Certainty equivalents and willingness to insure

Find the guaranteed wealth giving the same utility as the risky prospect. This is its certainty equivalent. The difference between expected wealth and the certainty equivalent is the risk premium. Risk-neutral people with linear utility have a zero risk premium in this framework, while strictly risk-averse people place a positive premium on eliminating genuine risk.

For full insurance, the premium paid by the owner reduces wealth in both states and the policy replaces the entire covered loss. Set guaranteed insured wealth equal to the uninsured certainty equivalent to find the maximum acceptable insurance price. This can exceed the expected loss, which helps explain a market for insurance covering administration costs.

What changes the comparison?

Wealth matters. The same absolute loss can be much more damaging to a small business than a large diversified owner. Insurance might also exclude part of the loss or require a deductible, an initial portion paid by the owner. Then final wealth is still uncertain and each insured state must be valued separately.

An expected-utility calculation depends on credible probabilities and the stated preference function. A result using square-root utility illustrates a decision rule for that person. It does not establish what every borrower or investor should choose. Explain whether more information would change probabilities, the payoffs themselves, or the preference assumptions.

Method

Value a full insurance offer

  1. List terminal wealth

    Write down what the person owns after all losses and payments in each state, with probabilities summing to one.

  2. Average utility

    Apply the utility function separately to each wealth amount and average its values. Reverse the function to obtain the certainty equivalent.

  3. Compare premiums

    Subtract the certainty equivalent from wealth in the no-loss state when full coverage restores that state. Compare this maximum premium with the quote.

Worked case

Insurance for a market stall

A stall owner has €100 before any loss. There is a 25% chance that €64 of stock is destroyed. The owner evaluates final wealth W by its square root. Full insurance pays any loss in full.

Working

  1. Uninsured wealth is €100 or €36, with utilities 10 or 6. Expected utility is 0.75 × 10 + 0.25 × 6 = 9.
  2. The certainty equivalent is €81 because its square root is 9. Expected uninsured wealth is €84, so the risk premium is €3.
  3. A maximum full-insurance premium of €19 leaves €81 guaranteed. Expected loss is €16. The difference of €3 is the value of removing variability for this owner.

Interpretation

A €17 quotation leaves €83 for certain, so it is attractive even though it costs more than expected claims.

A common mistake

The utility of expected wealth is substituted for expected utility.

How to correct it

Transform each possible final wealth before averaging, then convert back to a certainty equivalent. Taking the square root after averaging loses the effect of risk.

Quick check
Would this person prefer €65 for certain or this gamble?

The gamble is worth €64 for certain to this person. A sure €65 exceeds that certainty equivalent.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A consultant has €144 before a possible loss. With equal probabilities her final uninsured wealth is €144 or €64. Her utility is the square root of wealth, and full insurance restores the loss.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate expected wealth, expected utility, certainty equivalent and risk premium.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Find her highest acceptable full-coverage price and evaluate an offer at €42.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Expected wealth is €104. Expected utility = (12 + 8)/2 = 10. Certainty equivalent is €100, so the risk premium is €4.

Part B 4 marks

Maximum price is €144 − €100 = €44. Paying €42 produces €102 guaranteed, preferable to a gamble with €100 certainty equivalent. The premium exceeds the expected €40 loss by €2, within her risk premium.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How can a risk-averse founder willingly pay an insurer more than the expected cash loss?

Compare with a spoken answer

I would compare utility across the possible wealth levels. In this example the average wealth is €85, but the founder is just as satisfied with €81 guaranteed. That €4 difference is what the founder can pay beyond the €36 expected loss for full coverage. A €39 price leaves €82 for certain and is attractive on these preferences.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Could they pay the same loading if they cared only about expected euros?

Compare with the follow-up answer

With a linear utility function the certainty equivalent equals €85 and the risk premium is zero. The maximum full-cover price would then equal the €36 expected loss. A strictly larger premium lowers average terminal euros, so it would require some additional benefit outside this model.
