Market structure

Contrast competitive price taking with market power. A firm with market power considers how selling another unit affects the price on its other units.

The starting point

A wheat grower selling into a large market can often sell another small quantity at the prevailing price. A supplier of a distinctive product may need a lower price to attract the next buyer. If everyone pays that lower price, existing units earn less too.

Marginal revenue measures the change in all revenue when output increases. For a price-taking firm it equals market price. For a single-price firm facing downward-sloping demand, it can be below the price of the extra unit. Profit depends on marginal revenue compared with marginal cost.

Theory and method

Lerner index of market power

Price-cost margin · Inverse elasticity rule

A ticket platform knows its demand elasticity at the current fare. How can it use this to test whether its single-price markup is locally consistent with profit maximization?

A platform considering one more sale must lower its common fare enough to attract that customer. On a downward-sloping demand curve this reduction also affects continuing customers. The stronger the quantity response to a small price change, the smaller the optimal margin tends to be. The Lerner index compares price above marginal cost as a share of price. Under a specific single-price model that fraction is tied directly to demand elasticity.

How the result is built

(P − MC)/P = −1/ε at an interior single-price profit maximum.

P is the common price per ticket, MC is the incremental resource cost per ticket and ε is the signed point elasticity of demand. The markup and inverse elasticity are dimensionless.

  1. Write marginal revenue as P + Q(dP/dQ), with Q tickets sold. Divide the second term by P and multiply by P to get MR = P(1 + 1/ε).
  2. At an interior maximum equate MR to MC. Rearranging gives the price-cost share on the left and −1/ε on the right.
  3. At P = €50 and elasticity −2.5, the implied markup is 40%. Marginal cost consistent with the optimum is €50 × 0.60 = €30.

When the reasoning holds

  • The firm charges one price and operates at an unconstrained interior maximum with differentiable demand. Capacity limits or negotiated individual fares change the first-order condition.
  • The cost used is marginal cost at the chosen output. Average cost includes fixed expenditure and can imply a spurious markup comparison.

All notation examples

First example

A maker sells 10 lamps for €50 each, or 11 lamps for €48 each. Revenue increases from €500 to €528. The eleventh sale therefore adds €28 of revenue even though that customer pays €48.

The price-setting problem

A price-taking producer assumes its own sales have negligible impact on market price. If producing an extra unit raises cost by €7 and market price is €9, that unit increases operating contribution by €2. Limited capacity and shutting down can still matter, but there is no price cut on units already sold.

A firm setting one price for all customers faces an additional trade-off. To sell more units it may need to lower the price on every unit. Revenue gained from the additional customer is offset by revenue lost on earlier customers. Compare the change in total revenue with the change in total cost, rather than comparing the last quoted price with unit cost.

Deriving marginal revenue

Suppose inverse demand gives price P = a − bQ, where Q is quantity and a and b are positive constants. Total revenue is price times quantity, aQ − bQ². Differentiating with respect to Q yields marginal revenue a − 2bQ. It falls twice as fast as price in this linear example.

With constant marginal cost c, an unconstrained interior choice sets a − 2bQ = c. Compute Q and then return to demand to find the price customers will pay. For indivisible products evaluate neighboring feasible quantities directly. If a capacity limit is below that interior output, compare feasible choices at the boundary.

Market power and its limits

Facing downward-sloping demand does not guarantee positive economic profit. Research costs, fixed production costs and the possibility of entry can absorb the margin. Close substitutes make buyers more willing to leave. A high market share suggests an investigation but does not itself measure how strongly a price increase would reduce sales.

Price discrimination requires some ability to identify different willingness to pay and limit resale. A separate charge for an extra unit can allow extra sales without reducing the price to existing customers. That difference is why the single-price marginal-revenue formula should not be applied mechanically to a negotiated contract priced separately.

Method

Find a single-price optimum

  1. Write revenue

    Express the price customers accept at each quantity, then multiply by quantity for total revenue.

  2. Compare the margins

    Set marginal revenue equal to marginal cost when the functions and feasible range justify it. Otherwise use discrete changes in totals.

  3. Verify viability

    Insert the chosen quantity into demand, subtract variable and fixed costs and compare with available alternatives such as producing zero.

Worked case

Specialized training seats

A provider faces P = 70 − Q for seats in a course. Each seat costs €10 to deliver and there is a €400 avoidable course setup cost. Treat quantities as continuous and ignore taxes.

Working

  1. Revenue is 70Q − Q², so marginal revenue is 70 − 2Q. Solving 70 − 2Q = 10 yields Q = 30.
  2. At 30 seats, price is €40 and revenue is €1,200. Variable delivery costs are €300 and setup costs €400.
  3. The resulting €500 profit exceeds zero from cancelling the course. An additional full seat would require lowering the common price, so its direct fee is not its net revenue.

Interpretation

If the setup cost rose to €1,000, the same best positive-output quantity would generate a €100 loss. Cancelling would then be preferable if the fee can be avoided.

A common mistake

A solution finds the quantity where marginal revenue equals cost and reports marginal revenue as the selling price.

How to correct it

Read the actual price from demand at the selected quantity. Marginal revenue values an incremental change in total receipts and is generally different.

Quick check
If the eleventh lamp costs €32 to produce, how does moving to 11 sales affect profit?

Revenue rises €28 across all sales and costs rise €32. The supplier loses €4 on this expansion.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A venue faces P = 50 − Q with constant marginal cost €6 and an avoidable setup expense of €300. Treat seats as continuously divisible for the calculation.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Find output, price and operating profit at its best positive quantity.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Would the venue still open if the setup expense rose to €510?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

Revenue 50Q − Q² gives marginal revenue 50 − 2Q. Equate to 6 to get Q = 22, P = €28. Profit = (€28 − €6) × 22 − €300 = €184.

Part B 3 marks

The operating contribution before setup at the chosen quantity is €484. Opening yields −€26, below the €0 alternative if cancellation avoids the expense. Its optimal decision is then cancellation.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A ticket platform knows its demand elasticity at the current fare. How can it use this to test whether its single-price markup is locally consistent with profit maximization?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would calculate the observed share of price above incremental cost and compare it with the inverse elasticity magnitude. With price €50 and cost €30 the observed share is 40%, matching an elasticity of minus 2.5. That is a consistency check under the stated model. I would still verify capacity, marginal cost and whether the elasticity estimate really applies to the platform’s own price.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If the reported elasticity is −0.8 and marginal cost is positive, can these numbers describe an unconstrained interior optimum?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The formula would require a markup of 125% of price, which implies a negative marginal cost. With ordinary positive marginal cost this is inconsistent. In an inelastic region, raising the price also increases revenue while lower volume saves resources, so the firm can improve on the candidate point.
