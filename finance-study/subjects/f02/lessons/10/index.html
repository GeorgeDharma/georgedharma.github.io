Externalities and policy

A private return can omit costs borne by others. Apply the distinction to pollution or a bank whose failure disrupts payments for other firms.

The starting point

A factory can profit from extra production while a nearby village bears the cost of polluted water. A cost or benefit that falls on others outside the market price is an externality. The private decision can then differ from the socially preferred one.

Compare the extra benefit of another unit with both the producer’s extra cost and the harm to others. A charge equal to marginal external harm can align those incentives in a simple setting. In practice measuring damage and responding to changes in behaviour matters.

Theory and method

Coasean bargaining over an externality

Transaction costs · Efficient abatement

A mill can eliminate noise for €70 while neighbours suffer €100 of harm. Does it matter who initially has the right to quiet?

A mill’s overnight noise affects neighbours who never signed a sales contract. There are two physical choices, tolerate €100 of harm or spend €70 on a quieter machine. With a few identifiable parties, clear rights and cheap bargaining, both sides can benefit from choosing the lower resource cost. The initial legal entitlement affects who pays and who receives compensation. Negotiating, verifying damage or enforcing an agreement can consume the €30 of possible gain.

How the result is built

Net real gain from abatement = avoided external damage − abatement resource cost.

Abatement means reducing the harmful activity. Damage and cost are measured in euros over the same period. A payment between mill and neighbours divides these gains within the stated boundary.

  1. If neighbours hold the quiet right, the mill would pay at most €70 to avoid abatement. Neighbours need at least €100 to accept noise, so the mill abates.
  2. If the mill holds the noise right, neighbours can pay it between €70 and €100 to abate. At €85 each side gains €15 relative to noise.
  3. A €35 real negotiation cost exhausts the €30 available surplus under the second entitlement. Bargaining can then fail even with known damage and engineering costs.

When the reasoning holds

  • Parties can identify one another, verify the outcome and enforce the agreement. Many dispersed neighbours can create coordination and free-rider difficulties.
  • The simple efficiency result takes valuations as fixed across rights assignments. Material wealth effects or other unpriced harms can alter the physical choice.
Terms in this lesson
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Revenue
Amount earned by selling goods or services. The customer may pay later.
Expected credit loss
For a simple single-period example, default probability multiplied by exposure and the share lost if default occurs.
First example

A unit sells for €15 and costs €12 to make. If it causes €5 of additional downstream damage, the producer gains €3, while the combined net effect is €15 − €12 − €5 = −€2. A €5 charge changes the seller’s own calculation to −€2.

Private decisions and social costs

A trucking company considers an extra delivery. The fee benefits the company and the production cost enters its accounts. Noise or congestion imposed on nearby residents can also be a consequence even when the company makes no payment for it. A cost or benefit borne by outsiders outside the price is an externality.

To evaluate an additional unit, distinguish marginal private cost from marginal social cost. Social cost adds marginal external damage to the private resource cost. The efficient quantity equates marginal social benefit and marginal social cost under the usual assumptions. Average total damage divided by all units need not measure the extra harm caused by one more unit.

Taxes, standards and bargaining

A per-unit charge equal to marginal damage at the efficient quantity can make a privately maximizing supplier account for the external harm in a simple competitive model. A quantity standard directly limits production or pollution. The choice matters when damages, costs and enforcement differ among firms or are uncertain.

Charging a tax transfers money to the government. When calculating total social surplus in this simple setting, subtract actual resource costs and damage. Do not subtract tax revenue again as a new loss. Distribution still matters because customers, suppliers and taxpayers may be affected differently. Monitoring or collection can create real costs if specified.

Financial spillovers

A bank can judge an exposure using its expected private return while overlooking disruption to borrowers and payment networks if it fails. This gives a finance example of potential external cost. Distinguish the bank’s own expected credit loss from a loss occurring elsewhere that was not already in its payoff calculation.

A useful policy analysis asks what evidence would measure the additional spillover and what behaviour the intervention changes. A rule applied to one type of institution could move exposures elsewhere. A policy encouraging private precaution can still cost resources or reduce valuable lending, so assess both the external benefit and the real compliance or funding cost.

Method

Compare benefit and harm at the margin

  1. Construct a unit schedule

    For each feasible unit list benefit, private production costs and harm to outsiders. Give them consistent units and dates.

  2. Evaluate successive units

    Add an extra unit while marginal benefit exceeds the full incremental resource cost and damage. Check the entire feasible set if increments are irregular.

  3. Test an instrument

    Apply a fee or standard to the private decision and check whether it induces the desired quantity. Account separately for tax transfers.

Worked case

Three freight trips

Three optional trips deliver marginal benefits of €50, €38 and €28. Private resource cost is €20 per trip. External noise damage is €13 per trip. Assume payment fully reflects each customer benefit and earlier trips can be taken independently in order.

Working

  1. Without a fee, each benefit exceeds €20, so the company wishes to run all three. Net private gains are €30, €18 and €8.
  2. Full incremental cost is €33. The social net benefits are €17, €5 and −€5. Running two maximizes total net gain at €22.
  3. A €13 charge per trip makes the company compare €50, €38 and €28 with €33. It runs the first two. The €26 tax collected is a transfer within this example.

Interpretation

If the second trip instead took place at night with much greater damage, a uniform per-trip fee might fail to induce the efficient mix.

A common mistake

A solution subtracts both the pollution damage and the corrective tax from total social value.

How to correct it

Identify the real harm once. A payment received by another party within the same social boundary is a transfer unless additional collection or behavioural costs are specified.

Quick check
Under the same assumptions, how does the calculation change if the damage is only €1?

Subtract both extra production cost and damage from the benefit. €15 − €12 − €1 leaves €2 of total gain.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A lender can fund two independent projects. Their private net gains after expected credit losses are €12m and €7m. Estimated external payment-system disruption costs are €3m and €9m respectively. Ignore all other effects.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Find the privately preferred set and the socially preferred set.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Would a flat €5m charge per project lead to that set? Name one improvement and a practical limitation.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Both projects have positive private net gains. Social gains are €9m and −€2m after adding external harm, so only the first belongs in the efficient set.

Part B 4 marks

After €5m, private returns are €7m and €2m, still both positive. Risk-specific charges of €3m and €9m reproduce the desired decision in this model. Estimating marginal spillover reliably is difficult, especially if exposures move elsewhere.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A mill can eliminate noise for €70 while neighbours suffer €100 of harm. Does it matter who initially has the right to quiet?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

With effective rights and costless bargaining, quiet is efficient because €70 avoids €100 of harm. If the mill owns the right to make noise, an €85 inducement gives each side €15 versus the starting position. If neighbours already hold the right to quiet, the mill abates without that payment. I would ask what it costs to organize the neighbours and verify compliance.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Suppose new equipment reduces harm only to €40, for the same €70 cost. Is full spending still jointly worthwhile?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Avoided harm falls from €100 to €60, which is less than €70. Spending would destroy €10 of total value before negotiation costs. Under those changed technical facts, the parties cannot both gain from that particular retrofit without further benefits or a cheaper design.
