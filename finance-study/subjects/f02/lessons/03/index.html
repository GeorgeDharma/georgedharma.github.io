Demand and elasticity

Elasticity measures how sensitive quantity is to price. It helps explain why a price increase can raise or lower sales revenue.

The starting point

A cinema raises ticket prices and sees attendance fall. Knowing that customers buy fewer tickets leaves an important question open. Did the price rise outweigh the fall in quantity in the cinema’s total revenue?

Price elasticity of demand relates the percentage change in quantity to the percentage change in price, holding other influences fixed. With the midpoint method each change is divided by the average of its endpoints, so the result is the same whichever direction the comparison starts.

Theory and method

Point elasticity of demand

Price elasticity · Revenue elasticity

A pricing model predicts quantities using Q = 120 − 2P. At what price is a tiny increase roughly neutral for revenue?

A museum’s visitors react to higher prices, and its starting price determines how large that reaction is relative to the current sales base. On this straight demand curve two tickets are lost for each extra euro everywhere. Losing two out of eighty visitors is a smaller proportional response than losing two out of twenty. Point elasticity turns the slope into a percentage comparison at the exact operating point and connects that comparison with the direction of revenue.

How the result is built

ε = (dQ/dP) × P/Q. For R = PQ, dR/dP = Q × (1 + ε).

P is ticket price in euros, Q tickets per period, R revenue in euros, ε signed elasticity and dQ/dP the small quantity change per euro of price. Derivatives give local rates of change.

  1. From Q = 120 − 2P the slope dQ/dP is −2. At price €20, Q = 80 and elasticity is −0.5.
  2. A small price rise earns more on existing visitors but loses sales on marginal visitors. Differentiating price times quantity yields Q + P(dQ/dP).
  3. At €30, Q = 60, so ε = −1 and dR/dP = 0. At €40, Q = 40 and ε = −2, so raising price lowers revenue locally.

When the reasoning holds

  • The curve represents demand holding opening hours, competing events and income constant. A simultaneous shift breaks the price-only interpretation.
  • The derivative describes small price changes. For a large discrete rise, compute revenue at both endpoints because elasticity changes along this curve.
Terms in this lesson
Revenue
Amount earned by selling goods or services. The customer may pay later.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
First example

Tickets go from €10 to €11 and attendance from 1,000 to 900. Quantity changes by −100/950 and price by 1/10.5. Elasticity is about −1.11. Revenue falls from €10,000 to €9,900 because attendance responds strongly enough to outweigh the higher price.

Movement along a demand curve

If a cinema changes its own price while alternatives, incomes, preferences and showtimes stay fixed, the quantity response is a movement along its demand curve. If a new festival attracts people to the area during the same week, that event can shift the demand curve itself. Observed price and quantity changes mix both mechanisms.

A price elasticity divides a percentage change in quantity by a percentage change in price. It is unit-free, so ticket offices using euros or cents obtain the same answer. A slope expressed as seats per euro is different. On a straight demand line the slope can stay constant even as elasticity changes with the level of price and sales.

How to interpret the magnitude

With ordinary downward-sloping demand the elasticity is negative, and people often discuss its absolute magnitude. A magnitude above one describes a proportionately large response. Near a given point a small price rise then lowers total revenue. When the magnitude is below one, a small increase can raise revenue. The midpoint formula provides a consistent finite-interval calculation.

Revenue and profit need separate checks. More ticket sales may require expensive venue changes or incur royalties per seat. At the price that maximizes revenue, a further sale may still cost enough to reduce profit. When actual price-quantity pairs are known, compute total revenue and variable costs directly for both endpoints.

Substitutes, time and evidence

A commuter can switch travel time sooner than she can move house. Long-run demand responses can therefore differ from short-run responses. Market definition also matters. Switching from one cinema chain to another is easier than finding a substitute for all indoor entertainment. Elasticity estimated for one chain need not apply to the entire industry.

A better estimate isolates price changes from other demand shocks. Compare similar locations over the same period or use a credible pricing experiment when available. If discounts are offered only during slow weeks, the observed discount and low attendance can wrongly suggest that a lower price reduced demand.

Method

Measure a finite price change

  1. Compute midpoint changes

    Divide the change in quantity by the mean of its two levels and the change in price by the mean of its two levels.

  2. Form the ratio

    Divide the signed percentage quantity change by the signed percentage price change. Interpret both sign and absolute magnitude.

  3. Reconcile to results

    Calculate price times quantity at both prices, and subtract costs that vary with sales if the decision concerns profit.

Worked case

Museum admissions

A museum changes its price from €8 to €10. Attendance in comparable trial periods falls from 150 to 120 people. Variable cost is €2 per visit and fixed costs are unchanged.

Working

  1. Quantity changes by −30/135 = −22.22% using the midpoint. Price changes by €2/€9 = 22.22%. The midpoint elasticity is −1.
  2. Revenue is €1,200 at either endpoint. Variable cost nevertheless falls from €300 to €240.
  3. Contribution rises from €900 to €960. Equal revenue at the endpoints did not imply equal profit because serving visitors consumed resources.

Interpretation

If one period included school holidays and the other did not, this calculation still describes the observations, but price alone may not explain them.

A common mistake

The analyst reports a 20% quantity fall divided by a 25% price rise, then expects the same value after reversing the comparison.

How to correct it

For finite changes, use the same midpoint method in both directions or state clearly which initial-base convention was used. Recompute exact revenues as a check.

Quick check
If attendance instead stays at 980 after the price increase, what is the new revenue?

Multiply the new price by the new quantity, €11 × 980. A smaller attendance response leaves revenue above its initial level.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A parking site tests €4 and €5 hourly prices. Visits fall from 300 to 240. Serving each visit costs €1, with no change in other costs.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate midpoint elasticity and the change in revenue.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Calculate contribution at both prices and identify one fact needed before applying this result citywide.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Quantity change is −60/270 = −22.22% and price change is 1/4.5 = +22.22%, so elasticity is −1. Revenue is €1,200 in both trials.

Part B 4 marks

Contribution is (€4 − €1) × 300 = €900 and (€5 − €1) × 240 = €960. Verify comparable demand and substitutes, such as alternative parking availability, before generalizing.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A pricing model predicts quantities using Q = 120 − 2P. At what price is a tiny increase roughly neutral for revenue?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would use €30 as the revenue-neutral point for a tiny movement, since it gives sixty tickets and an elasticity of minus one. The additional euro collected per continuing sale is then offset at the margin by fewer sales. For an actual price card I would calculate complete revenues and costs, because moving from €30 to €40 reduces revenue from €1,800 to €1,600.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If every visitor costs €10 to serve, is €30 also the profit-maximizing price?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Contribution equals (P−10)(120−2P). Its slope is 140−4P, reaching zero at €35 and 50 visitors. Profit maximization tolerates lower attendance because each lost sale also saves €10 of serving cost. Fixed costs do not alter this interior choice if the venue stays open.
