Adverse selection

Before contracting, sellers or borrowers may know more about their quality. A price change can alter which applicants accept an offer.

The starting point

Two people apply for the same loan. Each knows more about their own project than the bank does. If safer borrowers walk away when the price increases, the bank’s customers change with the quoted interest rate.

Adverse selection arises from information differences before an agreement is made. A price can screen applicants unintentionally. Estimate the lender’s return over the borrowers who actually accept each offer, using their repayment probabilities and any recovery after default.

Theory and method

Akerlof’s market for lemons

Adverse selection · Pooling price

Can a market lose all its high-quality goods even though trading them would benefit both sides?

A buyer of a used machine cannot inspect its internal condition, but its owner knows it. A common price based on average quality can seem fair to the buyer. Yet owners of good machines compare that offer with the value of keeping their machines. If they withdraw, what remains for sale has lower average quality. The buyer then rationally lowers the offer. This feedback explains how private information can eliminate valuable trades.

How the result is built

Break-even pooling offer = Σᵢ Pr(type i given offered for sale) × buyer valueᵢ.

Type i is an observable-to-seller quality class. The conditional probability is the share among actual sellers at that offer. Buyer value and each seller’s reservation price are euros per machine.

  1. Half the machines are good, worth €160 to buyers and €130 to owners. Half are poor, worth €60 to buyers and €40 to owners. If all sell, buyers’ mean value is €110.
  2. At €110 good owners prefer their €130 outside value and withdraw. The sale pool is now entirely poor, so a buyer’s break-even offer falls to €60.
  3. Poor machines can trade between €40 and €60. Good machines fail to trade despite €160 buyer value exceeding €130 owner value by €30.

When the reasoning holds

  • Sellers know type before contracting and buyers cannot verify it cheaply. A credible warranty or independent test can separate types and restore some trades.
  • The derivation assumes competitive, risk-neutral buyers and voluntary participation. Mandatory pooling or other buyer preferences alter the offered price.

Terms in this lesson
Adverse selection
A contract can attract a different mix of applicants when people know more about their own quality or risk than the other side knows.
Interest rate
The price of borrowing money or the return for lending it, quoted as a percentage over a stated period.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
First example

At a €110 repayment, half the borrowers repay for certain and half repay with probability 0.8. With zero recovery on default, the bank expects €110 × 0.90 = €99. At a €120 repayment only the second group accepts, so expected receipts are €120 × 0.80 = €96.

Pooling people with private information

An insurer may see an applicant’s postcode but know less than the applicant about where the car will really be parked. Before a contract is agreed, people with different risk information can have different willingness to accept the offered terms. A pooled quote treats participating types alike even if their expected losses differ.

An average using the population is valid only if participation leaves that population mix intact. When premiums rise, lower-risk customers may be first to leave. The average cost among those still buying rises, potentially requiring another price increase. A market need not unravel completely. Underwriting information, compulsory participation or sufficiently large willingness to pay can support pooling.

Borrower participation and loan pricing

A bank receives the promised repayment only in states where the borrower pays, plus any recovery on default. The borrower’s payoff distribution can make high-rate loans attractive to riskier types who expect either a large success or a loss largely borne by the lender. Recalculate expected receipts at each quote using the people actually accepting it.

A lender can sometimes prefer rationing, turning down some applicants at an unchanged price, to raising the rate. Establishing this conclusion requires more than showing that rates are high. Show how acceptance changes, why expected net returns fall, and why demand remains above the amount of funding the lender is willing or able to supply.

Screening, signaling and evidence

Screening is action by the less-informed party to learn or separate types. A bank might offer a menu of interest and collateral terms to borrowers. Signaling is action by the informed party, such as investing in verifiable disclosure. A useful signal is easier or less costly for the type it is meant to identify.

Collateral requirements can also exclude capable applicants who lack wealth. A drop in observed default rates after stricter screening could reflect changing composition instead of improved behaviour. To assess selection, examine applications, acceptances, rejections and type indicators before the money is released, including those who declined the quote.

Method

Construct the selected pool

  1. Describe types

    List type shares, probabilities of repayment, recovery amounts and the maximum terms each type accepts.

  2. Recalculate participants

    For each offered price, drop those who refuse and rescale remaining weights to sum to one.

  3. Compare net receipts

    Calculate expected proceeds per funded loan after expected loss and costs. Distinguish an attractive per-loan return from total demand or program size.

Worked case

A regional equipment lender

Half of applicants repay with probability 1 and half with probability 0.70. There is no recovery on default. All accept a €110 promised payment per €100 loan, but safe clients leave at €125. Funding and administration together cost €90 per loan.

Working

  1. At €110 the pooled probability is 0.50 × 1 + 0.50 × 0.70 = 0.85. Expected receipts equal €93.50.
  2. At €125 only risky clients remain. Expected receipts become 0.70 × €125 = €87.50 despite the higher promise.
  3. Net of €90 cost, expected profit changes from +€3.50 to −€2.50 per loan. The lower quote therefore dominates these two offers on expected per-loan profitability.

Interpretation

If both types stayed at the higher rate, the bank would get €106.25 in expectation. Exit of safer borrowers is doing the economic work.

A common mistake

An observed increase in default is attributed to selection without checking behaviour after funding.

How to correct it

Look for evidence that a different set of applicants accepted the loan. If the same customers changed projects after receipt, the mechanism involves hidden action instead.

Quick check
What observation most directly tests the proposed explanation?

Selection concerns who accepts the contract. A change in behaviour by the same borrower after receiving money would call for a different explanation.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

An insurance pool contains 80 low-risk buyers with expected claims of €20 each and 20 high-risk buyers at €100. Ignore administrative costs. All buy at a €36 premium, but only high-risk buyers buy at €55.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Calculate expected profit per policy at both prices.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Why is replacing €36 by the original population average in the second calculation wrong? Identify a screening measure and one cost.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

At €36, average claims are (80 × €20 + 20 × €100)/100 = €36, so expected profit is zero. At €55 the selected pool has €100 claims per policy, producing a €45 expected loss per policy.

Part B 3 marks

Policy costs depend on actual buyers, now entirely high risk. Verifiable risk information can support differentiated pricing, but gathering it costs money and some lower-risk buyers may lack documentation.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Can a market lose all its high-quality goods even though trading them would benefit both sides?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Yes. A buyer offering €110 on the initial mix cannot attract owners who value their good machines at €130. Once the buyer recognizes that only poor machines remain, the break-even offer is €60. The missing good-machine transactions would individually create €30 of gains. The market can lose them because buyers cannot distinguish quality at the time of sale.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Would a perfectly reliable certificate costing €20 per good machine permit mutually beneficial good-machine trade?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Yes under these facts. A certified seller needs at least €130 plus €20 certification cost, or €150, while the buyer values that machine at €160. Offers strictly between €150 and €160 leave both better off. At a certification cost above €30 the good type’s potential surplus would be exhausted.
