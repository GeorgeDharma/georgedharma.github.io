Moral hazard and agency

After receiving funds, a borrower may change behaviour. Monitoring, incentives and contract terms can alter the payoff from taking hidden risks.

The starting point

Someone managing another person’s money may have different incentives once the funds are committed. If a borrower keeps most of the upside from a gamble while a lender absorbs much of the loss, an attractive contract can produce an unattractive choice.

Moral hazard describes how hidden behaviour after an agreement responds to incentives. Limited liability caps what the owner loses when the project fails. Covenants, owner funds at risk and monitoring can discourage risk shifting, though each has a cost.

Theory and method

Principal–agent incentive compatibility

Hidden action · Performance-based pay

What minimum success bonus would make a manager choose costly effort when the employer cannot observe that effort directly?

A manager can choose ordinary effort or spend extra time checking invoices. Extra effort is privately costly and reduces the chance of an expensive error. The employer observes whether a batch succeeds, but cannot contract directly on the hidden checking time. A bonus on a successful batch changes the manager’s expected pay. Compare the increase in expected bonus with the private effort cost to find when careful checking is in the manager’s own interest.

How the result is built

Incentive compatibility requires (pH − pL)B ≥ cH − cL.

pH and pL are the probabilities of success under high and low effort. B is the euro bonus on success. cH and cL are private effort costs in euro-equivalent units. A common fixed wage cancels from the comparison.

  1. Ordinary effort produces success with probability 0.60 and costs €0. Careful effort succeeds with probability 0.85 and costs the manager €50.
  2. The careful action adds 0.25B expected bonus. To recover the extra effort cost, 0.25B must be at least €50, so B must reach €200.
  3. At €200 the manager is indifferent. A slightly higher bonus strictly favours care, but the employer should still compare its total expected payout and the value of fewer errors.

When the reasoning holds

  • The manager is risk neutral over the exercise payoffs and uses the stated success probabilities. With aversion to uncertain bonuses a larger or differently structured payment can be needed.
  • Success can be verified and the employer can credibly pay the bonus. A manipulable success metric could reward behaviour that fails to improve invoice quality.

Terms in this lesson
Moral hazard
A contract can change behaviour after it is signed when the other party cannot fully observe or control those actions.
Liability
Something owed to another party, such as a loan or an unpaid supplier bill.
Covenant
A promise or condition in a financing agreement, such as maintaining an agreed level of cash.
Agency problem
People acting on behalf of someone else can have different goals and better information about their own decisions.
Principal
The amount borrowed or invested before interest is added.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
How to read the notation
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.

All notation examples

First example

A project owes a bank €100. A safe plan pays €110 for certain, leaving the owner €10. A risky plan pays €150 with probability 0.5 and €0 otherwise. The owner expects €25 from the risky plan, although total expected project proceeds fall from €110 to €75.

Different incentives after the agreement

A lender approves a project on the understanding it will invest in reliable machinery. Once funds are released, the owner may undertake a more volatile venture. If the choice is hidden or difficult to enforce, the original terms can influence which action the owner takes. This is moral hazard after contracting.

Agency problems arise when one person acts on behalf of someone with different interests. The fact that an outcome is bad does not establish misconduct or hidden action. A genuinely prudent project can fail. Identify the decision the agent could change, what the principal can observe, and how the contract changes the payoff from that decision.

Divide project payoffs by the contract

With simple debt of face value D and no other claims, a lender receives the smaller of project proceeds and D when it can claim all proceeds up to repayment. The owner receives the excess above D, floored at zero by limited liability. Project value is the sum of these two amounts in every state.

Debt can make additional upside attractive to the owner while shifting part of the downside to the creditor. Calculate expected total proceeds for each action and separately calculate the private payoffs. A privately preferred action with a lower total expected value demonstrates the incentive conflict. Which party bears a loss depends on security, guarantees and bankruptcy costs.

Changing incentives without eliminating the business

A covenant constrains an activity, such as taking additional senior debt or investing outside an agreed line. Monitoring can detect breaches, while collateral or owner co-investment can change who pays when a project fails. Each arrangement can also create costs or prevent a good future decision under unforeseen conditions.

Judge an intervention by comparing what it costs to implement with the loss it prevents. When behaviour is unobservable, monitoring must yield informative evidence in time to act. Guarantees need a guarantor able to pay in the bad state. A contractual promise that is impossible to enforce may make little difference to expected payoffs.

Method

Test debt-induced risk shifting

  1. Allocate by state

    Give the creditor up to its contractual claim in every state. Write the remaining non-negative payoff for the owner.

  2. Compare preferences

    Use the stated probabilities to calculate total, owner and lender expected payoffs for each project choice.

  3. Redesign terms

    Change a specific payoff or observable constraint and recompute the private ranking as well as the implementation cost.

Worked case

A borrower with a shipping license

Debt promises €80. A cautious route earns €100 for certain. A speculative route earns €160 with probability 0.5 and zero otherwise. The vessel has no other recoverable value. Ignore discounting and assume both routes cost the same.

Working

  1. The cautious route gives €80 to the bank and €20 to the owner. Total proceeds are €100.
  2. The speculative route gives the bank €80 and the owner €80 on success, zero to each on failure. Their expected payoffs are €40 and €40. Total is €80.
  3. The owner prefers €40 to €20 although society loses €20 of expected proceeds. A credible personal penalty above €40 in the failure state would make the owner's speculative expectation less than €20.

Interpretation

A loss of exactly €40 on failure leaves the owner indifferent. A proposed guarantee matters only if it can actually be collected on failure.

A common mistake

A loan officer treats every loss as evidence that the borrower secretly took extra risks.

How to correct it

Compare observable actions after lending, contract restrictions and plausible shocks. An outcome is compatible with several actions, even when a theoretical conflict exists.

Quick check
What does the lender expect to receive under the risky plan if it can collect only project proceeds?

In the successful state the bank receives its €100 claim. In failure it receives zero. Their equally weighted average is €50.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

Debt promises €60. Project S yields €75 for certain. Project R yields €120 with probability 0.4 and €20 otherwise. Project proceeds are the only assets available initially.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Compute the payoffs for each party and identify the preferred action for the owner and for total value.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    A separate guarantee collectible from owner wealth charges the owner €20 if R fails and S always succeeds. Does it change the owner’s choice?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

S pays bank €60 and owner €15. R pays expected bank 0.4 × €60 + 0.6 × €20 = €36, owner 0.4 × €60 = €24, total €60. Owner prefers R, but S creates €75 compared with €60.

Part B 3 marks

R’s expected owner payoff falls to €24 − 0.6 × €20 = €12. S still gives €15, so the owner prefers S. Enforceability and owner resources are necessary for this calculation.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

What minimum success bonus would make a manager choose costly effort when the employer cannot observe that effort directly?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would set the bonus above €200 if I need a strict preference for careful effort. The checking changes success probability by a quarter, so each €1 of success bonus raises the private payoff to checking by €0.25. A €200 bonus exactly offsets the €50 effort cost. I would separately verify the manager accepts the overall package and that the employer benefits from the improved quality.

Interviewer’s follow-up

At a €240 bonus the success probability under low effort rises to 0.70. Is the same contract still sufficient?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The effort advantage is now 0.85 minus 0.70, or 0.15. Its expected bonus benefit is €36, below the €50 cost. The threshold rises to €50/0.15, about €333.33, with a strictly higher amount for an unambiguous effort choice.
