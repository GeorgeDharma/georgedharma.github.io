Choice through time

A budget links spending today with spending later. The interest rate determines the exchange between the two dates when borrowing and lending are available.

The starting point

Buying a laptop today can reduce the money available for a future trip. A budget through time puts spending at different dates on comparable terms. It also shows how borrowing allows more spending now in exchange for repayments later.

With one year between dates, and a common borrowing and saving rate r, one euro saved now becomes 1 + r euros later. A spending choice must fit the present value of available resources. Fees, borrowing limits and different loan and deposit rates can change the feasible choices.

Theory and method

Intertemporal Euler equation

Consumption smoothing · Marginal rate of substitution

A worker can save freely across two years. What makes shifting one more euro out of this year worthwhile?

A worker choosing dinner today or a better dinner next year trades current enjoyment for future enjoyment. Saving one euro reduces current consumption by one euro and supplies 1+r euros later. The preferred allocation balances the marginal value of these two changes, allowing for impatience. This condition uses a budget to say what is possible and a utility function to say what the person values. A borrowing limit can stop the worker reaching the preferred balance.

How the result is built

u′(c₀) = β(1+r)u′(c₁). With u(c)=ln c, c₁ = β(1+r)c₀.

c₀ and c₁ are consumption euros now and next year, r is the annual real rate under consistent purchasing-power units, β is the weight on future utility, u′ is marginal utility, and ln is natural log.

  1. Lifetime resources are €100 now and €110 next year with a 10% rate. In present units the total budget is €100 + €110/1.10 = €200.
  2. For log utility and β = 1, the first-order condition implies c₁ = 1.10c₀. Substitute into c₀ + c₁/1.10 = 200.
  3. This yields c₀ = €100 and c₁ = €110. If endowments were €50 now and €165 later, lifetime resources still equal €200, so the same unconstrained plan requires borrowing €50.

When the reasoning holds

  • The person can borrow and save at the same rate at the desired quantity. A credit limit can leave marginal utilities unequal at the boundary.
  • The illustration assumes certain income, positive consumption and the stated time preference. Risk and uncertain future needs can create precautionary saving.
Terms in this lesson
Interest rate
The price of borrowing money or the return for lending it, quoted as a percentage over a stated period.
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
How to read the notation
Subscripts · S₀ and S₁
A small index names the time or item. If S₀ = €40 and S₁ = €42, the price moves from €40 at time 0 to €42 at time 1. A subscript i can label a company instead of a date.

All notation examples

First example

You have €100 today and expect €105 next year. At 5%, spending €60 today leaves €40 to save. The savings become €42, allowing future spending of €147 when next year’s income arrives.

Build the lifetime budget

A student can move money between this term and next term. Saving reduces today’s consumption but increases next term’s spending power. Borrowing does the reverse. With one common rate r, the present value of consumption cannot exceed the present value of resources, assuming repayment is required and there are no gifts or other funds.

Let y₀ and y₁ be resources available now and later and c₀ and c₁ consumption on those dates. If everything is eventually spent, c₁ = y₁ + (y₀ − c₀)(1+r). Its slope is −(1+r). Each extra euro consumed today gives up 1+r euros of future consumption, whether it reduces savings or increases borrowing under the common-rate assumption.

Preferences through time

The budget says which plans are affordable. It cannot tell you which one the student prefers. Time preference measures how much someone values consumption at one date against another. Expected income, urgent needs and the desire to smooth living standards can influence the choice. Two people with the same resources can therefore choose different plans.

A rise in interest rates increases the reward to postponing consumption, a substitution effect. It also changes purchasing power relative to an existing plan. A net saver may feel richer, while a net borrower faces a higher cost. A prediction about total current consumption requires both effects and information on preferences.

When borrowing and saving differ

Real borrowers can face a loan rate higher than the rate earned on deposits. The budget line then has a corner at the endowment point where neither borrowing nor lending occurs. Points using savings follow the deposit rate and points needing borrowing follow the loan rate. A cap on debt can rule out some plans entirely.

In an exam, begin with the direction of the transfer. A plan requiring money before income arrives may be valuable when discounted yet infeasible because no credit is available. Distinguish the value of a project with access to capital markets from this individual’s ability to finance it by the required date.

Method

Compare two-date plans

  1. Mark endowments

    Put income and obligations on a two-date timeline and identify whether a proposed plan is saving or borrowing now.

  2. Select the applicable rate

    Grow savings at the deposit rate or deduct repayment using the loan rate. Apply borrowing limits after calculating the required amount.

  3. Test affordability and preference

    Compare the future amount available with proposed spending. Ranking multiple affordable plans requires a preference or an explicit objective.

Worked case

Two rates for a family budget

A household receives €900 today and €840 next year. It can save at 4%, borrow at 10%, and borrow at most €100. Ignore inflation and fees.

Working

  1. Spending €800 today leaves €100 savings and €944 next year, from €840 income plus €104 deposit proceeds.
  2. Spending €980 today requires an €80 loan and leaves €840 − €88 = €752 next year.
  3. Spending €1,030 would require borrowing €130. The credit limit makes this plan infeasible even if the household would happily repay €143 next year.

Interpretation

There is no single straight-line tradeoff across all choices here. The change in slope and cap matter for today’s attainable consumption.

A common mistake

A loan-funded plan is calculated using the household’s savings rate.

How to correct it

Check the sign of current surplus first. A negative surplus indicates borrowing and requires a loan repayment schedule, as well as any applicable limit.

Quick check
How much future spending do you give up if you spend another €10 today from those savings?

The €10 could have grown to €10.50. Its future opportunity cost includes the principal and interest.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A teacher has €600 now and €660 next year, with 5% on savings, 12% on loans and a €60 credit cap.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Find next year’s consumption if today’s spending is €500 or €650 and all resources are eventually spent.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Can the teacher spend €670 now? Does a higher deposit rate automatically raise the future funds of someone choosing €650 of current spending?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Spending €500 leaves €100 savings, generating €105, so future spending is €765. Spending €650 borrows €50, repayable at €56, leaving €604.

Part B 4 marks

€670 requires €70 of debt and exceeds the €60 cap. At €650 the teacher borrows, so with that choice and unchanged loan terms the deposit rate does not change future funds.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A worker can save freely across two years. What makes shifting one more euro out of this year worthwhile?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would save until losing the marginal satisfaction from one euro today just equals the discounted satisfaction from the future euros it buys. With log utility and equal time weights the future consumption level is 1.10 times today’s at a 10% rate. For €200 of present resources that gives €100 and €110. Whether the worker must borrow to reach it depends on when income arrives.

Interviewer’s follow-up

For the €50-now, €165-later endowment, what if the worker can borrow only €20?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The preferred €50 loan is unavailable. Under the stated preferences the worker uses the €20 limit, consumes €70 today and repays €22 next year, leaving €143. The standard Euler equality need not hold at that binding limit because an additional transfer toward today is infeasible.
