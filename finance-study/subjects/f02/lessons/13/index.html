Untangle a price change from a loss of purchasing power

Construct a compensating budget that makes the original bundle affordable.

In this lecture

  1. A budget is a set of feasible bundles
  2. Build a comparison with the old bundle affordable
  3. Restore actual income and interpret the sign
  4. Rebuild the calculation
  5. Follow one household through three feasible choices
  6. A travel allowance that would preserve the old basket
  7. Independent exam problem
  8. Explain it in an interview
Theory and terminology

The Slutsky decomposition

income effect · substitution effect · compensated demand

What you should be able to explain

  • Construct a compensating budget that makes the original bundle affordable.
  • Separate a total change in quantity into a substitution effect and an income effect.

A budget is a set of feasible bundles

Let a household spend its weekly budget on trips and meals. The budget line gives the combinations it can afford. Its slope measures the meals given up for one more trip. Doubling the trip fare changes that trade-off even if wages are unchanged. It also makes the old pattern of spending more expensive.

Preferences determine which feasible bundle the household chooses. For a concrete example use a household that maximizes the product of trips and meals and can buy fractional units. Spending everything on just one good gives zero product. Its best interior choice allocates half its budget to each good. This rule is an assumption about this household, not a general fact about shoppers.

Build a comparison with the old bundle affordable

First find the old choice. Price the exact same quantities at the new prices. Give the household exactly that much money in a thought experiment, and let it choose again. The old bundle is feasible, but the household can respond to the changed relative prices by choosing a different bundle.

This construction is called Slutsky compensation. Another construction, Hicks compensation, holds the old level of satisfaction fixed instead of the ability to buy the old basket. For a large price change the intermediate quantities can differ. State the chosen convention in an exam or in an analyst's estimate.

Restore actual income and interpret the sign

The move from the old bundle to the compensated choice measures substitution. The move from the compensated choice to what the actual unchanged income will buy measures the income effect. Their sum is exactly the total change because the intermediate quantity cancels.

For a normal good, extra purchasing power raises demand at fixed prices. On a price rise, the income effect then reinforces substitution away from that good. For an inferior good, extra income reduces demand, so the income effect can offset substitution. Observed sales alone seldom establish the two channels without a demand model or additional variation.

Method

Build the result

M* = p₁x₀ + py y₀. Δx = (x* − x₀) + (x₁ − x*).

M* is expenditure at the new prices needed for the old bundle. p₁ is the new trip price and py the unchanged meal price. x₀, x* and x₁ are old, compensated and actual new trips. y₀ is old meals. Quantities are weekly.

  1. Find the original bundle with the old budget and prices.
  2. Reprice its quantities at the new prices, then apply the demand rule to that temporary budget.
  3. Reapply the rule at actual income and new prices. Record both movements with signs and check the sum.
Worked case

A travel allowance that would preserve the old basket

The household has €120 per week, trips cost €3 and meals cost €1. The fare rises to €6. At every price and budget this household spends half its money on each good.

Working

  1. Originally €60 buys 20 trips and €60 buys 60 meals.
  2. The old basket at new prices costs 20 × €6 + 60 × €1 = €180. The temporary compensation is €60.
  3. With €180, it would spend €90 on trips and €90 on meals, choosing 15 trips and 90 meals. Substitution reduces trips by 5.
  4. At actual income of €120 it chooses 10 trips and 60 meals. Removing the compensation reduces trips by another 5. The total fall is 10.

Decision and interpretation

A €60 allowance restores the option of the old bundle. It need not restore the original number of trips when the household can redirect the allowance.

Quick check
Old consumption includes 12 units of a good. Its price increases by £2, all other prices stay fixed. How much new money would make the original basket affordable?

Only the repriced item changes the cost of the old basket. Twelve units times £2 adds £24.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

A different household spends half of its £160 budget on books and half on meals. Books fall from £4 to £2. Meals remain £2. Use old-bundle compensation to split the change in book purchases.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked solution
  1. Originally it chooses 20 books and 40 meals. At the new prices this bundle costs £40 + £80 = £120.
  2. At the temporary £120 budget it chooses 30 books and 30 meals. The substitution effect on books is +10.
  3. At the actual £160 budget it chooses 40 books and 40 meals. The income effect is +10 and the total gain is 20 books.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A household buys fewer train trips after fares rise. How would you tell whether the new relative price or the squeeze on its budget explains the fall?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Calculate the trips it would choose at the new fares with just enough money to buy its old bundle. The move to that choice isolates substitution. Removing the temporary compensation measures the purchasing-power effect.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Would the same half-budget calculation fit a household that always needs one refill cartridge per printer?

Compare with the follow-up answer

No. Fixed-proportion preferences require analyzing complete pairs and comparing their combined cost. The chosen bundle need not rebalance spending equally when only one component changes price.
