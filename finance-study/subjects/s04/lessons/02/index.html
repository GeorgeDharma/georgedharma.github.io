Corporate Distress and Restructuring
Advanced finance / Lesson 2 of 13

Causes of distress

Separate temporary working-capital stress, structural operating losses and excessive leverage. A different cause requires a different remedy.

The starting point

A cash shortage can originate in the operating cycle, the basic economics of the business or the amount of debt. A profitable exporter paid three months after shipment may need temporary financing. A factory selling every unit below its variable cost loses more cash as it sells more.

Build a short bridge from sales to operating cash and then from operating cash to debt payments. Test whether the problem would persist with ordinary customer payment timing, an achievable operating plan or lower debt. More than one cause can be present.

Theory and method

Operating leverage and financial leverage

Contribution margin · Degree of operating leverage

A business has a healthy gross margin. Why can a modest fall in sales eliminate the money available for interest?

A theatre signs a substantial annual lease and earns a contribution on each ticket. Once its fixed costs are covered, extra tickets produce considerable additional operating profit. Near break-even the reverse effect is powerful because the same lease remains when attendance slips. Debt interest is another fixed commitment after operating profit. Separating operating leverage from financial leverage shows whether empty seats, an expensive lease or excessive borrowing makes the loss severe.

How the result is built

EBIT = Q(p − v) − F. DOL = Q(p − v)/EBIT. DFL = EBIT/(EBIT − I)

Q is annual units, p euro price per unit, v variable cost per unit, F annual fixed operating cost, I annual fixed interest, EBIT operating earnings, DOL operating leverage and DFL financial leverage. The leverage measures are local percentage sensitivities.

  1. At 100,000 tickets, a €30 price and €12 variable cost generate €1.8m contribution. Subtract €1.5m fixed costs to get €0.3m EBIT, so DOL equals 6.
  2. Interest of €0.2m leaves €0.1m pretax earnings. Financial leverage is 3. A 1% attendance fall removes €18,000 contribution, 6% of operating earnings and 18% of pretax earnings.
  3. The ticket volume covering both fixed operations and interest is (€1.5m + €0.2m)/€18, or about 94,445 whole tickets. Covering principal and reinvestment requires a separate cash test.

When the reasoning holds

  • Price, variable cost per ticket and fixed cost remain unchanged over the modeled volume range. Capacity shutdowns or discounts can create a nonlinear relationship.
  • The ratios become unstable close to zero earnings. Use the cash amounts and thresholds directly when the denominator is tiny or negative.
Terms in this lesson
Principal
The amount borrowed or invested before interest is added.
Margin
In business accounts, profit expressed relative to sales. In derivatives trading, margin is collateral required to support a position.
First example

A distributor generates €3m of steady operating cash after necessary investment. Annual interest and principal total €4m. Collecting a delayed €0.5m invoice helps this month, but leaves a recurring €1m annual gap if the debt schedule remains unchanged.

Trace the first break in the cash chain

Cash distress can arise from weak operations, a longer operating cycle or an unsuitable funding structure. Start with the product. Does each additional unit generate cash above its variable cost? Do total margins cover fixed overhead and necessary investment at a realistic sales volume?

Then trace how long the business must finance stock and customer credit. A profitable exporter can run short when buyers pay weeks late. Finally compare operating cash with contractual interest and principal. A healthy business may be unable to meet a maturity that was financed on the assumption of perpetual refinancing.

Separate a seasonal squeeze from structural loss

Seasonal inventory and slow collection can cause a reversible gap. Establish the evidence for reversal through customer orders, historical collection and actual post-period sales. A return to normal cannot be assumed when inventory is obsolete or customers have lost access to credit.

Contribution margin is selling price less variable cost per unit. Break-even sales volume equals fixed operating costs divided by unit contribution under constant price and cost assumptions. This operational threshold does not include debt repayment unless included expressly. A business can clear operating break-even yet fail to service debt.

Which intervention changes the cause?

A maturity extension buys time but does little for products that lose money on every sale. Repricing, changing product mix or exiting a loss-making contract can improve unit economics. Refinancing can smooth payments if the remaining business generates sustainable cash, but may transfer risk to new lenders.

Different causes can coexist. A recovery forecast might assume higher prices, quicker collections and cheaper funding simultaneously. Test which assumption matters most and whether they can hold together. A substantial price increase could slow sales or customer payments, offsetting the expected cash improvement.

Method

Diagnose in sequence

  1. Test production economics

    Separate price, variable cost and fixed operating outlay. Find the unit contribution and the volume needed to cover fixed costs before discussing financing.

  2. Test cash timing

    Translate profits into money actually available after tax, working capital and necessary investment. Identify which use of cash will release and which is permanent.

  3. Test debt service

    Compare normalized cash to interest and principal by period. Select an operating or financing action and show numerically which gap it removes.

Worked case

A manufacturer with two problems

A plant sells 100,000 units for €50 each and spends €38 per unit on variable costs. Fixed operating cash costs are €1m per year. Ignore tax and investment changes. A one-time stock build absorbs €0.25m. Annual debt service is €0.4m.

Working

  1. Contribution is €12 per unit, or €1.2m total. After fixed costs operating cash before stock is €0.2m. Operating break-even volume is €1m/€12, about 83,334 whole units.
  2. This year the stock build reduces cash before debt service to −€0.05m. Against €0.4m service the current shortfall is €0.45m.
  3. Even if the stock use disappears, normal operating cash of €0.2m still falls €0.2m short of debt service. Faster collection solves the timing component, not the recurring mismatch.

Interpretation

At the same unit margin, about 116,667 units would cover fixed costs plus €0.4m debt service, before further working-capital or investment needs. Check whether that volume is commercially feasible.

A common mistake

Using a single late customer payment to explain a cash problem that persists in a normal year.

How to correct it

Construct both the extraordinary-period and normalized cash bridges. Identify operating break-even, normal debt service and the specific evidence for any proposed reversal.

Quick check
A manufacturer receives immediate customer payment but loses €8 on every unit before financing costs. Which change addresses the operating cause most directly?

A loss on each incremental unit is structural. Faster volume growth or a new loan can increase the cash needed while the per-unit loss persists.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A firm sells 40,000 units at €30, incurs €20 variable cost per unit and €0.3m yearly fixed costs. It owes €0.18m annual debt service. Ignore taxes and investment.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate operating cash and recurring debt-service shortfall.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Would a one-time €0.1m collection improvement cure the recurring issue? Calculate the volume needed instead.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Contribution is €10 × 40,000 = €0.4m. After fixed costs €0.1m remains, leaving €0.08m less than debt service.

Part B 3 marks

The extra collection covers this year’s €0.08m but does not recur. At unchanged economics, (€300,000 + €180,000)/€10 per unit = 48,000 units are needed to cover both, before funding growth.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A business has a healthy gross margin. Why can a modest fall in sales eliminate the money available for interest?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would calculate contribution per ticket first. At €18, 100,000 tickets generate €1.8m but the fixed lease and other overhead absorb €1.5m. Interest takes another €0.2m, leaving only €0.1m. A small attendance decline therefore consumes most of the remaining earnings. If operations remain profitable before interest, changing the debt schedule and changing the operating cost base solve different parts of the problem.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The lender halves annual interest to €0.1m while attendance stays at 90,000. Is the business now cash-safe?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Operating earnings at that attendance are €0.12m, so interest leaves €0.02m pretax. That is only €20,000 before taxes, principal, maintenance capital and working-capital demands. I would recalculate cash by date and test the sustainability of attendance before declaring the rescue viable.
