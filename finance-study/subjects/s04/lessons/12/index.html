Valuing a distressed rights offering and its backstop

Calculate post-money equity value, dilution and the value of exercising a right.

Theory and terminology

Distressed rights offerings and backstops

backstop commitment · dilution · recovery waterfall

What you should be able to explain

  • Calculate post-money equity value, dilution and the value of exercising a right.
  • Separate old-claim recovery, new cash and backstop compensation in a creditor comparison.

Trace old consideration separately from new investment

Assume a plan exchanges claims for a pool of new shares. Those shares represent recovery on old claims. Alongside them, eligible creditors may receive the right to buy additional shares at a subscription price. That purchase supplies the cash needed to operate or pay closing obligations. Calculate value delivered on the old claim after deducting any new money the creditor supplies.

A right has economic value when the resulting share is worth more than its subscription price. A participating creditor gives up cash today to retain more future upside. A nonparticipant may sell a transferable right, but restricted eligibility, a deadline or a nontransferable right can make that impossible. State these terms in the recovery table.

Price all the shares after the financing

The pre-money equity value is the estimated value of the reorganized owners’ stake before the cash comes in. Add the new cash and deduct actual financing leakage such as a cash backstop fee. Divide by all the shares outstanding after issuance. Additional cash may be spent immediately, but its intended use must already be reflected consistently in the valuation.

Using the pre-money share price to mark newly issued shares ignores dilution. At a meaningful issue discount, part of the old owners’ value moves to exercisers. At-par financing on the same assumption creates no subscription bargain. Financing that saves the business can separately create operating value. The example holds underlying business value constant.

Examine the underwriting bargain

A backstop party commits to buy unsubscribed shares. Its risk rises if other lenders decline because the business deteriorates. A fee can compensate for committed capital and downside. Discounted shares, exclusive investment rights or control features can also convey value to the backstop. Estimate those elements together.

Show a no-take-up and a full-take-up state. An unexercised commitment may still earn its contracted fee. An observed gain calculated at an adviser’s valuation is a paper estimate. It cannot establish that the fee is fair or that all classes obtained equivalent rights.

Method

Build the result

Post-issue share value = (E + N − F)/(S + Q); exercise benefit per right = post-issue share value − subscription price.

E is pre-money reorganized equity value, N cash raised, F cash issue fees, S old-plan shares and Q additional shares. A right here purchases exactly one new share. Values are in one currency and shares in matching counts.

  1. Allocate S as old-claim recovery. Divide N by the subscription price to obtain Q.
  2. Compute the common post-issue value of every share, then value both the base allocation and the subscription rights.
  3. Subtract the creditor’s new cash from its gross equity value. Record fees received as separate compensation.
Worked case

An equipment group raises exit capital

Pre-money equity is $80m across 8m base-plan shares. The company raises $20m by issuing 4m shares at $5. It pays a backstop fee of $2m in cash. A creditor holds 10% of base shares and may buy 10% of the issue. Rights are nontransferable. Other valuation assumptions stay fixed.

Working

  1. Post-money equity is 80 + 20 − 2 = $98m. Twelve million shares imply $8.1667 per share.
  2. If the creditor declines, its 0.8m base shares are worth roughly $6.533m. It owns 6.67% after issuance.
  3. If it exercises, it pays $2m for 0.4m shares. Its total 1.2m shares are worth $9.8m. After subtracting the new investment, economic value is $7.8m, or roughly $1.267m more than declining.
  4. If the regular subscribers take just 3m of the 4m offered shares, the backstop buys 1m for $5m. At the modeled price its subscription gain is about $3.167m, plus the $2m fee. The company still raises $20m gross.

Decision and interpretation

Split the backstop’s specific compensation from the recovery it receives on any old claims. The estimated subscription gain can disappear if reorganized equity proves substantially less valuable.

Quick check
A creditor receives equity worth $7m after investing $2m of new cash. What is its net economic amount attributable to its original position and rights, ignoring taxes and fees?

Comparing the result with recovery from the old claim requires subtracting the separate $2m invested.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

Another debtor has €60m of pre-money equity and 6m base shares. It raises €12m for 2m new shares with no fees. A creditor has 5% of both allocations. Compare exercise and lapse.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked solution
  1. Post-money value is €72m over 8m shares, or €9 per share. Subscription costs €6 per share.
  2. Lapsing leaves 0.3m shares worth €2.7m. Exercising buys 0.1m more for €0.6m.
  3. Exercising leaves 0.4m × €9 − €0.6m = €3m net. The benefit is €0.3m.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why can two creditors with the same old claim finish a restructuring with different wealth?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

They can receive the same base equity and subscription rights, yet only one can afford to invest fresh cash. When the subscription price is lower than the value of the resulting shares, exercise preserves valuable participation. A backstop investor can also earn a fee or buy unsubscribed shares.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if subscription rights can be sold freely for their modeled value?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Selling 0.1m rights at €3 each would bring €0.3m, making the noninvesting holder’s total €3m before trading costs. Transferability can protect a constrained creditor from losing the discount.
