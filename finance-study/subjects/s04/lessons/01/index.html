Liquidity and solvency

Map near-term payments separately from the value of the entire business. A solvent borrower can face a timing crisis and an insolvent one can have cash today.

The starting point

Liquidity asks whether cash is available when a payment comes due. Solvency asks whether the business’s economic resources can cover its obligations overall. A valuable factory cannot pay tomorrow’s wages unless it can be sold, borrowed against or used to generate cash quickly enough.

Use two separate schedules. Put actual receipts, committed borrowing and due payments on a calendar to test the cash gap. Then compare reasonable values for assets or the operating business with all claims, including obligations beyond this month.

Theory and method

Cash-flow liquidity test and economic balance-sheet test

Funding gap · Economic solvency

A lender’s rescue loan meets tomorrow’s bill. How can the firm become more liquid yet less valuable to its existing shareholders?

A restaurant group owns valuable leases but its free account cannot cover a near-term tax bill. A new lender can exchange cash for a repayment promise. At drawdown cash rises and so does debt, so the immediate injection can fix a timing gap without improving the economic balance sheet. If the group pays arrangement fees or uses the money to keep loss-making branches open, the resources ultimately available to existing claimants can decline even while tomorrow’s bill clears.

How the result is built

Gapₜ = max(0, Pₜ + Bₜ − Aₜ). Economic net worth = V − D

Pₜ is payments due through date t, Bₜ required ending operating buffer, Aₜ cash available before those payments including verified inflows and drawable funding, V economic value of all assets and D economic claims on the same date. All are euros.

  1. A firm facing €1.5m of bills and needing €0.2m in reserve with €1.1m accessible needs €0.6m additional usable money. A €0.6m gross loan with a €0.05m upfront fee would leave a gap.
  2. A €0.65m loan less a fixed €0.05m fee provides exactly €0.60m usable cash. Before paying bills its cash rises €0.60m but its debt rises €0.65m.
  3. If initial assets are €12m and claims €11m, the fee lowers economic net worth from €1m to €0.95m before operating effects. Payment of an already recognized bill reduces cash and the claim together.

When the reasoning holds

  • The asset valuation includes each operating asset or future cash stream once. Adding assets already represented by going-concern value overstates the solvency cushion.
  • Facilities included in available cash must be drawable on the required date. A signed line conditional on a covenant the firm has breached may not fill the gap.
Terms in this lesson
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Covenant
A promise or condition in a financing agreement, such as maintaining an agreed level of cash.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Receivable
Money a customer owes for a sale already made.
First example

A business owns assets worth €15m and owes €12m. Tomorrow it must pay €1m, but has €0.3m accessible and no immediately drawable facility. It faces a €0.7m liquidity gap despite a positive €3m asset cushion under these valuations.

Two balance sheets for two questions

A company can own a profitable building and still miss tomorrow’s payroll. The building matters to overall economic value but cannot necessarily be sold, pledged and converted into spendable cash overnight. Liquidity concerns money available on the date it is needed. Economic solvency concerns whether resources cover obligations in value.

Cash is an asset in both tests, but the two analyses use different dates and comparisons. For liquidity, use unrestricted cash, expected cleared receipts and facilities actually drawable by the payment date. For solvency, estimate assets on an internally consistent sale or going-concern basis against the relevant claims.

Why published figures can mislead

The accounting balance sheet measures different assets under different rules and typically describes an earlier date. Book equity can be positive even if customers have stopped paying and specialist equipment would sell for very little. Economic value can also exceed book value where a profitable customer network is absent from the accounts.

Debts need equal scrutiny. A loan with years to maturity may be immediately payable if an enforceable acceleration provision is triggered. A supposedly committed facility might be unavailable following a covenant breach. Read the facts supplied. Legal insolvency tests and directors’ duties depend on jurisdiction and cannot be inferred solely from these teaching measures.

Match the remedy to the diagnosis

A temporary timing gap can sometimes be solved with a bridge that is repaid from a confirmed collection. Borrowing to cover a permanent shortfall in business value can delay a wider restructuring and reduce future recovery if it funds ongoing losses. Evaluate what cash flow will repay the extra borrowing.

A company with cash today but inadequate long-run value needs a credible operating or capital change before cash runs out. Conversely, forced liquidation of a valuable but illiquid company can destroy going-concern value. Prepare both tests before choosing a rescue, sale or standstill.

Method

Prepare a two-part diagnosis

  1. Map payment dates

    Start with free cash in the correct entity. List payments, cleared collections and drawable lines by day or week. Subtract the minimum needed to keep operating.

  2. Value the business

    Estimate ongoing or realizable assets and obligations on a shared valuation date. State assumptions for uncertain claims and avoid adding both operating value and the assets already supporting it.

  3. Link remedy to repayment

    Size the cash gap and explain whether the later proceeds cover the bridge plus its cost. If value is insufficient, identify whose claims or operating cash flows must also change.

Worked case

The freight operator’s Friday payroll

An operator has €0.7m free cash and a €0.4m confirmed draw available Friday. Payroll and rent of €1.4m fall due that day, with a required €0.2m buffer. A verified €1m receivable clears the following Tuesday. Independently, going-concern assets including receivables are valued at €15m against €12m total claims.

Working

  1. Usable resources this Friday are €1.1m. After the €1.4m payments cash would be −€0.3m. Preserving the €0.2m buffer requires €0.5m of additional funding.
  2. The separate economic value test shows a €3m cushion. Do not add the €1m receivable to €15m again, because the asset estimate already includes it.
  3. A €0.5m bridge could be repaid from Tuesday’s receipt while retaining €0.7m before other intervening cash costs and bridge charges. Verify clearing, access to the money and any other Tuesday payments.

Interpretation

The business is economically solvent on the stated valuation but faces a real Friday funding failure. The bridge is only defensible if the receipt and continuing operations support repayment.

A common mistake

Treating available borrowing as new net worth.

How to correct it

Drawing a loan brings in cash and creates a matching obligation before financing charges. It can cure a timing problem without adding economic value by itself.

Quick check
A company has €2m cash now and owes €1m tomorrow. All its assets together are worth €5m and all claims total €7m. Which description fits these assumptions?

A calendar shows adequate cash for that one payment. A valuation comparison shows a €2m overall shortfall. One test cannot substitute for the other.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A company has €0.4m unrestricted cash. It must pay €0.8m tomorrow and keep €0.1m for operations. Assets including cash are worth €6m and total claims are €7.2m. No other money arrives tomorrow.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Quantify the two shortfalls.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Would a new €0.5m loan solve both? Explain.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

The immediate funding gap is €0.8m + €0.1m − €0.4m = €0.5m. The economic value shortfall is €7.2m − €6m = €1.2m.

Part B 3 marks

It would close tomorrow’s cash gap if drawable immediately. Assets and liabilities would both rise €0.5m before charges, leaving economic value €1.2m short. A credible capital or operating remedy is still needed.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A lender’s rescue loan meets tomorrow’s bill. How can the firm become more liquid yet less valuable to its existing shareholders?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would show the dated funding gap alongside an economic assets-to-claims comparison. This case needs €0.60m of usable funds, so a €0.65m loan with a €0.05m fee covers the calendar gap. It also reduces equity value by €0.05m before considering any benefit from avoiding disruption. I would then test whether avoiding forced closure preserves more value than the rescue costs.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Suppose an immediate closure would destroy €0.40m of otherwise recoverable value. What is the economic case for this bridge?

Compare with the follow-up answer

A €0.05m fee can be defensible if the bridge genuinely prevents a €0.40m loss at the same valuation date and no larger future losses or charges arise. The incremental value versus closure is €0.35m. Confirm the next reliable repayment source, because extending a failing chain indefinitely would change that comparison.
