Debt overhang and new finance

A positive-value investment may benefit old creditors more than the investor funding it. Compare ways of sharing the incremental surplus.

The starting point

Debt overhang arises when existing claims absorb so much of an investment’s benefit that the party paying for it has little incentive to proceed. The project can increase the total amount available and still leave the proposed investor worse off.

Compare what each claimant receives with the project and without it, including the new contribution. New senior financing, a creditor concession or sharing the upside can change incentives. Those arrangements need feasible terms and the consents assumed in the case.

Theory and method

Myers debt overhang and risk shifting

Underinvestment · Asset substitution · Limited liability

Can a distressed firm reject a good investment and also favour a bad gamble? Explain the difference.

A deeply indebted company can turn down a positive-value machine because the old lender captures most of the extra proceeds. This is Myers’s debt-overhang mechanism. The same limited liability can make shareholders favour a different, highly risky strategy: they keep the upside once debt is paid and do not bear losses beyond their stake. The first concern is who funds and captures a new investment. The second is who benefits when the risk of existing assets changes.

How the result is built

Owner payoff = max(V − D, 0). Fund if E[max(V₁ − D, 0) − max(V₀ − D, 0)] ≥ I

V₀ and V₁ are estate proceeds without and with a project, D outstanding prior debt, I cash owners contribute, and E the state-weighted expectation. All values use a common date and currency.

  1. With €100m debt and €80m estate, owner value is zero. An investment costing €5m that adds €9m of certain proceeds creates €4m group surplus, but still leaves owners below the debt threshold.
  2. Separately consider replacing the certain €80m strategy with a gamble paying €120m with probability 40% and €40m otherwise. Expected total proceeds fall to €72m.
  3. Owners now expect 40% × €20m = €8m, while lenders receive €64m on average. Their loss from €80m exceeds the owners’ gain because the gamble destroys €8m overall.

When the reasoning holds

  • The prior debt rank is fixed and owners can choose the project without repricing it. Covenants, renegotiation and new senior financing can change the incentives.
  • The comparison assumes the given risk-neutral, same-date figures. A real decision must address timing, probabilities and stakeholder-specific risk exposure.

Terms in this lesson
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
First example

Assets will produce €80m for an old €100m debt next year. A €10m cash investment raises proceeds to €95m. Ignore discounting. The extra €15m creates €5m net value, but existing shareholders would fund €10m and still receive nothing if the old debt keeps full priority.

A good project can go unfunded

Imagine old creditors are already entitled to virtually every euro the current business is likely to earn. Shareholders might be asked to supply fresh cash for a project whose proceeds initially fill the old creditors’ shortfall. A positive gain for the business as a whole may leave the party paying for it worse off.

This is debt overhang, a conflict over who captures incremental value. Calculate payoffs with and without the project for old lenders, owners and the new financier. Subtract the new contribution from the appropriate party. Simply calculating the project’s total return misses the incentive problem.

Separate project value from its financing terms

A project costing €3m and producing €5m extra cash creates €2m surplus on a same-date or otherwise comparable basis. Paying the old creditors first can transfer part or all of the €5m to them. A change in priority, debt write-down or agreement to share incremental proceeds may enable financing.

New senior financing can itself harm old secured creditors if it displaces existing collateral. A viable negotiated deal must leave consenting parties at least as well off as their credible alternative, unless the governing formal rules provide another mechanism. New-money priority is a case assumption, not a universal legal rule.

Use the correct baseline

Old lenders’ legal face amount is not necessarily their economic outside option. If a €100m claim would recover €55m without the project, a rescue returning €60m might improve their position despite a substantial accounting write-off. Price the realistic no-deal recovery before identifying negotiable surplus.

Risk complicates fixed forecasts. If the project fails, who loses the additional contribution and which old assets are pledged? Compare cases as well as expected totals. A debt investor’s limited upside and substantial downside can produce different decisions from those of the shareholders.

Method

Identify a feasible rescue contract

  1. Build two waterfalls

    List old assets and claims without the project. Repeat after the incremental project cash arrives under the old priority terms. Apply dates and discounts consistently.

  2. Find the incentive gap

    Calculate project net value and each party’s change after contributing fresh cash. Identify who captures the increase and who is asked to pay.

  3. Test redesigned terms

    Compare proposed priority or payoff sharing with each participant’s outside option. Confirm receipts add to available value, including the new money’s required return.

Worked case

New equipment behind an old claim

A firm’s assets will yield €30m and old lenders are owed €45m. Installing equipment costs owners €5m now and adds €9m to future proceeds. Ignore time value and risk. Without financing changes, old debt keeps first claim.

Working

  1. Without installation, lenders recover €30m and owners zero. With installation, assets produce €39m, all still owed to lenders. Owners would contribute €5m and receive zero.
  2. Across the business, the project creates €9m − €5m = €4m net. The old priority nevertheless discourages owners from supplying the money.
  3. Suppose lenders consensually permit a new investor to receive €6m first for its €5m contribution. Remaining €33m goes to old lenders. The new investor gains €1m and old lenders gain €3m over their €30m baseline.

Interpretation

The redesigned contract allocates the €4m net gain. Owners receive zero in both funded and unfunded waterfalls, but do not provide the investment in the new arrangement.

A common mistake

Concluding a project has zero value because the shareholder who was asked to finance it receives no cash.

How to correct it

Calculate total incremental operating proceeds and investment first, then show how the old claim transfers the benefit away from that particular funder.

Quick check
A €6m investment adds €10m of proceeds, all of which would go to existing lenders. What is the project’s total surplus before bargaining, ignoring discounting?

The project adds €10m at a cost of €6m. A €4m total surplus can coexist with a refusal by shareholders who would pay the €6m and receive nothing.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

Assets will pay €40m against €65m old debt. A €6m project adds €11m proceeds. A new investor requests €8m first payment and old lenders can consent. Ignore time value.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Why might owners decline under the original terms even though the project creates value?

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Test the proposed first payment for each financing party.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Total surplus is €11m − €6m = €5m. With old lenders first, all €51m goes to debt, so owners supplying €6m receive nothing.

Part B 3 marks

The new investor receives €8m on €6m, a €2m gain. Old lenders receive €51m − €8m = €43m, gaining €3m over their €40m outside option. The gains sum to €5m.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Can a distressed firm reject a good investment and also favour a bad gamble? Explain the difference.

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Debt overhang is an underinvestment problem. The owners must put in €5m, yet the extra €9m still goes to old lenders, so they decline despite a €4m gain for the business. Risk shifting can run in the opposite direction. The risky replacement strategy gives owners €8m expected upside while total expected assets fall. I would consider a waiver or new senior claim for the first and risk limits for the second.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If old lenders fund the €5m productive machine themselves, can they rationally do so?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Their proceeds rise from €80m to €89m. After the €5m contribution their net improvement is €4m, assuming they control or can contract for that gain. They might fund directly or let an external provider take a priority return that splits the €4m surplus.
