Workouts and formal processes

Compare maturity extensions, interest concessions, new money and debt-for-equity swaps. Apply the legal rules specified for the case jurisdiction.

The starting point

A workout changes obligations by agreement. Extending maturity delays an upcoming payment. Lowering interest reduces ongoing cash demands. Exchanging debt for shares removes a fixed claim and gives creditors a stake in future outcomes. First identify which cash problem the proposal addresses.

Formal proceedings can provide tools for coordinating creditors, but the voting, enforcement and approval rules depend on the jurisdiction and contract. In a teaching case, apply the specified consent rules and compare expected recoveries, the cost of delay and the new obligations after restructuring.

Theory and method

Present-value haircut in a debt exchange

Maturity extension · Economic haircut

A borrower proposes repaying all principal after extending maturity. Is the lender receiving full economic value?

A lender expected a large repayment immediately and is offered small coupons plus the same principal several years later. Even if the new note prints the old face amount, its market value depends on the payment dates, required yield and expected collection. A present-value haircut compares the value surrendered with the value of all instruments received. This can differ substantially from both the face-value haircut and the borrower’s immediate cash relief.

How the result is built

PV(new) = Σₜ Cₜ/(1 + y)ᵗ + F/(1 + y)ᴺ. Economic haircut = 1 − PV(package)/Vₒ

Cₜ is the new coupon cash in period t, y the matching risk-adjusted annual yield, F principal payable at maturity N, and Vₒ the chosen pre-exchange benchmark value. Package also includes any cash or equity received, in euros.

  1. An old €10m principal is payable now under the comparison. A new three-year note pays €0.2m at each year-end and €10m at year three. Its face-value haircut is zero.
  2. At a 10% annual yield, the coupon PV is €0.2m × 2.48685 = €0.497m. Principal is worth €10m/1.10³ = €7.513m.
  3. The new note is worth about €8.011m. Relative to €10m currently payable, economic haircut is about 19.89%. Compare separately with expected enforcement recovery if the old face is uncollectible.

When the reasoning holds

  • The yield matches the expected risk treatment and currency. Discounting promised cash at a risky yield and separately applying a full duplicate default haircut can bias the result.
  • The benchmark must be identified. A haircut relative to face does not imply the creditor is worse off than an enforceable alternative worth only €7m.
Terms in this lesson
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Swap
A contract to exchange payment streams, such as fixed interest for floating interest.
Principal
The amount borrowed or invested before interest is added.
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
Enterprise value
Value of the business operations attributable to its capital providers. A bridge adjusts it to the amount due to shareholders.
First example

A borrower has €2m cash before debt service, owes €0.4m yearly interest and faces €5m principal due now. Extending the principal by two years fixes this immediate timing gap if counterparties agree. It still needs a credible way to pay or refinance €5m at the new date.

A restructuring instrument changes a specific promise

A maturity extension postpones principal. A rate reduction cuts future interest. A principal haircut lowers the amount owed. A debt-for-equity swap replaces a fixed claim with a residual ownership claim. Write down precisely which cash commitments change and which operating problem remains.

Extending debt can solve a short-term maturity wall without making an unprofitable firm viable. Cutting interest can leave an impossible balloon repayment at the new maturity. A sponsor promising new funds must establish the amount, timing, conditions and rights acquired for that money.

Why collective action matters

Different creditors have different rankings, tax positions, maturity dates and alternatives. A lender may prefer others to grant concessions while insisting on full payment itself. This holdout problem can frustrate an agreement whose total value exceeds liquidation. Identify which signatures or class votes the hypothetical rules require.

A formal process may help coordinate claims and impose outcomes under its applicable procedures, but timing, costs, eligibility and protections vary. Compare a negotiated workout and formal alternative under the stipulated case rules rather than assuming the law of one country applies everywhere.

Judge both affordability and creditor economics

For the company, calculate cash payments it can actually afford after essential operating investment. For the creditor, compare the present value and risk of the new terms with enforceable alternatives. A claim can keep its €10m face amount yet lose value if payment is pushed far into the future without compensation.

For equity offered in exchange, value the residual after the revised debt and new-money claims. Applying the creditor’s new ownership percentage directly to operating enterprise value overstates the payout when debt remains. Add the value of any debt retained to the value of shares received.

Method

Compare a workout menu

  1. Calculate the cash path

    List old and new interest, principal dates and required operating cash. Find the first shortfall remaining under each proposal and its funding source.

  2. Value the consideration

    Discount new debt payments and value any equity on a residual basis. Compare each creditor’s expected payoff with its alternative, using common dates and risk assumptions.

  3. Check implementability

    Identify consent, timing and execution costs in the case. Show whether the assumed rescue funding arrives before the business must pay workers or essential suppliers.

Worked case

Swapping part of a loan for shares

A company owes €12m at 8% interest and generates €0.75m annually for servicing debt. A lender would exchange €5m principal for 60% of the new equity. Remaining debt stays at €7m and 8%. Reorganized operating value is €13m with no other claims or cash.

Working

  1. Annual interest falls from €0.96m to €0.56m. Interest was €0.21m above cash and becomes €0.19m below cash. Principal still requires a repayment or refinancing plan.
  2. Reorganized equity is €13m − €7m = €6m. The creditor’s new shares are worth €3.6m. Assuming remaining debt is worth its €7m face for this calculation, total package is €10.6m.
  3. If liquidation would return €9m now, the package appears €1.6m better on the stated value basis. Check the operating valuation and whether the remaining debt truly merits face.

Interpretation

The nominal €5m exchanged is not the economic loss by itself. The creditor receives shares, and its original claim may already be worth less than face.

A common mistake

Multiplying a lender’s ownership percentage by operating enterprise value without subtracting debt that remains after the swap.

How to correct it

Derive residual equity first. Then add the creditor’s retained debt position and compare the entire package with its outside option.

Quick check
A loan costs €1m interest annually, while sustainable cash before interest is €0.7m. Which change alone directly closes the recurring annual gap?

At €0.6m interest the firm has €0.1m left before other debt payments. Moving principal alone leaves annual interest €0.3m above sustainable cash.

Written practice · 6 marks

Independent exam problem

A borrower exchanges €4m of a €10m loan at 10% for 50% of post-swap equity. Operating value is €11m after restructuring. Remaining debt is worth face. Cash before interest is €0.8m yearly.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate interest affordability before and after the swap.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Calculate the lender’s package and compare with a €7.8m immediate alternative, assuming comparable valuation dates.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Before, interest is €1m and exceeds cash by €0.2m. After, interest on €6m is €0.6m and leaves €0.2m before principal repayment.

Part B 3 marks

Equity is €11m − €6m = €5m. The lender gets €2.5m equity plus €6m debt, total €8.5m, €0.7m above the alternative under the assumptions.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A borrower proposes repaying all principal after extending maturity. Is the lender receiving full economic value?

Compare with a spoken answer

I would describe both the face amount and present value. Extending €10m at a 2% coupon for three years preserves printed principal but delivers only about €8.01m at a 10% yield. That is nearly a 20% economic reduction relative to immediate face. Whether I would accept depends on what I can recover otherwise and whether the new payment schedule is affordable for the borrower.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Enforcement is expected to yield €7.6m today. The board argues that accepting any haircut is irrational. What would you say?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The new package exceeds the stated enforceable alternative by roughly €0.41m on a common valuation date and risk basis. Its shortfall versus contractual face is still real. I would verify the assumed yield, enforcement estimate and cash-service capacity before choosing the higher economic alternative.
