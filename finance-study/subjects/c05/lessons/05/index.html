Capital asset pricing

CAPM relates expected excess return to exposure to the market. Estimate beta as covariance with the market divided by market variance.

The starting point

Some risks can be spread across many holdings. A risk shared with the whole market is harder for a diversified investor to escape. In the capital asset pricing model, or CAPM, the expected reward for holding a security depends on its sensitivity to market returns, called beta.

Beta is the covariance of the security and the market divided by the variance of the market. The model gives expected return as the risk-free rate plus beta times the market’s expected return above that rate. This is a statement about expectations under the model. A single year’s realized performance can differ widely.

Theory and method

Sharpe–Lintner CAPM and Hamada’s leverage adjustment

Equity beta · Unlevering and relevering beta

A highly levered listed peer has equity beta 1.6. Can its cost of equity be transferred unchanged to a project funded with less debt?

A listed company’s shares absorb operating shocks after its debt holders’ fixed payments. More debt concentrates an underlying business shock on a smaller equity base. The peer’s equity beta therefore reflects both business exposure and financing. A project analyst can remove a peer’s leverage under a stated tax-shield model, judge comparability of the operating activity, then add the proposed leverage and apply the market premium. A beta copied directly can confuse two economically different equity claims.

How the result is built

βU = βE/[1 + (1 − t)D/E]; kE = rf + βU[1 + (1 − t)d]MRP

βU is unlevered operating beta and βE the peer’s equity beta. D/E is the peer’s market debt-to-equity ratio, d the project target debt-to-equity ratio, t the assumed corporate tax rate, rf the risk-free rate, MRP the expected market risk premium, and kE the project’s required equity return.

  1. With t = 25% and peer D/E = 1, divide βE = 1.6 by 1.75 to obtain unlevered beta about 0.9143.
  2. At target d = 0.40, multiply by 1 + 0.75 × 0.40 = 1.30. Target equity beta is about 1.1886.
  3. With rf = 3% and MRP = 5%, required equity return is about 8.94%, versus 11% obtained from the peer’s original leveraged shares.

When the reasoning holds

  • The conventional Hamada expression here treats debt beta as negligible and applies its particular tax-shield assumptions. Materially risky debt or a different debt policy calls for a modified valuation.
  • The peer’s core activity must share the project’s operating exposure. Unlevering cannot fix a mismatch between, for example, regulated transmission and merchant energy trading.

Terms in this lesson
Capital asset pricing model (CAPM)
A model that relates an asset’s expected return above the risk-free rate to its exposure to the wider market. That exposure is called beta.
Asset pricing
Studying how risk, time and market conditions relate to the prices and expected returns of investments.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Regression
A way of estimating how an outcome varies with one or more observed inputs.
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
How to read the notation
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.

All notation examples

First example

In a monthly regression using the same return units, a stock’s covariance with the market is 0.003 and market variance is 0.002, so its beta is 1.5. With a 3% annual safe rate and a 4% annual market premium, the model gives 9% expected annual return.

Read beta as a regression slope

Plot a security’s excess returns against market excess returns over matching dates. In a linear regression the slope beta measures how much security return moves on average for a one-unit market move. A beta of 1.4 describes a conditional sensitivity, not a prediction that every 1% market increase produces precisely 1.4%.

The intercept measures average return left unexplained by the fitted sensitivity to the market. A residual varies month by month. A large surprise on a single announcement date is different evidence from a stable positive intercept over a long, pre-specified sample.

From sensitivity to required reward

CAPM adds an equilibrium claim to the statistical regression. If investors share the model’s opportunities and the market portfolio is the aggregate risky portfolio, expected excess returns lie on a line against beta. The risk-free asset has zero beta, while the market portfolio has beta one.

The relevant risk is covariance with the portfolio investors already hold. Total volatility includes firm-specific risks a broad holder can diversify. A volatile company with little market co-movement can consequently have a lower CAPM expected return than a quieter company with greater co-movement.

Choosing and interpreting the benchmark

The theoretical market includes all risky wealth. A local equity index is a practical proxy with omissions. Thin trading, leverage changes, sector shifts and a short estimation history can also change estimated beta. A failed CAPM test against one proxy may reflect both model and benchmark.

A negative-beta claim can protect purchasing power when the market performs poorly. The model can assign such a claim expected return below the safe rate. Its value comes partly from insurance, even if its standalone payoff varies.

Method

Test a pricing statement

  1. Estimate

    Use aligned excess returns, measure covariance and market variance in the same units, and compute their ratio.

  2. Apply

    Multiply beta by the expected market premium for the chosen horizon and add the corresponding safe rate once.

  3. Diagnose

    Compare the model prediction with separately measured performance, quantify estimation uncertainty and test whether an alternative benchmark changes the conclusion.

Worked case

Two securities with different risk profiles

A market has annual volatility 20%, expected premium 5%, and a 2% safe rate. Share X has volatility 30% and correlation 0.40 with the market. Share Y has volatility 20% and correlation 0.90.

Working

  1. Beta X = 0.40 × 0.30/0.20 = 0.60. Beta Y = 0.90 × 0.20/0.20 = 0.90. The more volatile X has the lower market exposure.
  2. Expected annual returns under CAPM are 2% + 0.60 × 5% = 5% for X and 2% + 0.90 × 5% = 6.5% for Y.
  3. A 40/60 value-weighted mix of X and Y has beta 0.4 × 0.6 + 0.6 × 0.9 = 0.78 and expected return 5.9%. Its total volatility would additionally require the covariance between X and Y.

Interpretation

The calculation separates the information needed for systematic risk from the information needed for total risk, a useful distinction when an exam supplies an incomplete covariance table.

A common mistake

Calling realized performance above the predicted expected return proof of a mispriced share.

How to correct it

Distinguish expected reward, realized noise and estimated alpha. Specify the benchmark and uncertainty before making an inference about pricing.

Quick check
A second security has a beta of 0.5 with those same annual inputs. What does CAPM imply?

The calculation is 3% + 0.5 × 4% = 5%. Only the market premium is scaled by beta.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A defensive asset has beta −0.25 and annual volatility 12%. The safe rate is 3% and the expected market premium is 6%.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find its expected return and explain why it may be held.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    A report gives its share of market risk as −3% by multiplying beta by asset volatility. Diagnose the error.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

CAPM gives 3% − 0.25 × 6% = 1.5%. Its negative systematic exposure can offset damage elsewhere when market returns fall, so investors may pay for insurance.

Part B 2 marks

Beta already measures covariance relative to market variance. CAPM scales it by the market risk premium to get expected excess return. Total asset volatility is relevant to a different question.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A highly levered listed peer has equity beta 1.6. Can its cost of equity be transferred unchanged to a project funded with less debt?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would start from comparable operating risk, then explicitly adjust for financing. Under the stated low-debt-beta and tax assumptions I unlever 1.6 to 0.914 and relever it to 1.189, yielding roughly 8.94% cost of equity. I would check market-value ratios, the reliability of the estimated beta and business comparability. I would also discount equity cash flows at this equity rate and use an appropriate enterprise rate for operating cash flows.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if the peer’s debt trades like a risky cyclical asset?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Debt investors then absorb part of systematic operating risk. Treating their beta as zero misallocates risk between claims. I would estimate or bound debt beta, select a consistent tax-shield and debt policy model, and recover the operating beta from the market-value-weighted claims before applying target leverage.
