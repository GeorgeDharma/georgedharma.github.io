The risk-free asset

Combining a risky portfolio with borrowing or lending changes total risk. Real borrowing constraints complicate the textbook separation result.

The starting point

To vary risk without picking an entirely new set of shares, an investor can divide money between a risky fund and an asset that pays a known amount at the chosen horizon. A short government bill held to maturity can approximate that second position in its own currency. Inflation and exchange rates may still affect what its proceeds buy.

With a constant risk-free rate, portfolio expected return is the safe rate plus the risky weight times the fund’s expected excess return. Volatility scales with the absolute risky weight. A risky weight above 100% involves borrowing. If borrowing costs more than lending earns, the straight-line textbook trade-off changes.

Theory and method

Capital market line and Sharpe ratio

Tangency portfolio · Two-fund separation with cash

Fund B has the higher Sharpe ratio. Is it always preferable to fund A for an investor who wants 15% volatility?

A committee can buy fund A, expected to earn 8% with volatility 15%, or fund B, expected to earn 6% with volatility 8%. Cash earns 2%. B provides more excess expected return per unit of risk under deposit funding, but reaching 15% volatility with B requires borrowing. The achievable trade-off depends on the actual price of that borrowing. A straight line through the cash point can turn into a different slope precisely where the client wants to invest.

How the result is built

SR = (μ − rL)/σ; μlevered = yμB − (y − 1)rB

SR is the Sharpe ratio, μ is a fund’s annual expected return, σ its annual volatility, and rL the deposit rate. y is the amount invested in B per euro of equity, μB its expected return, and rB the annual borrowing rate for the excess.

  1. Sharpe ratios are (8 − 2)/15 = 0.40 for A and (6 − 2)/8 = 0.50 for B, with figures expressed consistently as percentages.
  2. To reach A’s 15% risk, B needs y = 15/8 = 1.875, so each euro of equity borrows €0.875.
  3. At 2% funding B would earn 9.5% on equity. At 4% borrowing it earns 1.875 × 6% − 0.875 × 4% = 7.75%, less than A’s 8% at equal modeled risk.

When the reasoning holds

  • The target uses positive linear exposure and assumes financing adds no modeled variance. Margin liquidation or variable financing can add risk.
  • Calling a line the capital market line relies on the market portfolio as the tangency portfolio under equilibrium assumptions. A line for any particular risky fund is a capital allocation line.

Terms in this lesson
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Margin
In business accounts, profit expressed relative to sales. In derivatives trading, margin is collateral required to support a position.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
How to read the notation
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.

All notation examples

First example

With a safe rate of 2%, a fund expected to earn 8%, and 60% in the fund, expected return is 2% + 0.60 × 6% = 5.6%. If fund volatility is 15%, portfolio volatility is 9%.

Separation of the risky choice from scale

Suppose all investors can lend or borrow at one known rate and agree on expected returns and covariance. A blend of cash and a risky portfolio lies on a straight line in expected-return versus volatility space. Its slope is the risky portfolio’s excess expected return per unit of volatility, its Sharpe ratio.

The line with the highest feasible slope touches the risky frontier at a tangency portfolio. Within this textbook setting, investors can share that risky mix but hold different amounts of it. A cautious investor lends the balance. An aggressive investor can borrow to fund additional risky holdings.

The safe asset is horizon specific

A nominal repayment in one currency can be known at its maturity. If the investor must sell a long bond tomorrow, its price tomorrow is unknown. Even a perfectly matched nominal payment can be risky in real purchasing power or in the investor’s home currency.

If the institution lends at one rate and borrows at a higher one, the attainable line changes slope for leveraged positions. Borrowing limits, margin calls and collateral requirements can make some points unreachable. A single tangency solution need not govern every investor facing different borrowing terms.

Leverage scales losses as well as expected gains

For risky weight y, the cash position has weight 1 − y. When y exceeds one, that cash weight is negative. Equity risk is magnified because the borrowed amount is due even if the fund performs badly. The liquidity needed to avoid forced selling may differ from a model’s predicted annual volatility.

A return on invested capital and return on the investor’s own equity use different denominators. Write both amounts explicitly when comparing partially financed positions, and be clear whether borrowing must be repaid before or after a margin event.

Method

Choose the scale

  1. Select the risky opportunity

    Compare expected excess return per unit of risk with the funding terms realistically available for the intended direction.

  2. Size

    Use the target volatility divided by fund volatility for a positive risky allocation, then identify whether the residual is lending or borrowing.

  3. Stress

    Recalculate profit and equity after financing costs under both favorable and adverse risky returns. Test any margin or leverage constraint.

Worked case

A mandate with two funding rates

A €1m investor can hold a risky fund with 8% expected return and 12% volatility. Deposits earn 2%, borrowing costs 5%, and borrowing may not exceed €300,000. The target volatility is 15%.

Working

  1. Target risky weight is 15%/12% = 1.25. Buy €1.25m of the fund and borrow €250,000, within the limit. Expected fund earnings of €100,000 less interest of €12,500 give 8.75% expected equity return.
  2. If borrowing had been available at 2%, the result would have been 9.5%. Carrying over the lending rate overstates the realistic expected equity gain by €7,500.
  3. A realized fund loss of 10% leaves a €125,000 investment loss plus €12,500 interest, reducing the investor’s equity by 13.75% before any other charges.

Interpretation

The highest fund weight allowed is 1.30, corresponding to 15.6% modeled volatility. Targets beyond that ceiling require a change to the investment opportunity or the mandate.

A common mistake

Using the Sharpe ratio of an unlevered fund and assuming every point on its extended line is investable.

How to correct it

Calculate the borrowing rate, borrowing cap and collateral requirements on the actual leveraged section of the opportunity set.

Quick check
Someone invests €120 in this fund using €100 of savings and €20 borrowed at 4%. What annual return is expected on the savings?

Expected fund earnings are €9.60. After €0.80 of interest on €20 of borrowing, €8.80 remains on €100 of equity.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A fund offers 9% expected return at 18% volatility, the deposit rate is 3%, and an investor requires 12% volatility.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find the positive risky weight and expected return.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    If the horizon is one day, why might a ten-year government bond fail as the cash substitute?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

The risky weight is 12/18 = two thirds. The other third earns 3%. Expected return is (2/3) × 9% + (1/3) × 3% = 7%.

Part B 2 marks

Its maturity payment may be known in nominal currency, but its sale price tomorrow changes with market rates. A one-day liquidity need introduces price risk absent from the classroom safe asset.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Fund B has the higher Sharpe ratio. Is it always preferable to fund A for an investor who wants 15% volatility?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would compare attainable positions at the client’s target. Fund B’s unlevered Sharpe ratio is higher using the 2% reference, but 15% volatility requires 87.5 cents of borrowing per euro of equity. At a 4% borrowing rate the scaled B earns 7.75% versus A’s 8%. I would check the client’s borrowing limit, funding stability and margin liquidity before choosing either.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What is the borrowing rate at which the two positions have the same expected return?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Set 1.875 × 6% − 0.875rB equal to 8%. The break-even borrowing rate is about 3.7143%. Above this, A offers the higher expected return at the specified risk, provided the initial return and volatility estimates remain valid.
