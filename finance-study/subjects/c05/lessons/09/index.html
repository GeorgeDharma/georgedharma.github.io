Behavioural finance

Examine overconfidence, loss aversion and limits to arbitrage. Ask what distinctive evidence would support a behavioural account of a price pattern.

The starting point

An investor who feels a loss especially strongly might hold a losing share in the hope of returning to the purchase price. Another might trade excessively after a short run of wins. Behavioural finance turns such ideas into testable predictions about choices, prices and trading activity.

Similar observations can have different explanations. Taxes, liquidity needs or changing expected cash flows could also affect a sale. Correcting a suspected pricing error requires capital, time and an instrument that tracks it. Constraints on short selling and client withdrawals can leave mispricing in place even when informed traders recognize it.

Theory and method

Kahneman–Tversky prospect theory

Reference dependence · Loss aversion · Disposition effect

Can identical final wealth lotteries produce different choices when they are described relative to different reference points?

A client deciding whether to lock in a gain may prefer certainty, yet might gamble to avoid booking a similar loss. Prospect theory models outcomes as changes relative to a reference point and allows different curvature on each side. A loss can also receive greater weight in value than an equally sized gain. These distinct mechanisms generate testable choices. A brokerage record of selling winners alone does not identify which mechanism caused a given trade.

How the result is built

v(x) = √x for x ≥ 0, and v(x) = −2√(−x) for x < 0

x is a euro change relative to a defined reference and v is an illustrative value score. The multiplier 2 represents loss aversion in this example. Equal objective probabilities are used directly to isolate the value function from probability weighting.

  1. A certain €25 gain has value 5. A half chance of €50 and half chance of zero gives mean value about 3.54, producing preference for the certain gain.
  2. A certain €25 loss has value −10. A half chance of losing €50 and half chance of zero gives mean value about −7.07, producing preference for the loss gamble.
  3. A mixed 50–50 gamble gaining or losing €25 has mean value (5 − 10)/2 = −2.5, illustrating loss aversion across the reference point.

When the reasoning holds

  • The chosen reference and illustrative parameters are specified, not estimates for every client. Changing the reference can shift outcomes across the two regions.
  • Actual prospect theory can transform probabilities. Holding them fixed here isolates curvature and the loss multiplier for an interpretable demonstration.
Terms in this lesson
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Percentage point
The difference between two percentages. A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises one percentage point.
How to read the notation
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.

All notation examples

First example

An investor buys two shares at €50 each. One reaches €60 and the other €40. Selling only the winner realizes a gain and defers admitting a loss. To test the disposition effect, a researcher compares sale propensities across many holdings while accounting for taxes and other reasons to trade.

Give a behavioural story a measurable prediction

The disposition effect is a tendency to realize gains more readily than losses relative to a reference price, often the purchase price. To test it, count opportunities to realize gains and losses as well as the sales made. A bull market produces many winning holdings even for someone choosing sales at random.

Loss aversion describes an asymmetric response to comparable gains and losses around a reference point. It does not alone predict every selling decision because beliefs about future returns matter. State a specific comparison that would distinguish reluctance to realize a loss from sensible expectations of recovery.

Consider competing explanations

A household can sell winners to rebalance a concentrated portfolio. Tax rules can favor realizing losses near a reporting deadline. Very small or illiquid losing positions may be disproportionately expensive to sell. Portfolio cash needs may force sales unrelated to any reference point.

Compare similar opportunities within an account or across comparable securities and dates. Observe subsequent performance without assuming investors knew it in advance. Ask whether the pattern survives plausible differences in costs, taxes, rebalancing and desired portfolio weights.

Why informed traders may not immediately erase mispricing

An arbitrageur can recognize a valuation discrepancy but be unable to short the expensive asset, borrow it cheaply or hold the trade until convergence. The assets may have related rather than identical payoffs. Interim losses can provoke client withdrawals before prices correct.

A behavioral explanation of prices requires both a reason for biased demand and a reason informed capital cannot fully offset it. A pattern in individual trading is evidence about investors’ choices. Its effect on aggregate prices requires additional evidence of scale and limited substitution.

Method

Design an account-level test

  1. Opportunity set

    For each sale date, determine which other holdings could have been sold at gains and which at losses using a clearly defined reference.

  2. Normalize

    Divide realized gains by all available gain opportunities and realized losses by all available loss opportunities under the same observation rule.

  3. Discriminate

    Check competing economic motives and pre-specify what evidence would weaken each explanation. Distinguish behaviour, welfare and market pricing.

Worked case

Two sale frequencies from one account panel

On observed sale dates a sample records 36 realized gains and 144 unrealized winning opportunities. It records 20 realized losses and 180 unrealized losing opportunities. Assume consistent classification and equal-quality price information.

Working

  1. The proportion of gains realized is 36/(36 + 144) = 20%. The proportion of losses realized is 20/(20 + 180) = 10%. The difference is 10 percentage points.
  2. A count-only comparison, 36 versus 20, disregards how often each sale was possible. Comparing the normalized rates better isolates selling preference, though it does not establish motivation.
  3. Check whether sales corrected unusually large weights, whether losing holdings carried extra spreads, or whether different tax incentives apply around the measurement date.

Interpretation

The price paid for a share has no direct effect on its future operating cash flows. It can nevertheless influence taxes, beliefs and a trader’s psychological reference point. Distinguishing these pathways sharpens the hypothesis.

A common mistake

Explaining a price reversal with hindsight by saying that investors must have been irrational.

How to correct it

Specify the decision bias, its observable timing, a competing risk or information explanation, and evidence of limits preventing an immediate correction.

Quick check
Why might a fund hesitate to short an apparently overpriced share even with a sound estimate of long-term value?

Funding and time horizons matter. Temporary further increases can trigger margin calls or withdrawals before the fund’s valuation is borne out.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A report observes 15 realized gains among 60 gain opportunities and 18 realized losses among 120 loss opportunities.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Which type is realized more often relative to opportunity and by how much?

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Name one observation that would weaken a pure reference-price explanation.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Gains are realized in 15/60 = 25% of opportunities and losses in 18/120 = 15%. Gains are sold 10 percentage points more frequently despite a lower raw sale count.

Part B 2 marks

If nearly all winner sales restore pre-specified portfolio weights after appreciation and the difference disappears among otherwise comparable holdings, rebalancing becomes a credible account. Tax timing or cost differences can also be tested with suitable data.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Can identical final wealth lotteries produce different choices when they are described relative to different reference points?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would ask which reference point the investor uses, then separate curvature from loss aversion. In the specified example the investor prefers a certain €25 gain yet prefers gambling over a certain €25 loss. A mixed symmetric gamble is unattractive because the €25 loss carries twice the value magnitude. To explain actual sales I would test for taxes, rebalancing needs, transaction costs and changed fundamentals.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If the loss multiplier fell from two to one while the square-root curvature stayed fixed, which prediction changes?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The symmetric mixed gamble becomes neutral since +5 and −5 average to zero. Preferences within the all-gain and all-loss comparisons remain in the same directions, because scaling both possible losses by the same positive constant preserves their ordering.
