Holding-period returns

Include income as well as price movement. Convert all results to the same currency and distinguish arithmetic averages from compounded growth.

The starting point

When you buy a share, you can receive cash while you own it and cash when you sell it. The holding-period return adds those receipts, subtracts your original payment and divides the gain by that payment. Use the same currency for every amount. For a foreign dividend paid midway, use its exchange rate when received or specify how you reinvest it.

Over several periods, the closing balance becomes the next opening balance. Multiply each period’s growth factor, such as 1.10 for a 10% return. An arithmetic mean answers what a typical single period returned. A compound growth rate answers what a constant rate would have done to the investment.

Theory and method

Modified Dietz return

Time-weighted return · Money-weighted return

An external contribution arrived halfway through the month and the portfolio was never valued that day. Which return can you defend?

An investment account begins with €100, receives €50 midway and closes at €160. The asset manager earned €10 after removing the owner’s contribution. The first €100 worked for the entire month and the additional €50 for roughly half of it. Modified Dietz estimates return by dividing the monetary gain by time-weighted capital. A valuation immediately before the transfer would let the analyst calculate the actual linked subperiod return without estimating the exposure through time.

How the result is built

rᴅ = (V₁ − V₀ − ΣCᵢ)/(V₀ + ΣwᵢCᵢ)

rᴅ is the period’s Modified Dietz return. V₀ and V₁ are opening and closing euro values. Cᵢ is an external euro transfer, positive for money added, and wᵢ is the fraction of the evaluation period remaining after transfer i. Σ sums across transfers.

  1. Remove €50 of external funding from the €60 increase in value, leaving €10 of gain attributable to the portfolio.
  2. Weight the midmonth transfer by one half to get €100 + 0.5 × €50 = €125 of estimated return-bearing capital.
  3. The estimate is €10/€125 = 8%. If the portfolio had been worth €110 just before the flow, precise linked return would instead be 1.10 × 160/160 − 1 = 10%.

When the reasoning holds

  • Transfer dates and signs must be correct. A distribution received within the portfolio forms investment income and should not be misclassified as an external deposit.
  • The approximation is sensitive to large flows and volatile interim performance. A denominator near zero after withdrawals can make it unstable.
Terms in this lesson
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
Discount rate
Rate used to express a future payment in today’s money, reflecting time and the relevant risk.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
How to read the notation
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.

All notation examples

First example

A share bought for €50 pays €2 and sells for €53. Its total return is (€53 + €2 − €50)/€50 = 10%. If the full €55 then loses 10%, the final balance is €49.50, a 1% loss over the two periods.

Define the investment boundary

A percentage return compares gain with the capital exposed to earn it. Record all shares, cash inside the account, and costs included in the calculation. A dividend is investment income. An owner’s additional deposit is new capital. Calling both investment income can make an ordinary strategy appear extraordinarily profitable.

For an individual security with income paid at the end, divide final price plus income by starting price and subtract one. If income arrives earlier, decide what happens to it. A total-return index generally states a reinvestment convention. Cash left idle can produce a different terminal wealth.

Separate the manager from the investor

Time-weighted return breaks the history just before external deposits or withdrawals. Calculate each subperiod return and multiply the growth factors. The result measures the performance of a unit following the chosen strategy independent of the amount the client placed with the manager at different dates.

A money-weighted return solves for the single discount rate that makes the dated contributions and receipts balance. It answers what happened to the investor’s money. Large contributions before a poor period have a bigger influence. Different answers can both be correct because the economic questions differ.

Match return and horizon

An annualized compound rate converts the entire period’s wealth change to an equivalent constant yearly rate. It assumes exponential compounding for the conversion. It cannot tell you how evenly wealth grew. A three-month result repeated four times algebraically is a scenario rather than evidence of what later quarters will deliver.

For purchasing power, divide the nominal growth factor by the matching price-index growth factor, then subtract one. A five-percentage-point difference between nominal return and inflation is only an approximation to the real return.

Method

Audit a performance claim

  1. Classify

    Mark cash as investment income, an expense or an external transfer. Specify currency and the beginning and ending dates.

  2. Link

    Divide at every external cash flow, calculate period growth on the funds actually present, and chain those factors.

  3. Interpret

    Compute money-weighted or inflation-adjusted return if that matches the investor’s question. Label annualization and reinvestment assumptions.

Worked case

A deposit arrives before a bad year

A fund starts with €100, rises to €120 over year one, then receives a €100 client deposit. The balance loses 10% in year two and all funds are redeemed at the end. There are no fees or taxes.

Working

  1. End wealth is (€120 + €100) × 0.90 = €198. Time-weighted total return is 1.20 × 0.90 − 1 = 8%, equivalent to about 3.92% per year.
  2. The client contributed €200 in total and received €198. The annual money-weighted rate solves €100(1 + r)² + €100(1 + r) = €198, giving about −0.67%.
  3. More of the client’s capital experienced the down year. Classifying the second contribution as profit would instead inflate the first investment’s performance.

Interpretation

The client’s euro loss and the strategy’s positive time-weighted return answer different reporting questions. Stating the timing of cash makes both results reproducible.

A common mistake

Averaging a 20% gain and a 10% loss and reporting 5% annual compound growth.

How to correct it

Chain the 1.20 and 0.90 growth factors and take the square root for the annual geometric rate over two years.

Quick check
After a 20% fall, what gain restores the initial balance?

€100 becomes €80. Restoring €20 requires €20/€80 = 25% because the second return uses a smaller starting balance.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A portfolio worth €80 rises to €100, then an investor withdraws €20. The remaining capital rises to €88. Prices in the economy rise 10% across the whole interval.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find the total time-weighted nominal return.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Calculate the matching real return and describe what it measures.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

First return is 25%. Second return is €88/€80 − 1 = 10%. Total growth is 1.25 × 1.10 = 1.375, a 37.5% return.

Part B 2 marks

1.375/1.10 − 1 = 25%. A unit invested through the strategy gained 25% in purchasing-power terms under the chosen price index and reinvestment convention.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

An external contribution arrived halfway through the month and the portfolio was never valued that day. Which return can you defend?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would disclose an 8% Modified Dietz estimate, the midpoint assumption and the missing valuation. I could establish the €10 gain directly, but I could not establish an exact time-weighted result because returns before and after the contribution may differ. For manager evaluation I would request valuations at large transfers. For the client’s personal experience I would also calculate the money-weighted return from dated cash flows.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if the portfolio instead fell to €90 just before receiving €50 and still ended at €160?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The exact linked return would be (90/100) × (160/140) − 1 ≈ 2.86%. Modified Dietz remains 8% on the sparse opening, closing and flow record. Strongly different subperiod returns and the large transfer explain the approximation error.
