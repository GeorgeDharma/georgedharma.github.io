Efficiency and anomalies

A test of excess returns also tests a benchmark model. An apparent anomaly may reflect a missing risk factor, a bias or a cost of trading.

The starting point

Suppose someone discovers that a group of cheap-looking shares has historically beaten the market. To ask whether this contradicts efficient pricing, specify which information was available when the trades could have been placed. A model is also needed to say what return would have been reasonable compensation for risk.

This is the joint-hypothesis problem. Apparent excess performance can arise from mispricing, from the benchmark omitting a risk, or from how the study was built. Testing on periods held aside in advance, keeping firms that later failed, and deducting costs make the claim more informative.

Theory and method

Fama’s joint-hypothesis problem and event studies

Market model · Cumulative abnormal return

A share rises on the day of a public announcement. How would you test whether investors could earn a risk-adjusted profit after the announcement became available?

A positive announcement return can be the market’s immediate incorporation of news. A researcher needs a timestamp, a defined trading strategy and a model of normal performance before examining whether a delayed adjustment remains. Comparing the stock with its market sensitivity yields an abnormal-return series. Sampling uncertainty, other simultaneous announcements and a benchmark fitted using the same event can change the inference. The test therefore evaluates both informational efficiency and the model used to measure normal return.

How the result is built

ARₜ = Rₜ − (α + βMₜ); CAR = ΣARₜ

On trading day t, Rₜ is the stock return, Mₜ the benchmark return, α the daily normal intercept, and β the estimated sensitivity from a separate pre-event period. ARₜ is abnormal return and CAR the sum over the specified short event window.

  1. Use α = 0 and β = 1.2. For benchmark returns of +0.5%, +1.0% and −0.5% the fitted stock returns are +0.6%, +1.2% and −0.6%.
  2. Actual returns +1.2%, +2.4% and −0.6% imply abnormal returns +0.6%, +1.2% and zero, so three-day CAR = 1.8 percentage points.
  3. If daily residual volatility is 1% and the residuals are independent with estimation error ignored, the three-day standard error is √3 × 1% ≈ 1.73%, giving a statistic near 1.04.

When the reasoning holds

  • Estimate α and β using pre-event data free of announcement contamination. A sector-specific concurrent shock can make a broad market model inadequate.
  • The simple standard error assumes stable variance and independent daily residuals. Market-wide event clustering, parameter error and multiple testing need appropriate inference.

Terms in this lesson
Benchmark
A reference chosen in advance for comparison, such as a market index or an equal-weight portfolio.
Risk factor
A common source of variation in returns, such as market movements or a change in interest rates.
Percentage point
The difference between two percentages. A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises one percentage point.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Regression
A way of estimating how an outcome varies with one or more observed inputs.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
First example

A study reports 3 percentage points of annual outperformance. A risk adjustment accounts for 1.5 points and realistic trading costs absorb another point. The remaining 0.5 point must be assessed against its statistical uncertainty and the variety of rules originally tried.

Specify what efficiency would predict

An informationally efficient price reflects a specified information set in a way that leaves no systematic abnormal earning opportunity after allowing for the costs and risks of the test. The word abnormal requires an expected-return benchmark. Historical price information, public disclosures and private knowledge represent different information sets.

A price can move sharply after unexpected public news in an efficient market. The relevant questions concern what was known before the announcement, how quickly it entered the price and whether an implementable strategy could have earned a predictable risk-adjusted gain afterwards.

Reconstruct the research clock

Create an information date, signal date, execution date and evaluation date. Financial statements are often published weeks after their accounting period ends. Constituent lists rewritten using today’s survivors and revised macroeconomic data can leak later information into a historical simulation.

Multiple testing compounds the problem. Trying thousands of signals and reporting the top one creates apparent success even in random data. Lock the rule, keep a genuinely untouched period and compare results with implementation costs, turnover and realistic liquidity in the securities traded.

Separate risk compensation from mispricing

A small-company strategy might beat a broad index because its returns fall especially far in recessions. Its positive average excess return then has a risk interpretation. Alternatively, prices might respond slowly to news. To distinguish them, describe an observation that would be more likely under one mechanism than another.

A factor-adjusted regression can supply a risk benchmark, but the factor model itself is estimated and debatable. Even a statistically unusual alpha is conditional on the model and sample. Trading profits are also net of the spread, market impact, borrow costs and fees.

Method

Audit a published anomaly

  1. Information

    Mark when each input was actually accessible and price trades at the first feasible execution following that access.

  2. Benchmark

    Use economically relevant exposures and a comparison strategy facing similar implementation constraints. Preserve delistings and failed firms.

  3. Inference

    Show sampling uncertainty and tests on untouched data. Reconcile gross, risk-adjusted and net results with an explicit convention for costs.

Worked case

A profitable signal on paper

A backtest reports annual gross excess performance of 4.8 percentage points. Under its factor model, risk exposures account for 2.1 points. The strategy trades €6 of one-way gross notional per €1 of portfolio equity per year, and cost is 0.30% per euro actually traded. Fees are 0.40% of equity.

Working

  1. Annual execution drag is 6 × 0.30% = 1.80%. Model-adjusted net excess is 4.80 − 2.10 − 1.80 − 0.40 = 0.50 percentage point.
  2. If the standard error of the annualized net estimate is 0.70 point under an appropriate dependence adjustment, the estimate is only about 0.71 standard errors above zero.
  3. If the signal used year-end accounts before their public release, moving trading to the true publication date may change every component. Simply adding another transaction-cost penalty would not correct the information error.

Interpretation

The contribution of risk adjustment and execution is observable only relative to stated conventions. In particular, annual turnover definitions can double-count trades if the cost convention does not match.

The three-day cumulative abnormal return is +0.58%. The stock fell on day 0 and still outperformed its predicted return. To make a statistical claim, estimate the uncertainty in that gap and investigate other simultaneous news. One window is evidence of an association.

A common mistake

Calling a simulated strategy successful because its winner-only average return exceeds a broad index.

How to correct it

Keep firms that subsequently disappeared, use information available at execution and compare against a benchmark matching the strategy’s distinct exposures.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A rule requires a €2m buy and €2m sale per year on €1m equity. Gross return above its risk benchmark is 2%. One-way cost is 0.35% and annual fees are 0.2%.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate estimated net abnormal return.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Why would repeatedly choosing the best rule on the same period weaken an inference about that 0.4%?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

€4m actually trades, costing €14,000 or 1.4% of equity. After fees, the estimated net abnormal return is 2% − 1.4% − 0.2% = 0.4%.

Part B 2 marks

Selection rewards favorable noise. Freeze the rule before testing it on unused data, report alternatives tried and assess uncertainty allowing for the search. A small estimated excess can disappear outside the design sample.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A share rises on the day of a public announcement. How would you test whether investors could earn a risk-adjusted profit after the announcement became available?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would timestamp when the news could have been read and when the order could have filled. The three-day modeled gain is 1.8 points, but the later day has zero abnormal return and the rough statistic is only about one. I would examine a separately specified post-release window, trading costs and competing industry news before claiming delayed processing or a profitable public rule.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Suppose residuals across days had pairwise correlation 0.5 with the same daily volatility. What happens to the three-day uncertainty?

Compare with the follow-up answer

There are three daily variances and three distinct covariance pairs counted twice. Total variance is 3 × 0.01² + 6 × 0.5 × 0.01² = 0.0006. Standard error rises to about 2.45%, reducing the statistic to roughly 0.73.
