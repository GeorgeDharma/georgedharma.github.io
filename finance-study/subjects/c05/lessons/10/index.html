International portfolios

Currency movements, country concentration and common shocks affect diversification. Estimate performance in the investor’s spending currency.

The starting point

An investor who pays expenses in euros ultimately cares about euros. A foreign holding has two moving parts, the local investment value and the euro value of one unit of its currency. Multiply the two growth factors to calculate the return measured in euros.

Geographic variety also needs examination. Firms listed in different countries may sell to the same customers or share financing risks. Currency hedges can reduce one source of variation, though hedge costs, contract dates and the uncertain size of the holding affect the result.

Theory and method

Minimum-variance currency overlay

Hedge ratio · Natural currency hedge

Why could a euro investor’s risk-minimizing dollar hedge be smaller than the initial dollar market value of the foreign shares?

Dollar weakness may coincide with strong local equity performance, leaving the euro investor partly protected by the business itself. Selling the initial dollar amount forward eliminates one source of movement but can remove this natural offset. The investor can instead estimate how the complete euro value responds to currency fluctuations over the hedge horizon, then choose a forward amount that minimizes measured variance. The chosen liability currency and hedge objective determine what risk is being reduced.

How the result is built

h* = Cov(Rᵤ,F)/Var(F); Rₕ = Rᵤ − hF

Rᵤ is the unhedged home-currency portfolio return, F is the long foreign-currency forward gain per unit of home-equivalent exposure expressed as a return, h is the size of the short forward relative to capital, Rₕ is hedged return, and Cov and Var are covariance and variance.

  1. Expand Var(Rᵤ − hF) to Var(Rᵤ) − 2hCov(Rᵤ,F) + h²Var(F). Differentiate with respect to h and set the result to zero.
  2. Suppose euro portfolio volatility is 12%, forward return volatility 10%, and their correlation 0.50. The covariance is 0.12 × 0.10 × 0.50 = 0.006 and h* = 0.60.
  3. At h = 0.60 variance becomes 0.0144 − 2 × 0.60 × 0.006 + 0.60² × 0.01 = 0.0108, or about 10.39% volatility.

When the reasoning holds

  • The forward series must use the same horizon and units as portfolio returns. Spot changes can omit forward carry or roll effects when those are variable.
  • This is a minimum-variance overlay under stable estimates and no trading limits. Cash margin needs, hedging costs or liability-specific objectives can lead to a different hedge.
Terms in this lesson
Diversification
Combining exposures that do not always move together to reduce dependence on a single outcome.
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
Forward
A contract to exchange an agreed amount at a set price on a future date.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
First example

A Japanese equity investment gains 8% in yen. Over the same horizon one yen buys 5% fewer euros. A starting €100 becomes €100 × 1.08 × 0.95 = €102.60, giving a 2.6% euro return before costs.

Translate foreign wealth at each cash-flow date

State the exchange-rate quotation in words. If one dollar buys e euros, the home value of a dollar holding is its dollar amount times e. For a single foreign holding with reinvested income, its home-currency growth factor equals local growth times the currency growth factor. Reversing the quote reverses the division or multiplication required.

An interim foreign dividend converted immediately is exposed to its own exchange rate, not necessarily the final rate. When a study assumes local reinvestment, distinguish that convention from one where distributions are transferred home or held as foreign cash.

Define the currency hedge amount

A currency forward can lock in the home-currency proceeds of a specified foreign amount. Hedging the initial foreign value leaves the uncertain local investment gain or loss exposed. Rebalancing a hedge reduces the mismatch but adds cost and intermediate liquidity needs.

An apparent free benefit from forward points may disappear when funding differences are included. Compare the cost and payoff of the hedge at matched dates, currencies and collateral terms. A futures hedge can have cash calls while the equity position itself supplies no current cash.

Country labels and economic exposures

Two funds with different listing countries may both be dominated by the same exporters or benefit from the same global demand shocks. Look at revenue sources, borrowing currency and underlying industries. International diversification can lessen some domestic shocks while increasing currency or liquidity risks.

An investor’s liability benchmark matters. A French institution paying a future dollar obligation may naturally match part of that obligation with dollar assets. The same holding can increase risk relative to euro-only spending and reduce it relative to a dollar liability.

Method

Evaluate the home-currency position

  1. Translate

    List foreign amounts, initial and terminal spot rates, interim distributions and all contract rates using one clearly defined quotation.

  2. Hedge

    Calculate forward receipts and foreign currency left over or required at maturity. Compare hedged and unhedged home-currency outcomes.

  3. Interpret

    Attribute asset and currency movements including their cross-product. Examine liability matching, common industry exposures and liquidity under stress.

Worked case

A hedge leaves the investment gain open

A euro investor buys US$100,000 of foreign shares when one dollar buys €0.90. The investor sells US$100,000 one year forward at €0.91 per dollar. The holding grows to US$112,000, and spot ends at €0.80. Ignore costs and intermediate income.

Working

  1. Initial cost is €90,000. Without a hedge the terminal euro value is US$112,000 × €0.80 = €89,600, a loss of €400 or about 0.44%.
  2. Delivering US$100,000 into the forward yields €91,000. The remaining US$12,000 converts to €9,600 at spot. Total terminal wealth is €100,600, giving an 11.78% return.
  3. If the holding had fallen to US$85,000, the investor would need to purchase the US$15,000 shortfall at terminal spot to meet the fixed delivery obligation.

Interpretation

The hedge protects a specified dollar amount. It changes the distribution of euro results, while asset returns, forecast errors and the uncertain terminal hedge ratio still matter.

A common mistake

Adding local return and currency return as if their bases never change.

How to correct it

Multiply growth factors when the local asset and currency move together over the same period. Convert separate cash flows on their own dates.

Quick check
A foreign share falls 10% locally and its currency gains 10% against the euro. What is the unhedged euro return?

For each initial euro the ending value is 0.90 × 1.10 = 0.99 euros. The exchange-rate gain applies to the reduced local holding.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

An asset bought for 100 foreign units becomes 90 units. One foreign unit initially costs €2 and finally €2.30. A forward sells 100 units at €2.05 each at maturity.

  1. Part A · 2 marks

    Compute the unhedged euro return.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Compute the hedged return and explain the difference.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 2 marks

Initial cost is €200 and terminal value is 90 × €2.30 = €207, giving +3.5% despite a 10% local decline.

Part B 3 marks

Buy 10 foreign units for €23 to deliver 100 under the forward, which pays €205. Net terminal value is €182, a −9% return. The fixed forward removes the benefit of the foreign currency gaining value in euros on the original principal, with the shortfall purchased at the higher spot rate.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why could a euro investor’s risk-minimizing dollar hedge be smaller than the initial dollar market value of the foreign shares?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would estimate the currency sensitivity of the full euro portfolio, including its underlying businesses, and specify the client’s liability currency. Here the covariance divided by forward variance gives a 60% short hedge and about 10.39% forecast volatility. A 100% short against the opening notional need not minimize total euro risk, especially if the share prices and currency already offset one another.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if the client must pay a known US$1m in a year and already expects US$1m of asset proceeds then?

Compare with the follow-up answer

I would recast the objective around the dollar liability and its date. Matched dollar asset proceeds can fund that payment directly. Selling them forward for euros could reopen dollar funding risk, even if the trade reduced volatility of the stand-alone euro asset value.
