Arbitrage and factors

A portfolio can carry several common exposures. Factor models describe variation in returns and can be tested as explanations for average returns.

The starting point

Companies can react to more than one common influence. A factor model describes a return using sensitivities, called loadings, to shared movements such as a broad stock-market return or a traded portfolio representing a particular style. The security also has a part left unexplained by the selected factors.

For a realized-return calculation, multiply each loading by the matching realized factor return and add the contributions. A pricing model makes a further claim about expected rewards for those exposures. A large residual over one month could be company news. Persistent residuals require a test against uncertainty, alternative factors and realistic trading costs.

Theory and method

Ross’s arbitrage pricing theory

Factor-mimicking portfolio · Diversifiable residual risk

If two portfolios have equal factor exposures but different expected returns, have you found a riskless arbitrage that can be traded tomorrow?

An analyst finds several pairs of diversified-looking funds with identical estimated sensitivities to a market and a credit factor. Buying the apparently cheap member and selling the rich one leaves an expected spread after matching factors. Ross’s model explains why such persistent spreads face pressure in large diversified markets. A finite trade can still lose money through idiosyncratic shocks, estimated loadings, correlated residuals or funding. Making the residual averaging explicit clarifies how close the trade is to the theory.

How the result is built

E[Rᵢ] − rf ≈ Σβᵢⱼλⱼ; σequal = σe/√N under independent residuals

Rᵢ is return of portfolio i, rf the safe return, βᵢⱼ exposure to factor j, λⱼ its expected premium, and Σ sums priced exposures. N is number of equally weighted factor-neutral trades, σe their common individual residual volatility, and σequal the combined residual volatility.

  1. Construct matched factor exposures and a financed long–short position. A residual expected return beyond factor premiums is the proposed pricing difference.
  2. For N independent zero-mean residuals of variance σe² and weight 1/N each, the combined variance is N × σe²/N² = σe²/N.
  3. At σe = 12%, four pairs leave 6% volatility, 16 leave 3% and 64 leave 1.5%. A 1.2% expected gross spread is still uncertain at each finite N.

When the reasoning holds

  • Residual independence or sufficiently weak dependence is needed for the simple averaging rate. Omitted common exposure creates a risk floor.
  • Matched factor loadings and available shorting, financing and collateral must hold for the implementable positions. A paper spread can be consumed by borrow and execution costs.
Terms in this lesson
Risk premium
Extra expected return for bearing risk, or the amount a risk-averse person would give up to replace a gamble with certainty, depending on context.
Regression
A way of estimating how an outcome varies with one or more observed inputs.
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
Settlement
The final transfer of cash or securities needed to complete a transaction.
First example

A fund’s market loading is 0.8 and its value-factor loading is 0.5. In one month the market excess return is 3% and the value-factor return is −2%. With zero intercept the factors explain 0.8 × 3% + 0.5 × (−2%) = 1.4% excess return.

A factor model has two jobs

A statistical factor model decomposes realized returns into exposures to shared movements and a residual specific to the security. A pricing model adds the claim that differences in average returns are explained by exposure to factors with risk premiums. A regression that explains daily movements well need not explain average returns.

Factors can be market-traded portfolio returns or surprises in macroeconomic variables. Their units matter. A coefficient on a one-percentage-point inflation surprise is different from a loading on a one-percent traded portfolio return. A macroeconomic shock has no obvious purchase price until a replicating or tracking strategy is defined.

The logic behind arbitrage pricing

Build two well-diversified portfolios with the same common exposures. If one offers permanently higher expected return without extra residual risk, buying it and shorting the other yields a positive expected spread with little modeled risk. This motivates linear relationships between expected returns and factor sensitivities.

A finite long–short trade can still carry residual risk, estimation error, financing costs and short-borrow risk. Positive expected spread is weaker than strict arbitrage, which requires a nonnegative payoff in every state and positive payoff in at least one possible state.

Constructing neutral exposure

If one fund has market loading 1.2 and another 0.8, equal money long and short leaves market loading 0.4. To isolate a manager’s nonmarket contribution, scale the hedge to match exposure. Adding a second factor usually requires another independently exposed instrument.

Track how hedge weights change the budget and funding. A long–short book with zero net purchase price can still require collateral and capital. Its return convention must specify whether results are divided by equity, gross exposure or another defined capital base.

Method

Separate fitted return from pricing error

  1. Measure

    Write each factor’s unit, estimated loading and realization or expected premium. Use realizations for a period’s attribution and premiums for a pricing calculation.

  2. Hedge

    Solve simultaneous exposure equations to offset the target loadings and check the residual or unmodeled positions.

  3. Evaluate

    Assess uncertainty, execution and funding. State whether the result is ex-post attribution, an expected pricing residual or an actual state-by-state arbitrage.

Worked case

Two hedges for two influences

A €1m long position has market loading 1.2 and industry loading 0.6. Per euro invested, market tracker M has loadings (1,0), and sector tracker I has loadings (0.5,1). Their loadings are assumed stable over the evaluation horizon.

Working

  1. Offset the industry exposure by shorting €0.6m of I. This also offsets €0.3m of market exposure. The remaining market exposure is €0.9m, removed by shorting €0.9m of M.
  2. Before hedging, factor moves of +2% market and −3% industry would contribute 1.2 × 2% + 0.6 × (−3%) = +0.6%, or €6,000, to the long position.
  3. The two hedge positions offset that €6,000 under the model. An unexpected company settlement can still move the security, and short borrow and collateral affect the attainable net gain.

Interpretation

The total short market value is €1.5m. Factor neutrality alone does not imply equal gross long and short positions or zero financing needs.

A common mistake

Interpreting a residual after two factors as a guaranteed gain.

How to correct it

Identify unmodeled influences and residual variance, test out of sample, and account for borrowing and implementation before attributing an apparent excess to skill.

Quick check
If the fund actually earns a 2.0% excess return, what is its unexplained part that month?

Observed minus fitted excess return gives 2.0% − 1.4% = +0.6 percentage points. One residual gives little evidence about lasting skill.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A €2m holding has loadings (0.9,0.4). A pure market tracker has (1,0), and a second tracker has (0.25,1).

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Size the two short positions to neutralize both factors.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    If company-specific news creates a loss of 1% on the original position while factors stay flat, what is the approximate hedged loss?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Industry dollar loading is €0.8m, so short €0.8m of the second tracker. This offsets €0.2m market loading. Original market loading €1.8m leaves €1.6m to short in the pure tracker.

Part B 2 marks

€2m × 1% = €20,000 before costs under the stated model. The factor hedges do not deliver a compensating payment for purely company-specific news.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

If two portfolios have equal factor exposures but different expected returns, have you found a riskless arbitrage that can be traded tomorrow?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would describe an apparent factor-adjusted pricing difference and work through replication and residual risk before using the word arbitrage. Ross’s argument gains force as independent residual risks are diversified across many trades. With sixteen such pairs I still have 3% volatility on a 1.2% expected spread, before costs. I would stress common residuals, verify factor matching and obtain actual short-borrow rates.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if every pair of residuals has correlation 0.25?

Compare with the follow-up answer

For equal volatility the portfolio variance is σe²[0.25 + 0.75/N]. With sixteen trades volatility is 12% × √0.296875 ≈ 6.54%. Even infinitely many pairs leave 6% volatility. The pattern suggests common risk missing from the original factor model.
