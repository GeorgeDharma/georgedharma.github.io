State prices

A payoff delivered in bad times may be especially valuable. A discount factor weights a payment by both when and in which state it arrives.

The starting point

A payment that arrives when income is scarce can be particularly valuable. A state price is the amount paid today for one unit of money in a particular future outcome and zero in the others. It combines the likelihood of that outcome, time discounting and the value investors attach to money in that outcome.

Once the state prices are known, multiply each possible payoff by its state price and sum. Two contracts with the same average payoff can have different prices because their money arrives in different circumstances. Adding all state prices gives the price today of one unit delivered for sure at that date.

Theory and method

Arrow–Debreu state prices and the stochastic discount factor

Fundamental pricing equation · Consumption-based risk premium

How can you show algebraically that a security paying poorly when marginal money is valuable needs a higher expected return?

A saver cares more about an extra euro when income is low. The stochastic discount factor weights future payoffs according to the state in which the euro arrives, as well as its date. Multiplying an outcome’s physical probability by its discount factor gives the price today of a euro conditional on that outcome. If a share’s payoff tends to be high when a euro has relatively low value, its owners require a larger average payoff to justify a euro invested today.

How the result is built

1 = E[mR]; E[R] − Rf = −Cov(m,R)/E[m]

R is the asset’s gross return per euro invested, m is the state-dependent discount factor, E is expectation under physical probabilities, Cov is covariance under those probabilities, and Rf is the gross certain return for the same horizon.

  1. A risk-free gross payment Rf meets 1 = Rf E[m], giving Rf = 1/E[m]. For a risky claim expand E[mR] into E[m]E[R] + Cov(m,R).
  2. In equally likely good and bad states, take m = 0.7 and 1.1, and R = 1.6 and 0.8. Weighted prices total (1.12 + 0.88)/2 = 1, and E[m] = 0.9.
  3. Expected R = 1.2 and Cov(m,R) = 1 − 0.9 × 1.2 = −0.08. Expected premium is 0.08/0.9 ≈ 8.89 percentage points over certain gross 1.1111.

When the reasoning holds

  • The returns and discount factor must refer to the same currency, horizon and information set. Mixing dates or numeraires can produce the wrong covariance.
  • A single admissible discount factor prices the traded claims considered. In incomplete markets prices may permit several such factors for outcomes outside the traded span.
Terms in this lesson
Discount factor
The price today of one unit of currency payable at a particular future date under specified risk assumptions.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
No-arbitrage
A pricing condition that rules out a feasible trade producing a certain gain for no net investment and with no risk of loss.
First example

Suppose €1 paid only in a downturn costs €0.60 today, while €1 paid only in a boom costs €0.35. A contract paying €10 in a downturn and €2 in a boom costs 10 × €0.60 + 2 × €0.35 = €6.70.

State prices from traded cash flows

Imagine writing a contract that pays one euro only if a specified future state occurs. The price today of this elementary claim is a state price. Any contract paying different amounts across those same states is a bundle of these claims. Its price is the sum of state prices multiplied by the corresponding cash flows.

If elementary claims do not trade directly, try to reproduce them with traded assets. With two states, a risk-free bond and a share with different payoffs across the states can span both outcomes. Two copies of securities with proportional payoffs add no new state coverage, so some claims may remain unpriced by replication.

Probabilities, discounting and insurance

Let the physical probability of a state be p and let m represent how much a euro in that state is worth today per unit of probability. The state price is p × m. A severe downturn may receive a high m because a euro is especially useful when other resources are scarce.

Adding all state prices gives the current price of a guaranteed euro. Dividing each state price by this sum creates pricing probabilities that sum to one. Those weights price a claim when paired with risk-free discounting. They need not equal the frequencies a forecaster assigns to actual events.

Positivity and a price consistency test

If someone can acquire a claim at zero or negative cost that pays a nonnegative amount in every state and a positive amount in a genuinely possible state, unrestricted trading permits an arbitrage. Under the usual no-arbitrage assumptions relevant possible states can be assigned strictly positive weights.

If a market price implies a negative state price in a complete two-state setup, identify a replicating position before claiming a usable trade. Bid–ask spreads, short-sale restrictions or an omitted state can invalidate the simplified economic conclusion.

Method

Infer the price of protection

  1. Specify

    Create a table with one row per jointly exhaustive future state, one column per traded asset and the price of each asset today.

  2. Solve

    Write one pricing equation per asset and solve for state prices. Test whether securities span the claims of interest.

  3. Interpret

    Price the new claim, check the cost of a guaranteed euro, and compare normalized state prices with actual probabilities only when physical estimates are explicitly supplied.

Worked case

Insurance against a bad harvest

A bond costs €0.95 and pays €1 in either outcome. A share costs €1.00 and pays €0.50 after a bad harvest or €1.50 after a good harvest. Both settle on the same date with no intermediate income.

Working

  1. Let bad and good state prices be b and g. The bond requires b + g = 0.95. The share requires 0.50b + 1.50g = 1.00. Substitution gives g = 0.525 and b = 0.425.
  2. A crop-insurance claim paying €10 only in the bad harvest costs €4.25. A good-only €10 claim costs €5.25. Together they cost €9.50 and pay €10 for sure.
  3. Suppose the bad harvest’s physical probability is 0.25. Its state price per unit of probability is 0.425/0.25 = 1.70, versus 0.525/0.75 = 0.70 in the good state.

Interpretation

Even though the bad-only contract costs less in absolute terms, money conditional on that state has greater value per unit of its occurrence probability.

A common mistake

Reading the larger state price as proof the corresponding state is more likely.

How to correct it

A state price combines likelihood with the value of money in that state. Normalize to get pricing weights or supply physical probabilities separately.

Quick check
What is the price of a contract paying €4 in either outcome?

A certain €1 costs €0.60 + €0.35 = €0.95. Four such claims cost €3.80. The state prices already incorporate discounting.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A two-state bond pays one in both states and costs 0.90. Recession state price is 0.50. A claim pays 6 in recession and 2 in expansion.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find the expansion state price and claim value.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Find the recession pricing probability and explain its interpretation.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Expansion state price is 0.90 − 0.50 = 0.40. The claim costs 6 × 0.50 + 2 × 0.40 = 3.80.

Part B 2 marks

0.50/0.90 ≈ 55.56%. This is the normalized weight that reproduces prices when expected payoffs are discounted with the bond. Further economic information is needed to infer a physical probability.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How can you show algebraically that a security paying poorly when marginal money is valuable needs a higher expected return?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would derive it from a euro’s price today. The risky gross payoff must satisfy E[mR] = 1, and the safe one satisfies Rf = 1/E[m]. Expanding the covariance gives a premium equal to minus Cov(m,R) divided by E[m]. Here the covariance is −0.08, so the asset needs about 8.89 points above the safe rate. That is a state-contingent explanation of risk compensation.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What is the price of a claim paying €2 only in the bad state, and why might its expected return be below the safe rate?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The bad state has price 0.5 × 1.1 = €0.55 per unit, so the claim costs €1.10. Its average payoff is €1 and expected gross return about 0.9091. It pays precisely when money is most valuable, which can support its insurance value despite low average return.
