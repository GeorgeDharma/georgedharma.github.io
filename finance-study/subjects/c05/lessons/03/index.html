The efficient frontier

Find the best expected return feasible at each risk level. Estimated means and covariances can move the frontier substantially.

The starting point

An investment committee can usually assemble many portfolios from the same assets. First list the combinations the rules allow. For each combination estimate the expected return and the risk, measured here by volatility. A portfolio is dominated if another feasible choice offers at least as much return and at most as much risk, with one improvement.

The efficient frontier traces the best remaining trade-offs. Which point a person prefers depends on how much risk they can bear. A computer can optimize precisely from estimates that are themselves uncertain, so an analyst checks whether small changes to expected returns, correlations or position limits alter the answer.

Theory and method

Markowitz efficient frontier and two-fund separation

Global minimum-variance portfolio · Mean–variance opportunity set

Two portfolios both have 8.53% volatility but expected returns of 2% and 6%. How can they lie on the same minimum-variance boundary?

Suppose a manager knows the global minimum-variance risky portfolio G and another efficient portfolio T. Mixing them can move to either side of G in expected-return space. G is the turning point for risk, so equally distant targets can require the same variance. Only the upper half offers the greater return at that risk. This distinction matters when an optimizer reports the full minimum-variance boundary rather than only the options a risk-averse investor would retain.

How the result is built

μ(w) = μG + w(μT − μG); σ²(w) = σ²G + w²(σ²T − σ²G)

w is the weight in T and 1 − w is the weight in G. μG and μT are annual expected returns. σ²G and σ²T are annual return variances. The second equality uses Cov(G,T) = σ²G for the unrestricted minimum-variance portfolio.

  1. For a fully invested perturbation from G toward T, the first derivative of variance at w = 0 is zero. Therefore Cov(G,T − G) = 0.
  2. Expand Var[G + w(T − G)]. The cross-term vanishes and Var(T − G) equals Var(T) − Var(G).
  3. Take μG 4%, σG 7%, μT 8%, σT 12%. Weights ±0.5 give 2% or 6% expected return and common variance 0.0049 + 0.25 × 0.0095 = 0.007275, or 8.53% volatility.

When the reasoning holds

  • This derivation assumes an unrestricted fully invested risky opportunity set with an interior minimum. Binding short-sale or position caps can change the frontier segments.
  • G and T share the same estimated return distribution and horizon. Mixing weights drawn from separate estimation regimes invalidates their covariance relationship.

Terms in this lesson
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
Correlation
A measure of how closely two series move together in a linear pattern.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Liability
Something owed to another party, such as a loan or an unpaid supplier bill.
How to read the notation
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.

All notation examples

First example

Portfolio A offers 6% expected return and 10% volatility, B offers 7% and 10%, and C offers 8% and 14%. B dominates A. Neither B nor C dominates the other because C buys extra expected return with extra risk.

Feasibility before efficiency

A portfolio belongs to a feasible set if it meets the budget, borrowing, short-selling, liquidity and position rules. A mathematically elegant allocation involving short sales can be outside an actual mandate. First describe the set to which the efficient frontier applies.

Efficiency in mean–variance analysis eliminates choices where expected return could rise without extra volatility, or volatility could fall without reducing expected return. The minimum-variance portfolio is the leftmost feasible point. Portfolios below the upper branch may offer less expected return for the same risk.

Solve for a minimum with two assets

Write variance as a quadratic in the weight w invested in A. Minimizing it gives w = (variance of B − covariance)/(variance of A + variance of B − 2 × covariance), if the denominator is positive. The unrestricted solution may fall outside zero to one.

When short sales are prohibited, compare the permissible boundary if the optimal unconstrained weight is outside it. If a target expected return is imposed, the two-asset budget and return equations may fix the weights, leaving no freedom for further risk reduction.

Uncertainty in the frontier

Expected returns are estimates of future averages, often imprecise relative to monthly volatility. Small changes can swing an optimizer toward extreme weights that exploit apparent differences. Asset caps, alternative estimates and comparisons across samples help reveal where conclusions depend on noisy inputs.

Variance penalizes unexpectedly good and bad returns symmetrically. A pension plan concerned about failing to pay benefits may also need a shortfall or liability-relative analysis. The mean–variance frontier summarizes a chosen objective, investment universe and set of constraints.

Method

Construct a defensible frontier

  1. Define

    Specify permitted assets, horizon, return unit, trade limits and whether the investor may borrow or short.

  2. Optimize

    For a given expected return, minimize variance subject to the budget and mandate. Repeat across feasible returns, discarding dominated points.

  3. Test

    Shift uncertain inputs and compare weights, predicted risk and turnover. Report sensitivity and any binding constraint alongside the selected portfolio.

Worked case

The same frontier and two goals

Two uncorrelated assets have expected returns of 4% and 10% and volatilities of 10% and 20%, respectively. Both can be held long with weights adding to one. Ignore trading costs.

Working

  1. For minimum variance, weight in the first asset is 0.04/(0.01 + 0.04) = 80%. The mix earns expected 5.2% and has variance 0.8² × 0.01 + 0.2² × 0.04 = 0.008, or 8.94% volatility.
  2. A 7% expected-return target requires equal weights. Its variance is 0.25 × 0.01 + 0.25 × 0.04 = 0.0125, giving 11.18% volatility.
  3. A proposal holding only the first asset earns 4% at 10% volatility. The 80/20 mix dominates it under these estimates, since it improves both coordinates.

Interpretation

An investor wanting a higher expected return still faces a genuine trade-off between the minimum-variance mix and the target mix. Dominance alone cannot select between those two.

A common mistake

Selecting the portfolio with highest expected return and declaring it universally best.

How to correct it

Compare both expected return and risk, then apply the actual investor’s preferences and constraints to choose among efficient alternatives.

Quick check
A new feasible portfolio D offers 7% return at 9% volatility. Which existing portfolio does D certainly dominate besides A?

D matches B’s expected return at lower risk. C still offers a higher expected return than D, so neither of those two dominates the other.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

Assets X and Y have expected returns 3% and 9%. A policy caps Y at 50% and prohibits borrowing and shorting.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Can the policy reach a 7% expected return? Show a feasible bound.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Would a substantially lower correlation alone make the 7% target attainable?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

The highest permissible weight in Y is 50%. The maximum feasible expected return is 0.5 × 3% + 0.5 × 9% = 6%. A 7% target needs two thirds in Y, exceeding the cap.

Part B 2 marks

No. Correlation changes risk for each mix. With unchanged means, weights and constraints, it cannot change the maximum feasible expected return.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Two portfolios both have 8.53% volatility but expected returns of 2% and 6%. How can they lie on the same minimum-variance boundary?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would locate both portfolios against the global minimum. The 2% and 6% points here sit symmetrically around G’s 4% return, so the squared variance term makes their risks equal. The 6% point dominates the 2% point for a mean–variance investor. I would still inspect holdings and mandate feasibility before treating the dominant theoretical point as an available investment.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if holding either portfolio requires shorting while the client permits long positions only?

Compare with the follow-up answer

I would reconstruct the feasible set under the client’s actual constraints. The unconstrained two-fund result can call for holdings outside the mandate, and an active bound changes the local minimizing weights. A portfolio absent from the admissible set cannot establish dominance for that client.
