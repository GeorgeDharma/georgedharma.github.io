Volatility calibration

Infer volatility from prices and inspect skew, maturity effects and fit errors. A model can fit a calibration sample and hedge poorly on later dates.

The starting point

Implied volatility is the volatility input that makes a chosen option-pricing formula match a market quote, with other inputs and conventions fixed. A collection of implied volatilities indexed by strike and maturity forms a surface. Differences across strikes at one maturity are often called skew or smile.

Calibration selects model parameters to reduce discrepancies with selected quotes. Weighting errors by price, implied volatility or bid–ask width can favor different instruments. A small fitting error at a quoted strike says little about interpolation elsewhere, stability across dates or how the model’s hedge reacts to a market move.

Theory and method

Breeden–Litzenberger state-price density

Butterfly convexity · Implied volatility surface

How would you check a fitted call-price surface for an impossible implied probability before trusting its volatility smile?

European calls at neighboring strikes can be combined so their terminal portfolio payout is nonnegative and concentrated around the middle strike. Consequently their prices must form a convex curve in strike when maturities and other terms match. Breeden–Litzenberger links the curvature of this call-price curve to discounted risk-neutral terminal probabilities. This gives a test in traded-price space, even if each individual quote is conveniently displayed as implied volatility.

How the result is built

∂²C(K,T)/∂K² = D(T) fᵠ(K)

C is the current European call price, K the strike in currency units, T common expiry, D(T) its deterministic discount factor, and fᵠ the risk-neutral density of the terminal asset price, per currency unit.

  1. For maturity payoff max(Sₜ−K,0), a small rise in strike reduces payoff by that increment on paths ending above K. The first strike derivative is the negative discounted upper-tail probability under regularity.
  2. Differentiate with respect to strike again. The rate of decline in tail probability is minus the density, so call curvature equals positive discounted density.
  3. For equally spaced strikes 90,100,110 and prices 13,7,4, the long-wing short-two-middle butterfly costs 13−14+4=3. The central curvature estimate is 3/10²=0.03 per currency unit.

When the reasoning holds

  • Use comparable European calls with common maturity and dividend/forward conventions. For stochastic rates, the relevant distribution is under the maturity-forward measure with its bond price.
  • A classical density formula assumes sufficient smoothness. With atoms use state-price measures and slope jumps, and on a finite grid interpret a butterfly as weighted local probability information.
Terms in this lesson
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
Calibration
Choosing model parameters so that the model reproduces specified market observations as closely as possible.
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
Percentage point
The difference between two percentages. A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises one percentage point.
Option
A contract giving the buyer a right to buy or sell on stated terms. The buyer pays a premium for the right.
No-arbitrage
A pricing condition that rules out a feasible trade producing a certain gain for no net investment and with no risk of loss.
How to read the notation
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.

All notation examples

First example

At an implied volatility of 20%, a model call costs €5.00. Its local vega is €0.20 per one percentage point of volatility. A market quote of €5.60 gives a first estimate of 20% + 0.60/0.20 = 23%. Repricing and iterating accounts for the changing slope.

Invert an option quote

For a plain European option under a chosen pricing formula, implied volatility is the value σ that makes model price match a quote with other inputs fixed. If the price is outside relevant no-arbitrage bounds or other contract inputs are wrong, inversion may fail or produce misleading outputs.

The sensitivity of price to volatility is vega. At a current trial σ, a Newton step updates σ to σ + (market price − model price)/vega. The units of vega must match the volatility increment. Near zero vega, a small price discrepancy can imply an unstable volatility estimate.

Fit a surface, choose a loss

Options across strikes and maturities reveal a surface rather than one number. Calibration selects parameters to minimize weighted discrepancies between model and observed quotes. A price-error fit emphasizes large monetary errors, while volatility-error or spread-scaled fitting weights different instruments differently.

Raw midquotes may violate no-arbitrage relations due to stale or mismatched observations. Inspect bid–ask spreads, timestamps, put–call parity and strike shape before attributing every pattern to a volatility model. Interpolation between quotes should respect relevant economic restrictions.

Separate fitting from prediction

A calibrated surface describes prices observed now. Its movement after changes in the underlying affects hedge results. Models that fit today’s quotes equally well can predict different movements in the surface and different sensitivities.

Use out-of-sample strikes, later dates and realistic rehedging to examine those implications. Record parameter bounds, objective weights and optimizer start points. A parameter can be poorly identified even when the combined price fit is excellent.

Method

Adjust an implied-volatility estimate

  1. Check contract and bounds

    Confirm exercise style, dividends, rates, strike and maturity against observations from the same time.

  2. Take a controlled step

    Use the price residual divided by matching-unit vega and reprice. Bracket or limit a step if sensitivity is very small.

  3. Evaluate the surface

    Compare fit errors against spreads across strikes and maturities, then inspect parameter stability and later hedge error.

Worked case

Reconciling two calibration quotes

Two options have market mids €4.00 and €7.00. Their half-spreads are €0.05 and €0.25. A candidate model prices them at €4.10 and €6.80. A second model gives €4.04 and €6.70.

Working

  1. The first model’s residuals are +0.10 and −0.20, giving unweighted squared error 0.01 + 0.04 = 0.05. The second’s is 0.0016 + 0.09 = 0.0916.
  2. Scaling by half-spreads, the first model’s residuals become +2 and −0.8, squared sum 4.64. The second’s are +0.8 and −1.2, squared sum 2.08.
  3. Thus an unweighted price objective prefers the first fit and a spread-weighted objective prefers the second. State why the selected objective suits trading or valuation use.

Interpretation

A better fitted score under the declared objective is evidence about those quotes. It does not establish how well either model hedges tomorrow.

A common mistake

Treat two models with the same calibration loss as economically interchangeable.

How to correct it

Inspect which maturities and strikes drive the errors, how parameters change under perturbations and which hedge sensitivities or scenario values differ.

Quick check
Two options on the same asset and maturity imply 18% at one strike and 24% at another. What does this show within the specified pricing formula?

Each implied volatility inverts its own quote. The observed strike pattern describes relative market pricing under the formula. It does not identify one certain future volatility.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

At implied volatility 18%, a model price is €3.00 and market mid €3.30. Local vega is €0.15 per percentage point. A repricing at the updated volatility gives €3.31.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Compute the first volatility update and an approximate next correction assuming the same vega.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Why should the uncertainty in the result be reported for a near-expiry far-out-of-the-money option?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Initial residual +0.30 divided by 0.15 gives +2 percentage points, or 20%. Residual at 20% is −0.01, implying roughly −0.067 point and a new estimate near 19.93%.

Part B 4 marks

Vega may be tiny. Bid–ask noise or stale prices can then imply a large volatility range, and inversion may be sensitive to rounding and model assumptions. Quote the spread, vega scale and contract conditions.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How would you check a fitted call-price surface for an impossible implied probability before trusting its volatility smile?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would reconstruct European call prices on a consistent strike grid and check monotonicity, convex butterflies and maturity consistency where applicable. Here the butterfly costs +3 and delivers a nonnegative triangular payout. A negative price for that combination using genuinely executable wing asks and middle bids would be a different, much stronger concern than one noisy fitted midpoint.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Suppose the middle call were 9 while the wings remained 13 and 4. What would the discrepancy be?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The butterfly would cost 13 − 18 + 4 = −1. Its final payoff is nonnegative for the equal strike spacing. Check synchronization, bid–ask execution, settlement and quote conventions before treating a fit violation as a tradable arbitrage.
