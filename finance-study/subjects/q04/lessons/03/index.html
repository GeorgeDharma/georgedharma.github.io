Monte Carlo estimation

Simulate paths and discount their payoffs. The sample standard deviation divided by the square root of independent paths estimates sampling uncertainty.

The starting point

Monte Carlo pricing approximates a weighted average over possible future paths. Simulate market moves under the pricing model, calculate the contract’s payoff on each path, discount each payoff consistently and take their average. If rates are random and relevant, discounting may need to follow each individual path.

Sampling error measures uncertainty from using a finite number of paths. With independent paths of finite variance, the estimated standard error of the mean is the sample standard deviation of discounted payoffs divided by the square root of the number of paths. This describes uncertainty within the implemented model.

Theory and method

Central limit theorem and Monte Carlo mean-squared error

Monte Carlo standard error · Bias–variance decomposition

A numerical price has a tiny reported standard error but stays away from an analytic benchmark. How would you diagnose it?

An engine simulates discounted derivative payoffs on a time grid. As the path count grows the average converges to the expectation of whatever the implemented grid actually computes. That expectation can differ from the continuous-model price. Independent path variation controls random error while a biased time approximation shifts all runs in the same direction. Improving confidence around one error source can make the other easier to see.

How the result is built

MSE(V̂ₙ,h) = Var(Xh)/n + (E[Xh] − V)²

V is the target euro price, Xh a single discounted euro payoff generated at grid spacing h, V̂ₙ,h its n-path mean, and MSE the expected squared error in squared euros. Var and E refer to the simulation law.

  1. Write the estimation error as the centered Monte Carlo mean plus its population bias E[Xh] − V. The expected cross term is zero.
  2. For independent paths finite variance σ² gives Var(mean)=σ²/n. A central limit theorem supports an approximate interval around E[Xh].
  3. If σ=6, n=900 and bias is €0.30, standard error is €0.20 and root-MSE is √(0.04 + 0.09) ≈ €0.361. Quadrupling paths leaves the bias intact.

When the reasoning holds

  • Independent samples or an appropriate dependence adjustment are needed for the 1/n term. Antithetic paths require analysis of their independent pair averages.
  • The stated confidence interval concerns sampling around the grid expectation. Bias assessment needs a finer-grid study, analytic benchmark or another independent method.
Terms in this lesson
Standard error
An estimate of how much a statistic would vary across repeated samples under the assumed sampling process.
Discount factor
The price today of one unit of currency payable at a particular future date under specified risk assumptions.
Benchmark
A reference chosen in advance for comparison, such as a market index or an equal-weight portfolio.
How to read the notation
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.
Subscripts · S₀ and S₁
A small index names the time or item. If S₀ = €40 and S₁ = €42, the price moves from €40 at time 0 to €42 at time 1. A subscript i can label a company instead of a date.
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.

All notation examples

First example

For 10,000 independent paths, suppose the mean discounted payoff is €8 and the sample standard deviation is €12. The standard error is €12/100 = €0.12. A large-sample approximate 95% interval is €8 ± 1.96 × €0.12, or about €7.76 to €8.24.

An estimator of the pricing expectation

If X is a discounted payoff under a pricing measure, an arbitrage-consistent model gives value V = E[X] for the claim under its assumptions. Generate independent copies X₁,…,Xₙ using the correct stochastic dynamics, path observations and contract. The estimator V̂ is their arithmetic mean.

When paths and discounting are exact, the sample mean is unbiased for that expectation under integrability. If the simulated dynamics or barrier observation grid are approximate, the sample mean can be precise around an already biased number. Distinguish simulation noise from approximation bias.

Quantify uncertainty

For independent paths with finite variance σ², Var(V̂) = σ²/n because the variance of a sum adds n individual variances and dividing by n scales variance by 1/n². Estimate σ using the sample standard deviation of discounted payoffs.

A large-sample interval uses V̂ ± 1.96s/√n under a suitable normal approximation. An option with rare large payments can have an unstable variance estimate or many all-zero samples. Diagnostics should include tail contribution and independent reruns in addition to the headline standard error.

Allocate computation to the right error

Increasing sample size reduces sampling error only at a square-root rate. Halving a time step may reduce discretization error but also raises work per simulated path. Compare multiple sample sizes at fixed grid, and multiple grids under matched random inputs, to distinguish the two sources.

Path-dependent contracts require a carefully specified observation schedule. A continuously monitored knock-out sampled only daily may miss barrier breaches. More daily paths cannot remedy that missing within-day event unless the simulation or payoff evaluation addresses it.

Method

Estimate with reproducibility

  1. Generate under the pricing law

    Specify drift, volatility, random seed policy and whether discount factors depend on the same path.

  2. Build a pathwise payoff

    Apply cash-flow timing and discount each outcome consistently before averaging.

  3. Estimate sampling error

    Use independent paths or account for dependence from variance reduction. Repeat with a different grid to examine discretization separately.

Worked case

A rare digital payoff

A digital pays €100 with risk-neutral probability 0.04 after one period and zero otherwise. The deterministic discount factor is 0.96. Suppose 400 independent simulated paths produce 20 successes.

Working

  1. The estimated success probability is 20/400 = 0.05. Each success has present payoff €96, so the simulated price is €4.80.
  2. Using the Bernoulli plug-in variance gives standard error about 96√(0.05 × 0.95/400) ≈ €1.05. An illustrative normal interval is roughly €2.75 to €6.85.
  3. The model price known in this example is 96 × 0.04 = €3.84. The estimate’s difference is ordinary sampling noise. The known probability lets us benchmark the pricing implementation.

Interpretation

With very few successes, use a more suitable interval or sampling design for the event probability. A zero observed count does not prove a zero model price.

At the board

Measure simulation error against a price you can check

Price a one-year European call. Share and strike are both 100, the continuously compounded risk-free rate is 3%, volatility is 20%, and there are no dividends. For each independent standard normal draw z, the terminal share is 100 × exp(0.03 − 0.20²/2 + 0.20z). Discount the positive part of terminal share minus strike. See the reproducible code
from math import exp, sqrt
from random import Random
from statistics import fmean, stdev

rng = Random(1729)
payoffs = []
for _ in range(16384):
    final = 100 * exp(0.03 - 0.20**2/2 + 0.20 * rng.gauss(0, 1))
    payoffs.append(exp(-0.03) * max(final - 100, 0))
print(round(fmean(payoffs), 3), round(stdev(payoffs)/sqrt(len(payoffs)), 3))

The Black–Scholes model price for these inputs is 9.413. Under independent draws, the price standard error is the sample standard deviation of discounted payoffs divided by the square root of the path count. Approximately four times as many paths halve the standard error. A confidence band quantifies sampling error inside this model. It says nothing about a weak volatility assumption or a miswritten payoff.

A common mistake

A narrow confidence interval proves the model fairly values the contract in the market.

How to correct it

A simulation interval describes sampling uncertainty conditional on its model and implementation. Challenge inputs, observed contract terms, grid bias and market calibration independently.

Quick check
About how many independent paths are needed to cut this standard error to €0.06 if payoff variability is similar?

Halving standard error requires quadrupling the number of independent paths. More paths can improve precision while leaving a mistaken payoff formula or model assumption intact.

Independent exam problem

For 2,500 independent paths a mean discounted payoff is €6 and sample standard deviation €15. A second run on a finer time grid has mean €6.60.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Compute the first standard error and an approximate 95% interval.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Can the €0.60 difference establish time-step bias? What comparison would improve the diagnosis?

Part A 4 marks

Standard error is 15/50 = €0.30, producing €6 ± €0.588, or [€5.412, €6.588]. Assumptions include independent paths and a suitable mean approximation.

Part B 4 marks

A difference can reflect noise in both runs. Couple fine and coarse paths using consistent underlying random shocks, estimate paired differences and their uncertainty, and compare several refinements.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A numerical price has a tiny reported standard error but stays away from an analytic benchmark. How would you diagnose it?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would hold the grid fixed and repeat independent runs to check whether reported sampling variability is credible. Then I would vary the grid with paired underlying shocks and estimate the change in the mean. Here a thirty-cent bias limits accuracy even when the path count is quadrupled. I would also test the contractual payoff and discount convention independently.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Can you claim a 95% probability that the true continuous price lies in mean ± 1.96 standard errors?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The standard interval targets the implemented expectation under a repeated-sampling approximation. To cover the continuous price, quantify or remove the grid bias and justify the uncertainty method. An unmodeled €0.30 shift can exceed the entire sampling interval at high path counts.
