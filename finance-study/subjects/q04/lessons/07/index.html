Interest-rate dynamics

Compare a mean-reverting short-rate model with a market-implied initial curve. Distinguish fitting today’s prices from forecasting how curves will move.

The starting point

The short rate is the model’s instantaneous interest rate. A mean-reverting model pulls it toward a long-run level while allowing random surprises. In a simple constant-parameter model the expected future rate equals the long-run level plus today’s deviation multiplied by exp(−κt), where κ controls the speed of reversion.

The probability measure matters. Parameters used to value bonds under a pricing measure need not describe future realized rates under historical probabilities. A model can be extended or calibrated to reproduce today’s observed term structure. Matching that cross-section says little by itself about tomorrow’s curve moves or hedging performance.

Theory and method

Vasicek short-rate transition law

Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process · Mean-reverting Gaussian diffusion

A desk quotes the conditional mean rate but gives no uncertainty around it. Can you derive the missing conditional variance?

A modeled overnight rate drifts back toward a long-run level after a shock, but it also receives fresh random shocks before the forecast date. Early shocks have more time to decay, while shocks just before the horizon remain nearly undiminished. The exact Vasicek transition sums these independent shocks with exponential weights. Conditional variance starts near zero at a short horizon and tends toward a finite long-run level for positive mean reversion.

How the result is built

Var(rₜ₊ₕ | rₜ) = σ²[1 − exp(−2κh)]/(2κ)

r is annual short rate as a decimal, h is forecast horizon in years, κ positive reversion speed per year, σ instantaneous rate volatility in decimal rate per square root of year, and Var conditional variance in squared rate units.

  1. Multiply the centered Vasicek equation by an integrating factor. Future randomness becomes σ∫ exp[−κ(t+h−s)] dWₛ over the forecast interval.
  2. Itô isometry gives the integral of the squared kernel, σ²∫₀ʰ exp(−2κu)du. Integration yields the displayed variance.
  3. If κ=0.5 and σ=0.02, one-year variance is 0.0004(1−e⁻¹)≈0.00025285. Conditional standard deviation is about 1.59 percentage points.

When the reasoning holds

  • Constant parameters and a Gaussian Brownian driver deliver this exact transition. Rate-dependent volatility such as in a CIR process changes the distribution.
  • Specify whether the transition is under a historical or pricing measure. Fitting current bonds under pricing parameters does not identify physical forecast probabilities.
Terms in this lesson
Interest rate
The price of borrowing money or the return for lending it, quoted as a percentage over a stated period.
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Coupon
The interest payment specified in a bond’s terms.
Risk premium
Extra expected return for bearing risk, or the amount a risk-averse person would give up to replace a gamble with certainty, depending on context.
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
Calibration
Choosing model parameters so that the model reproduces specified market observations as closely as possible.
How to read the notation
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.
Time and random increments · dt, dW
Over a short interval Δt, a standard Brownian increment has mean zero and standard deviation √Δt. If a year is one time unit, a quarter-year increment has standard deviation 0.5. A drift term scales with elapsed time itself.
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.

All notation examples

First example

Take a starting short rate of 5%, a long-run level of 3% and κ = 0.5 per year. The one-year conditional mean under the specified measure is 3% + (5% − 3%) × exp(−0.5) ≈ 4.21%. Random shocks leave the realized rate uncertain.

Mean reversion in the short rate

A short-rate model represents the rate earned over an instant. In the constant-parameter Vasicek model dr = κ(θ − r)dt + σdW, κ > 0 controls how quickly the drift pulls r toward θ. The local shock size σ is independent of r in this model.

Taking conditional expectations leads to m′(h) = κ[θ − m(h)], with m(0) = r₀. Separation or an integrating factor gives m(h) = θ + (r₀ − θ)exp(−κh). A deviation’s expected half-life is ln(2)/κ in the model’s time unit.

Separate the pricing curve from an expected rate

A zero-coupon price is an expectation under the relevant pricing measure of exp(−∫rₛds). In general it differs from exp(−∫E[rₛ]ds) because the exponential is nonlinear. Discounting at the future mean short rate misses the dispersion of its accumulated path.

Parameters estimated from historical movements describe a physical measure. Risk-neutral parameters value traded interest-rate claims and can embed term risk premiums. A model can fit today’s bond prices yet imply unrealistic empirical forecasts.

Assess model trade-offs

Gaussian short rates permit negative values and give tractable calculations. Whether that feature is appropriate depends on market and contract. Alternative models can give volatility dependent on the rate level. Time-dependent parameters may fit today’s term structure but add calibration and stability choices.

Inspect how the model represents level, slope and curvature changes across maturities. A single-factor model often imposes strong cross-maturity dependence. Portfolio hedges sensitive to independent twists may need additional factors or explicit use constraints.

Method

Compute a conditional rate path

  1. Choose the measure

    Label parameters as historical or pricing parameters. Identify units and the horizon in years.

  2. Solve the mean equation

    Start from r₀ − θ, multiply by the decay factor exp(−κh), then add back θ.

  3. Challenge the curve behavior

    Compare calibration with later curve changes and inspect whether the modeled number of independent rate shocks is adequate.

Worked case

A policy-rate deviation

Under a specified historical model r₀ = 6%, θ = 3% and κ = ln 2 per year. The model has nonzero Brownian noise. Evaluate expected rates over two years.

Working

  1. The initial deviation is three percentage points. Because exp(−κ) = 1/2, the expected deviation after one year is 1.5 points.
  2. Hence the one-year conditional mean is 4.5%. After two years the deviation shrinks to 0.75 point and the conditional mean to 3.75%.
  3. The realized rate can exceed 6% after either interval. Mean reversion describes conditional drift and leaves uncertainty around the mean.

Interpretation

These are historical expectations in this example. To value a rate option, a separately justified pricing model, volatility and calibration are required.

A common mistake

Use an exactly fitted initial yield curve as proof that the model gives accurate future rate distributions.

How to correct it

Test time evolution separately. Compare out-of-sample curve changes, option prices where relevant and hedging residuals across maturities and regimes.

Quick check
Suppose a pricing model reproduces every calibration bond price today. What additional test addresses its hedging use?

A cross-sectional fit checks the initial curve. Out-of-sample hedge errors address how well modeled sensitivities and dynamics translate into protection as market conditions change.

Independent exam problem

Assume κ = ln 4 per year, θ = 2% and current short rate 10%.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Find the half-life and the model’s one-year and half-year conditional means.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Can a bond price generally be calculated by discounting at these physical forecast means? Explain both issues.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Half-life is ln 2/ln 4 = 0.5 year. After one year the eight-point deviation is quartered, giving 4%. After half a year it is halved, giving 6%.

Part B 4 marks

Pricing generally requires a risk-neutral or otherwise justified valuation measure, which may differ from the physical distribution. Even under the pricing measure the expectation of an exponential discount factor differs from discounting at an expected rate path.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A desk quotes the conditional mean rate but gives no uncertainty around it. Can you derive the missing conditional variance?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would use the exact variance from the integrated mean-reverting shock. Recent shocks remain almost fully in the terminal rate, while older shocks are damped. With these parameters the one-year dispersion is about 1.59 points regardless of today’s rate. A conditional mean of 3% alone is therefore an incomplete description of the paths that matter to an option.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What happens as mean reversion κ tends toward zero with σ fixed?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Use 1 − exp(−2κh) ≈ 2κh. The variance tends to σ²h, the usual variance of a Brownian rate shock without pullback. The finite stationary variance ceases to exist as the reversion term vanishes.
