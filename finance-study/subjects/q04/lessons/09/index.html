Greeks and hedge error

Compare analytic, bump-and-revalue and simulated sensitivities. Balance finite-bump bias against numerical noise and assess discrete rebalancing error.

The starting point

A Greek measures how a model price responds to an input. Delta is sensitivity to the underlying price and gamma is the change of delta with that price. A centered bump-and-revalue delta uses the price at S + h minus the price at S − h, divided by 2h.

Large bumps average over curvature, while extremely small bumps can magnify numerical noise. Reusing the same random draws for up and down valuations can reduce noise in simulation-based differences. A delta hedge removes a local first-order exposure. Time between rebalances, jumps, volatility moves and trading costs can still create hedge error.

Theory and method

Pathwise derivative estimator for delta

Infinitesimal perturbation analysis · Likelihood-ratio method

Can you estimate a European call delta from the same paths as its price without bumping the spot twice?

Suppose a stock’s simulated terminal price equals today’s spot multiplied by a random growth factor. When spot changes slightly with each path’s growth factor held fixed, an in-the-money call’s payoff changes by that factor times the spot move. Out-of-the-money paths have zero local payoff slope. Under suitable interchange conditions, averaging those discounted pathwise slopes gives the derivative of the model price, providing an independent comparison with finite differences.

How the result is built

Δ = D Eᵠ[G 1{S₀G > K}]

Δ is shares per one-share call, D a deterministic discount factor, G a dimensionless simulated pricing growth factor independent of the initial spot parameter, S₀ and K current spot and strike, and 1{·} an indicator.

  1. Write C(S₀)=D E[max(S₀G−K,0)]. With positive G and no point mass at the exercise boundary, the pathwise slope is G on exercising paths and zero elsewhere.
  2. A suitable integrable bound permits exchanging the derivative and expectation. Discount the average pathwise slopes.
  3. For four illustrative growth factors 0.8,1.0,1.1,1.3, S₀=100, K=105 and D=0.95, two paths exercise. Estimated delta is 0.95(1.1+1.3)/4=0.57.

When the reasoning holds

  • The pathwise rule needs differentiability almost surely and justification for exchanging expectation and derivative. A digital payoff can have zero pathwise derivative almost everywhere yet a nonzero price delta.
  • If recalibration changes G with initial spot, the simple expression omits that dependence. Specify whether market calibration is frozen or rebuilt.

Terms in this lesson
Greeks
Measures of how an option’s value changes with an input. Delta measures its sensitivity to the underlying price.
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
Derivative
A contract whose value depends on something else, such as a currency, an interest rate or a share price.
Convexity
Curvature. In fixed income it describes how a bond’s price sensitivity to yield changes as the yield moves.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
How to read the notation
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.
A change · Δ
ΔS commonly means the new asset price less the old price. A move from €50 to €53 gives ΔS = €3. In options, delta can also name a sensitivity. A delta of 0.6 means a €1 rise in the underlying predicts about a €0.60 rise in the option, for a small move.

All notation examples

First example

At stock prices €99, €100 and €101 a call is valued at €5.40, €6.00 and €6.70. Its centered delta is (6.70 − 5.40)/2 = 0.65. Its centered gamma is 6.70 − 2 × 6.00 + 5.40 = 0.10 per euro for this €1 grid step.

Estimate local slopes and curvature

Delta is the derivative of value with respect to the underlying input. Gamma is the second derivative. For a smooth price function, centered delta uses [V(S+h) − V(S−h)]/(2h). Centered gamma uses [V(S+h) − 2V(S) + V(S−h)]/h².

Taylor expansions explain the cancellation. The even powers cancel in the delta numerator and the constant and odd powers cancel for gamma. Under sufficient smoothness, both centered formulas usually have leading truncation error proportional to h².

Balance numerical error sources

Making h smaller reduces truncation error but can amplify roundoff or simulation noise. Gamma is especially sensitive because the surviving difference is small and divided by h². Reuse the same random draws for all bumps to reduce unrelated sampling variation.

Some payoffs have kinks or discontinuities and a finite bump crosses a boundary. A sensitivity may then be unstable or have a different interpretation. Compare several bump sizes and independent methods, and document whether curves or implied-volatility surfaces are frozen or recalibrated during the bump.

What a delta hedge leaves behind

For a small move ΔS, an option has approximate change ΔΔS + ½Γ(ΔS)², plus effects of time, volatility and other inputs. Offsetting its delta with stock removes the linear stock contribution locally. The squared term remains between hedge rebalances.

If a desk is short convexity, either direction of a sufficiently large move can hurt a delta-neutral position, all else equal. Real hedge profit also contains time decay, financing, dividends, spreads and volatility changes. A finite illustrative move alone cannot establish expected trading profitability.

Method

Audit hedge quantities

  1. Match quote units

    Confirm option size, contract multiplier, delta convention and the underlying unit actually traded.

  2. Build centered estimates

    Price at the base and symmetric positive and negative bumps under identical non-bumped inputs.

  3. Reconcile a finite shock

    Multiply sensitivities by position size and shock size, then compare approximated P&L with full repricing and financing assumptions.

Worked case

A delta hedge across a €2 move

An option on one share is worth €4.00 at S = €50, €3.45 at S = €49 and €4.65 at S = €51. The desk is short 200 such options. Assume time and volatility are frozen for an illustrative stock-only move.

Working

  1. Centered delta is (4.65 − 3.45)/2 = 0.60. Gamma is 4.65 − 2 × 4 + 3.45 = 0.10 per euro.
  2. Short-option delta is −120, so buying 120 shares offsets it. The combined stock-plus-option gamma is −20 per euro because shares have zero gamma.
  3. For a €2 change, the local quadratic P&L after delta hedging is about ½ × (−20) × 2² = −€40 under either sign, holding the estimated gamma fixed.

Interpretation

For larger moves the value curve may change shape. Reprice the option at the scenario stock price and compare actual change with this estimate.

A common mistake

Dividing a €1 price bump by the spot price automatically converts gamma into delta.

How to correct it

These derivatives measure different orders of response. Verify formulas, units and position multipliers before aggregating across instruments. A relative spot shock needs conversion to an absolute price move.

Quick check
A desk is short 100 of these calls, each on one share. Which immediate stock trade offsets the estimated delta?

The short calls have estimated aggregate delta −100 × 0.65 = −65. Buying 65 shares provides +65 delta at current inputs. It will generally require rebalancing later.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

At S ± €0.50 call values are €2.58 and €3.48, respectively for the down and up bumps. At S the value is €3.00. There are 50 short calls, one share per call.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate delta and gamma for one call.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Give the hedge and approximate curvature P&L for a €1 rise assuming other inputs fixed.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Centered delta is (3.48 − 2.58)/(2 × 0.50) = 0.90. Gamma is (3.48 − 6 + 2.58)/0.50² = 0.06/0.25 = 0.24 per euro.

Part B 4 marks

Buy 50 × 0.90 = 45 shares. Combined gamma is −50 × 0.24 = −12. Curvature P&L is about ½ × (−12) × 1² = −€6.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Can you estimate a European call delta from the same paths as its price without bumping the spot twice?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I can differentiate the smooth part of each path payoff and average if the required interchange is valid. In this four-path example the two exercising paths contribute 1.1 and 1.3 before discounting, producing a 0.57 delta. I would compare it with a centered bump using common draws and check the sampling error and frozen-calibration assumptions.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Why would this procedure fail for a cash-or-nothing digital?

Compare with the follow-up answer

A tiny bump typically leaves an individual digital payoff unchanged, so naive pathwise slopes are zero. Moving spot nevertheless moves probability across the strike boundary. Conditional smoothing, a suitable likelihood-ratio estimator or a carefully controlled common-random-number bump can capture that boundary contribution.
