Price a European payoff with a Fourier-cosine expansion

Relate the real part of a characteristic function to expected cosine terms.

Theory and terminology

The Fourier-cosine option pricing method

COS method · characteristic function · payoff coefficients

What you should be able to explain

  • Relate the real part of a characteristic function to expected cosine terms.
  • Price a bounded-state call with the COS formula and diagnose truncation and normalization errors.

Why a transform helps

The characteristic function of a random variable X is E[exp(iuX)], where i²=−1 and u is a frequency. Its real part gives the average cosine of uX and its imaginary part the average sine. In some jump and stochastic-volatility models the characteristic function is available in a convenient form even when an ordinary probability density is expensive to evaluate.

A European claim depends only on the terminal state. Suppose its payoff is g(X) and discounting is deterministic. Pricing amounts to averaging g under the pricing distribution and discounting. If g is represented by recognizable cosine functions, each term can be averaged separately without sampling thousands of terminal values.

Separate payoff coefficients from model coefficients

Fix an interval [a,b]. The functions cos(kπ(x−a)/(b−a)) form a cosine basis there. For each frequency the payoff coefficient is an ordinary integral of g times that cosine. The zeroth coefficient is treated with half weight because the constant basis function has a different squared integral from the higher cosines.

The model coefficient is the expected shifted cosine. Compute it by multiplying the characteristic function by exp(−iua), then taking the real part. Using the correct frequency with the wrong origin can reverse a contribution. The same model coefficients can be reused across payoffs if variable, interval and model remain fixed.

Two separate approximation decisions

The standard COS method truncates the state space to a finite interval and retains a finite number of frequencies. Test whether widening the interval changes the value. This test is sensitive to omitted tail probability times payoff size. Separately increase the number of frequencies while holding the interval fixed. A digital discontinuity or payoff kink often demands more terms than a smooth payoff.

For a state genuinely supported on a bounded interval the support truncation disappears. In the worked case the density itself has just two nonzero cosine expectations, so the price becomes exact even though the call has a kink. This special case makes a strong unit test for signs, the factor of two and discounting.

Method

Build the result

V₀≈D Σ′ₖ Re[exp(−iuₖa)φ(uₖ)]Gₖ; uₖ=kπ/(b−a); Gₖ=2/(b−a)∫ₐᵇg(x)cos[uₖ(x−a)]dx

V₀ is present currency, D is the discount factor, x is the terminal state, i the imaginary unit, and φ(u)=Eᴽ[exp(iuX)]. k ranges from 0 to the last retained frequency. The prime gives k=0 half weight. Gₖ is a payoff coefficient in currency.

  1. Expand the payoff on [a,b] into its constant half-coefficient plus successively higher cosine terms. Each Gₖ is obtained by integrating against the corresponding cosine.
  2. Taking a risk-neutral expectation replaces each cosine by Re[exp(−iuₖa)φ(uₖ)]. The k=0 expectation is one because φ(0)=1.
  3. Multiply and add the coefficients, discount once, and test tail and frequency errors. In a bounded-support exact benchmark, compare directly with the density integral.
Worked case

A call on a bounded contract index

A terminal performance index X is defined on [0,1] and has risk-neutral density f(x)=1+0.6 cos(πx) there. It integrates to one and stays positive. A European certificate pays 100 max(X−0.5,0). Its discount factor is 0.97. This is an illustrative bounded-state claim.

Working

  1. The model expectations are E[1]=1, E[cos(πX)]=0.6×½=0.3, and zero for all higher cosines by orthogonality. Only the constant and first modes matter.
  2. G₀=2×100×∫₀.₅¹(x−0.5)dx=25. The constant contribution before discount is half of this, 12.5.
  3. Integration by parts gives ∫₀.₅¹(x−0.5)cos(πx)dx=−1/π². Thus G₁=−200/π²=−20.2642 and the weighted contribution is 0.3G₁=−6.0793.
  4. Expected cash is 12.5−6.0793=6.4207. Discounting gives 6.2281. Direct integration of payoff times 1+0.6 cos(πx) gives the same number.

Decision and interpretation

The first cosine puts more mass near zero and reduces a call's value from the uniform-state benchmark. This directional check catches a sign error even before the exact comparison.

Quick check
At zero frequency φ(0)=1. How should a constant payoff of 10 be represented on any finite interval?

Integrating the constant in the definition of G₀ gives 20. Its half weight restores the payoff of 10 before discounting.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

On [0,1] a new pricing density is 1−0.4 cos(πx). A digital pays 25 if X exceeds 0.5 and D=0.96. Compute its value directly and show why the sign is sensible.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked solution
  1. The uniform part gives probability 0.5. The cosine integral from 0.5 to 1 is [sin(πx)/π]₀.₅¹=−1/π.
  2. The upper-half probability is 0.5+(0.4/π)=0.62732. The present digital value is 0.96×25×0.62732=15.0558.
  3. It exceeds the uniform digital value 12. The negative cosine tilt shifts probability toward the upper end of the interval, where this payoff is positive.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How can a quant price a European claim when the model provides a characteristic function more readily than a density?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I write the payoff as a sum of cosine terms on a suitable interval. The characteristic function gives the expected value of each cosine at the chosen frequencies. I add the weighted contributions, discount, and test both the interval and the number of retained terms.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Does doubling the number of COS terms fix an interval that excludes a material tail of an unbounded call?

Compare with the follow-up answer

No. More terms can improve the representation within the interval. Check the discarded tail's contribution or widen the interval, then revisit the frequency count and benchmark.
