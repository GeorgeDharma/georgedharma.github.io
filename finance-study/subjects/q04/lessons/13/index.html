Theory and terminology

Euler–Maruyama and Milstein discretization

strong convergence · weak convergence · stochastic differential equation

What you should be able to explain

  • Compute Euler–Maruyama and Milstein updates with correct time scaling.
  • Design coupled grid tests and distinguish path accuracy, expectation bias and Monte Carlo uncertainty.

Freeze the coefficients over one short interval

A stochastic differential equation describes how the state changes under a predictable drift and a random diffusion shock. Euler–Maruyama freezes both coefficients at the start of an interval of length h. The Brownian change ΔW has mean zero and variance h. A standard normal Z therefore gives ΔW=√h Z, rather than hZ.

If the random-shock coefficient depends on the state, the first portion of the shock changes how strongly the remaining portion moves the state. In one dimension Milstein adds the first correction for that interaction. Its term contains (ΔW)²−h. Conditional on the starting point this has mean zero, but it affects individual paths.

Which error does the application care about?

Strong convergence measures how closely the simulated trajectory's state matches the reference state driven by the same Brownian motion, for example mean absolute terminal error. Weak convergence measures bias in an expected test function, such as the discounted value of a sufficiently smooth payoff. A scheme can average out path errors and produce better weak than strong order.

For sufficiently regular scalar coefficients and smooth payoff functions, Euler–Maruyama typically has strong order one-half and weak order one. Scalar Milstein typically attains strong order one and weak order one. Discontinuous payoffs, irregular coefficients, constraints or boundary hitting can invalidate a textbook rate. The scalar formula also omits stochastic area terms that arise for general multidimensional noise.

Design a fair numerical test

For a coarse step h and two fine steps h/2, first draw the two fine Brownian increments and use their sum as the coarse increment. Independent draws would measure variation across different market paths as well as discretization error. Use many paired paths and several grid widths, reporting uncertainty in the slope or mean difference.

For bias in a price, reduce the time step while separately controlling sampling error with adequate draws. For exposure profiles or hedge replay, inspect pathwise differences and boundary violations. The exact geometric Brownian transition makes a useful transparent test. A high-probability-looking number from one draw cannot establish a convergence rate.

Method

Build the result

Euler: X′=X+a h+b ΔW. Milstein: X′=X+a h+b ΔW+½ b bₓ[(ΔW)²−h]

X is the current state, X′ its next approximation, h is time in years, ΔW has variance h, and a and b are the local drift and diffusion at X. bₓ is the derivative of b with respect to X. Terms added to X all have state units.

  1. Freeze coefficients to obtain the Euler update. In the noise coefficient's first local expansion, its change is approximately bₓ b times the Brownian change.
  2. The corresponding iterated stochastic integral is ∫(Wₛ−Wₜ)dWₛ=½[(ΔW)²−h], from Itô’s formula for a squared Brownian increment.
  3. Substitute that integral to obtain Milstein. When b is state-independent, bₓ=0 and the two single-step schemes agree.
Worked case

A fixed Brownian shock against an exact stock transition

For dS=μSdt+σSdW, set S=100, annual μ=0.04, annual σ=0.20 and h=0.25. One realized ΔW is +0.45. The exact transition is S exp[(μ−σ²/2)h+σΔW]. Compare the terminal states without discounting.

Working

  1. The drift move is 0.04×100×0.25=1. The diffusion move is 0.20×100×0.45=9. Euler gives 110.000.
  2. Here b=0.20×100=20 and bₓ=0.20. The correction is ½×20×0.20×(0.45²−0.25)=−0.095. Milstein gives 109.905.
  3. The exact exponent is (0.04−0.02)×0.25+0.20×0.45=0.095. Exact endpoint is 109.96589. Absolute errors are about 0.03411 for Euler and 0.06089 for Milstein.
  4. For a coupled refinement one possible split is +0.20 and +0.25 across the two half-intervals. Using h/2=0.125 and restarting the second Euler step at the first updated state gives 100×1.045×1.055=110.2475.

Decision and interpretation

In this realization Euler happens to land closer than Milstein and the fine Euler step also misses by more. Orders concern aggregates as the grid tends to zero. An individual path can behave differently.

Quick check
Two half-step Brownian changes are +0.12 and −0.05. Which coarse change should a paired strong-error comparison use?

Adding the Brownian increments gives +0.12−0.05=+0.07 on the same coarse interval. Preserving the same driving path isolates discretization effects.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

A second stock starts at 80 with μ=0.03, σ=0.25, h=0.25 and ΔW=−0.40. Compute the two updates and compare with the exact transition.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked solution
  1. Euler gives 80+0.03×80×0.25+0.25×80×(−0.40)=72.60.
  2. Milstein adds ½×(0.25²)×80×(0.16−0.25)=−0.225 to get 72.375.
  3. Exact price is 80 exp[(0.03−0.25²/2)×0.25−0.10]=72.36438. Milstein is closer on this draw. Multiple coupled paths are required to assess the order.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How should a model validator compare two time discretizations of the same stochastic model?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I align the Brownian paths, compare each scheme to an exact benchmark where one exists, and measure pathwise errors separately from errors in an expected payoff. Making the grid finer and increasing the path count address different sources of error.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Would an ordinary Monte Carlo standard error reveal a large time-step bias?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The sample standard error measures uncertainty in the mean of the implemented discretized payoff. A tight interval can surround a biased target. Refine the grid, compare schemes and validate against an exact or independently converged price.
