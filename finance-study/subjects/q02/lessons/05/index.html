Identification strategies

State what would have happened without the event or policy. Examine parallel trends for difference-in-differences and exclusion for an instrumental variable.

The starting point

A causal estimate needs a counterfactual, an account of what the affected group would have experienced without the intervention. Difference-in-differences compares the treated group’s change with a comparison group’s change. Its central assumption concerns parallel trends in the absence of treatment, alongside conditions such as no anticipation or spillovers.

An instrumental variable uses outside variation that alters treatment. The instrument must be relevant, and its connection to outcomes must satisfy the proposed exclusion and independence assumptions. A convenient predictor of treatment can fail these requirements if it changes the outcome through another channel.

Theory and method

Group-time difference-in-differences

Callaway–Sant’Anna group-time ATT · Parallel trends

Policies roll out in different quarters. Why compute an effect separately for each adoption group and date?

A bank adopting underwriting software early can still be improving when another bank adopts later. If the early bank is then used as an untreated comparison, its ongoing treatment effect can contaminate the estimated change. A group-time design compares each adoption cohort with units still untreated on the relevant dates, or with a never-treated group. It reports how effects evolve and specifies how different cohorts contribute to any aggregate.

How the result is built

ATT(g,t) = [E(Yₜ−Yg₋₁ | G=g)] − [E(Yₜ−Yg₋₁ | C=1)]

G is first treatment date, g the cohort under study, t an observation at or after g, Y the approval rate in percentage points, C the eligible untreated comparison group, and ATT(g,t) that cohort’s treatment effect at t under the stated design.

  1. Choose the last untreated period g−1 as baseline and calculate the cohort’s observed change through t.
  2. Subtract the change over the same period for a never-treated or eligible not-yet-treated comparison. Justify parallel untreated trends, possibly conditional on covariates.
  3. For early cohort 20→24→24, late 21→23→28 and never 18→20→21, early effects are +2 points at period 1 and +1 at period 2. The late effect at period 2 is (28−23)−(21−20)=+4.

When the reasoning holds

  • Treatment is absorbing in this simple design and there is no anticipation before the chosen baseline. Announced future adoption may require an earlier baseline.
  • The eligible controls must support the needed untreated trend and comparable covariates. An already-treated control with changing effects can invalidate that comparison.
Terms in this lesson
Counterfactual
What would probably have happened without the event, decision or policy being studied.
Percentage point
The difference between two percentages. A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises one percentage point.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
How to read the notation
Conditional probability · P(A | B)
Read the vertical bar as “given.” If four of 100 late-paying customers default, P(default | late payment) in that group is 4%. To find P(late payment | default), start with the group of customers who defaulted.
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.

All notation examples

First example

After a guarantee program, borrowing costs fall from 8% to 6% for eligible firms and from 7% to 6% for comparison firms. The difference-in-differences estimate is (6 − 8) − (6 − 7) = −1 percentage point. A simultaneous industry subsidy targeted only at eligible firms would threaten attribution.

Difference-in-differences and untreated trends

When a policy covers one group but leaves another outside it, compare their changes across the same period. Subtract the control change from the treated change. Constant pre-existing level differences can remain because the identification assumption concerns untreated trends, not levels.

The average causal effect for treated units equals this contrast when untreated trends would have been parallel and treatment has no anticipatory, spillover or composition effects that violate the comparison. Pre-policy trajectories help evaluate plausibility, although matching past trends does not prove matching future untreated trends.

How an instrument identifies an effect

An instrument Z shifts exposure D without affecting outcome Y by another route, under the identifying assumptions. For a binary instrument the Wald ratio is [E(Y|Z=1) − E(Y|Z=0)]/[E(D|Z=1) − E(D|Z=0)]. The denominator is the first-stage effect.

With an appropriate independent instrument, exclusion, no defiers under monotonicity and a nonzero first stage, the ratio has a local causal interpretation for units whose treatment changes because of the instrument. It need not equal the population-wide average effect. Weak first stages make the ratio unstable.

Distinguish the two research designs

Difference-in-differences borrows a change from a comparison group. Instrumental variables use an external source of variation in treatment take-up. An administrative eligibility cutoff or randomized encouragement might support the second design, but factual knowledge of the institution determines which assumptions make sense.

Descriptive balance tests, placebo dates and alternative comparison groups can reveal weaknesses. A placebo that finds a pre-policy effect should prompt examination of anticipation, timing and unequal trends. Strong statistical fit does not by itself repair a broken identifying assumption.

Method

Audit the counterfactual

  1. Name the target

    Specify which units and exposure change the estimate describes, including whether it pertains to treated units or instrument-induced participants.

  2. Write a credible failure

    Identify one institutional event that would break parallel trends or one route from the instrument to the outcome bypassing treatment.

  3. Translate the contrast

    Calculate group changes or the reduced form and first stage. State units and explain the assumptions needed to give the result a causal reading.

Worked case

A randomized credit invitation

An invitation is randomized among eligible applicants. Loan take-up is 60% for invitees and 35% for others. Mean subsequent annual operating profit differs by €500 in favor of invitees. The invitation contains no other services in the proposed design.

Working

  1. The first stage is 0.60 − 0.35 = 0.25. The reduced form is an average €500 change in profit associated with invitation.
  2. The Wald estimate is €500/0.25 = €2,000 per additional loan induced by invitation, under the design assumptions.
  3. If the invitation included management training, it might raise profit even without borrowing. Its direct effect would enter the reduced form and invalidate the simple exclusion argument.

Interpretation

The estimate pertains to applicants who take a loan because of the invitation. Firms that always borrow may have very different expected effects.

A common mistake

Treat a successful first stage as evidence that the instrument satisfies exclusion.

How to correct it

Relevance requires a relationship with the treatment. Exclusion requires a different claim about the absence of other outcome pathways, which needs institutional reasoning and diagnostic evidence.

Quick check
A researcher uses distance to a new loan office as an instrument for credit uptake. Which finding most directly challenges exclusion?

Increasing sales directly gives distance a route to the outcome other than loan uptake. Predicting borrowing supports relevance. Neither fact alone establishes a valid instrument.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

Average loan approval rises from 40% to 54% in a pilot city and from 30% to 39% in a comparison city. Separately, an encouragement changes adoption by 0.10 and profits by €60.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate the pilot difference-in-differences estimate and state its identifying trend assumption.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Compute the encouragement ratio and name a direct-channel threat.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

The estimate is (54 − 40) − (39 − 30) = +5 percentage points. Without the pilot, the pilot city’s approval rate must have followed the comparison city’s change under the relevant design conditions.

Part B 4 marks

€60/0.10 = €600 per induced adoption. A free advisory session included in the encouragement may independently raise profit, violating exclusion.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Policies roll out in different quarters. Why compute an effect separately for each adoption group and date?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would calculate each cohort’s change relative to a clean untreated group over the same dates. Here the late group gains five points when untreated controls gain one, giving four points. The early group’s effect at that date is just one. Pooling the two without stating weights hides that difference, and using the already-treated early group as a clean control could distort it.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If 80% of treated borrowers at period two belong to the late cohort, what is the borrower-weighted effect?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Assuming each cohort estimate represents its borrowers, average 0.8 × 4 + 0.2 × 1 = 3.4 points. This differs from the equal-cohort 2.5-point figure because the target population weights cohorts differently.
