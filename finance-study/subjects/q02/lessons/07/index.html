Volatility and long-run relations

ARCH and GARCH models capture time-varying conditional variance. Cointegration concerns a stable long-run combination of otherwise nonstationary series.

The starting point

Large daily price swings often come in clusters. A GARCH model forecasts conditional variance, the expected squared size of the next innovation given the available history. A common form adds a baseline, a weight on the last squared surprise and a weight on the previous variance forecast. An ARCH model uses past squared surprises directly.

Cointegration addresses a different time-series question. Two series may each drift without a stable level while a particular linear combination fluctuates around a stable relationship. A level correlation alone cannot establish that relationship. The economic meaning of the combination and the behavior of its residual both matter.

Theory and method

Engle–Granger cointegration and error correction

Granger representation theorem · Error-correction model

Two nonstationary asset prices track a stable spread. What would a negative adjustment coefficient mean, and what would it leave unresolved?

Suppose a cash security temporarily rises above the value suggested by a linked instrument. The gap can influence later price changes if the relation has a genuine economic anchor. Cointegration formalizes a stationary combination of otherwise integrated levels. An error-correction model relates subsequent differences to the prior equilibrium gap and other short-run dynamics. Calling the gap stationary does not specify which price moves or guarantee it closes before a position must be funded.

How the result is built

sₜ = Aₜ − bBₜ, ΔAₜ = c + αsₜ₋₁ + short-run terms + εₜ

A and B are integrated price levels in chosen comparable units, b their long-run coefficient, s stationary equilibrium deviation, ΔA the one-period change, α adjustment coefficient per period, c drift and ε the innovation.

  1. Specify the economic relation and estimate b on a suitable sample or derive it from contract quantities. Test stationarity using critical values appropriate to an estimated cointegrating relation.
  2. If A is 6 units above equilibrium and α=−0.25, the error-correction component predicts a −1.5-unit movement in A next period, holding other terms fixed.
  3. If B stays fixed and shocks and other terms vanish, s evolves as 0.75s. Starting at 6 gives successive values 4.5 and 3.375. Both prices could adjust in a full vector model.

When the reasoning holds

  • A and B must have compatible integration behavior, and their spread must remain stable across the study period. A structural break can invalidate the estimated anchor.
  • Interpreting α as a causal adjustment requires stronger identification. Cointegration and predictability need not identify who initiates trades or reacts to information.
Terms in this lesson
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
Correlation
A measure of how closely two series move together in a linear pattern.
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
How to read the notation
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.
Subscripts · S₀ and S₁
A small index names the time or item. If S₀ = €40 and S₁ = €42, the price moves from €40 at time 0 to €42 at time 1. A subscript i can label a company instead of a date.

All notation examples

First example

Use variance forecast 0.00001 + 0.10 × last shock² + 0.85 × previous variance. A 4% shock and previous variance 0.0004 give 0.00001 + 0.00016 + 0.00034 = 0.00051. Taking the square root gives about 2.26% conditional volatility for the next period.

Conditional variance as a forecast

Even when tomorrow’s return direction is hard to predict, its spread around the forecast can vary with recent news. Conditional variance is E(εₜ₊₁² | information at t) when innovations have conditional mean zero. Its square root is conditional volatility in return units.

An ARCH model links that variance to earlier squared surprises. A GARCH(1,1) adds its previous conditional variance. Its recursion is hₜ₊₁ = ω + αεₜ² + βhₜ. Under suitable innovation assumptions, positive ω, nonnegative α and β, and α + β < 1 imply a finite long-run variance.

Derive the long-run quantity

Taking expectations of the recursion gives E(h) = ω + (α + β)E(h) at a stationary solution because E(ε²) = E(h). Rearranging yields E(h) = ω/(1 − α − β). As α + β approaches one, shocks dissipate slowly in expected forecasts.

The square root of expected variance is not generally expected volatility because the square root is nonlinear. State whether forecasts are in daily variance, daily standard deviation or annualized volatility. Annualization requires a model of dependence and the horizon.

Stable combinations of drifting series

A pair of integrated level series can move apart temporarily while one linear combination remains stationary. This is cointegration. A possible error-correction model relates next price changes to past deviations from that long-run combination and other predictors.

For a proposed spread sₜ = Aₜ − bBₜ, cointegration concerns stationarity of s, while volatility modeling concerns the conditional dispersion of its innovations. A big correlation between A and B in levels cannot establish either claim. An estimated hedge ratio also changes the appropriate statistical test.

Method

Separate the two models

  1. Name the random object

    Use return innovations for a volatility recursion or a specified linear combination of nonstationary prices for a cointegration question.

  2. Check the restrictions

    Verify variance parameters and horizon conventions. For a spread, examine integration orders, the theoretical link and appropriate stationarity evidence.

  3. Assess forecast behavior

    Inspect residuals and out-of-sample variance forecasts or the stability and adjustment of the proposed price spread.

Worked case

An elevated volatility forecast

Assume daily GARCH parameters ω = 0.000008, α = 0.10 and β = 0.85. Today’s conditional variance is 0.0004 and today’s return innovation is −5%, in decimal units.

Working

  1. The squared innovation is 0.0025. Tomorrow’s variance forecast is 0.000008 + 0.10 × 0.0025 + 0.85 × 0.0004 = 0.000598.
  2. Its standard deviation is √0.000598 ≈ 2.45% for the day. The negative surprise enters this symmetric model through its square, just like a +5% shock.
  3. The stationary long-run variance is 0.000008/0.05 = 0.00016, corresponding to a standard deviation scale of about 1.26%. Thus tomorrow’s forecast is elevated.

Interpretation

A model that gives equally sized positive and negative shocks the same effect can miss leverage asymmetry. Compare alternatives using future forecast evidence.

A common mistake

A stationary price spread should have constant conditional variance and be riskless to hold.

How to correct it

Stationarity can coexist with changing conditional variance and costly deviations before any adjustment occurs. The hedge ratio, liquidity, funding horizon and stability of the underlying relationship matter.

Quick check
Two drifting price series are proposed to be cointegrated with coefficient 2. Which diagnostic fits that proposal?

Cointegration refers to the long-run behavior of a specified combination. High level correlation and conditional volatility dynamics answer different questions.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

Assume ω = 0.00001, α = 0.15 and β = 0.75. Two firms’ equity prices also exhibit high correlation in levels.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate long-run daily variance and its standard deviation scale.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Suggest evidence needed before calling the two prices cointegrated.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Two nonstationary asset prices track a stable spread. What would a negative adjustment coefficient mean, and what would it leave unresolved?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

A negative α means that a positive prior spread predicts a lower subsequent change in A, other specified terms equal. That is consistent with A correcting part of the disequilibrium. The six-unit gap contributes −1.5 units to the next expected change in this example. I would also model B’s response and verify the long-run relation survives later data and trading frictions.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Could the spread be cointegrated while A itself has α=0?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Yes. In a multivariate error-correction system B can do the adjusting while A is weakly exogenous for the long-run parameters under suitable conditions. Estimate both adjustment equations before claiming that A specifically restores the relation.
