Vectors and matrices

Put holdings into a vector and exposures into a matrix. Matrix multiplication adds each holding’s contribution to several risks at once.

The starting point

Suppose a portfolio holds several contracts, each sensitive to more than one market move. A vector is an ordered list, such as the number of lots in each contract. A matrix is a rectangular table. Put one market sensitivity on each row and one contract on each column.

Multiplying each row by the holdings vector means multiplying matching entries and adding them. The resulting vector contains one total per risk. Keep units visible. Euros per interest-rate move and euros per currency move belong in separate rows, and a short position carries a negative holding.

Theory and method

Jacobian chain rule for factor exposures

Scenario matrix · Linear sensitivity mapping

A desk computes instrument exposure to market quotes, but risk management shocks underlying economic factors. How are the two maps reconciled?

A policy shock can move several quoted yields at once, while a single bond responds to several of those yields. Two sensitivity maps connect policy factors to quoted instruments and quoted instruments to the portfolio. Multiplying them in the correct order gives the implied exposure to a factor. Dimensional checks matter because the quote changes may be in basis points while the portfolio response is in thousands of euros per basis point.

How the result is built

ΔV ≈ gᵀJf

V is portfolio value in thousands of euros; g contains portfolio sensitivities in thousands of euros per quote basis point; J maps one unit of each factor to quote changes in basis points; f is a vector of factor shocks; ᵀ is transpose.

  1. Map economic shocks into quote moves with Jf. Then use the portfolio gradient g to translate those compatible quote moves into euros.
  2. Alternatively compute gᵀJ first. Each resulting component is the portfolio change for one factor unit, so it can be reused in many scenarios.
  3. With g=(−2,3), a parallel factor moves quotes by (1,1) and a twist by (−1,1). Factor sensitivities are +1 and +5 thousand euros, respectively.

When the reasoning holds

  • The first-order map uses local differentiability and stable quote-to-factor exposures. Large rate moves can change hedge ratios and introduce curvature.
  • Use the same quote conventions and shock scaling in both matrices. A factor specified as ten basis points must be converted if J describes a one-basis-point factor.
Terms in this lesson
Forward
A contract to exchange an agreed amount at a set price on a future date.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Derivative
A contract whose value depends on something else, such as a currency, an interest rate or a share price.
Percentage point
The difference between two percentages. A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises one percentage point.
How to read the notation
Transpose and multiplication · wᵀr
A transpose turns a column into a row. Multiplying that row by a column pairs their entries and adds the products. Weights (0.75, 0.25) multiplied this way by returns (4%, 8%) produce a portfolio return of 5%.

All notation examples

First example

One lot of A has sensitivities 4 and 1, and B has sensitivities 2 and −3 to the same two shocks. Holding two A and short one B gives 2 × 4 − 2 = 6 on the first row and 2 × 1 − (−3) = 5 on the second.

Read columns as instruments

Picture a desk holding currency forwards and interest-rate futures. Each has several sensitivities. The first column of an exposure matrix can list one instrument’s euro gain from each named standard shock. A negative entry means that particular long position loses under the defined shock.

Put position quantities in the same order as the matrix columns. If A has m rows for risks and n columns for instruments and h contains n quantities, Ah has m entries. Each result uses one row’s shock convention. Checking the dimensions can expose a transposed input before it produces a plausible risk total.

Combine portfolios and shock scenarios

Matrix multiplication is linear. For two desks with position vectors h and k priced under the same conventions, A(h + k) = Ah + Ak. This is how exposures can be aggregated without repricing every combined holding. It requires that the per-unit sensitivity is independent of position size.

If s is a vector of scenario sizes in the defined shock units, the first-order profit estimate is sᵀAh. Compute Ah first to see the portfolio sensitivities, or Aᵀs first to see each instrument’s scenario move. The scalar result agrees because the same products are being added.

Understand the range of the linear map

A matrix of first derivatives approximates a local response. If a stock option is near its strike, doubling a large stock shock may produce more than twice its price gain because the payoff curves. Market impact can also make the cash gained from selling a large holding depend on its size.

Standardizing the shock definitions is an economic decision. One desk might record euros per 1% rise in a currency and another euros per 1 percentage point of interest. Summing their entries in a single unnamed row would hide a unit error that numerical matrix operations cannot find.

Method

Translate a position file

  1. Name row shocks

    Give each row a sign, a factor and a unit, such as a one-basis-point yield rise or a 1% rise in the euro-dollar quote.

  2. Order quantities

    Match contract identifiers to the matrix columns. Set shorts to negative quantities and apply any contractual multiplier once.

  3. Check two routes

    Multiply rows by holdings and then apply scenario sizes. Independently calculate each instrument’s scenario profit and aggregate it to confirm indexing.

Worked case

A desk with three contracts

In euros per lot, sensitivities to one rate shock are (−5, 4, 1). Sensitivities to one currency shock are (2, 0, −3). The desk holds (3, −2, 4) lots. A scenario applies two rate units and minus one currency unit.

Working

  1. The rate exposure is −5 × 3 + 4 × (−2) + 1 × 4 = −19. The currency exposure is 2 × 3 + 0 − 3 × 4 = −6.
  2. The linear scenario gain is 2 × (−19) + (−1) × (−6) = −€32. The negative currency shock offsets €6 of rate losses.
  3. Per-lot scenario changes are (−12, 8, 5). Multiplying by (3, −2, 4) produces −36 − 16 + 20 = −€32, reconciling with the risk-row calculation.

Interpretation

This is a local sensitivity estimate. For a distant stress, reprice the actual contracts to detect nonlinear terms omitted from the matrix.

A common mistake

Two desks both report a rate sensitivity of 10, so combining them gives 20.

How to correct it

Check which quote was bumped, in which direction, how large the bump was and whether figures are in the same currency. Convert to one definition before adding exposures.

Quick check
For that same risk table, what exposures come from long one A and long two B?

The first row gives 4 + 2 × 2 = 8. The second gives 1 + 2 × (−3) = −5. Applying the sign of each sensitivity also captures offsetting risks.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

Two instruments have per-lot sensitivity columns (2, −1) and (−3, 4) in euros. The book holds (5, 2) lots.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate both row exposures and interpret their signs.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Estimate the amount under a scenario of −2 first shocks and +3 second shocks. Confirm by instrument.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Row one is 2 × 5 − 3 × 2 = +4 euros per first shock. Row two is −5 + 4 × 2 = +3 euros per second shock. Both positive quoted shocks would produce local gains.

Part B 4 marks

By row, −2 × 4 + 3 × 3 = +€1. The instruments move −7 and +18 per lot, respectively. Their weighted changes −35 + 36 also give +€1.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A desk computes instrument exposure to market quotes, but risk management shocks underlying economic factors. How are the two maps reconciled?

Compare with a spoken answer

I would compute the quote movement produced by the scenario, then multiply it by the price gradient. For the supplied desk the parallel and twist risk are +€1,000 and +€5,000 per factor unit. In a mixed shock both routes give −€3,000. A disagreement would lead me first to scaling, quote order or currency conversion.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Does a zero parallel sensitivity protect the portfolio from every yield-curve move?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Only the particular parallel direction has been neutralized. A steepening or localized key-rate shock can have a component outside that direction. Check the portfolio gradient against multiple economically meaningful factors and directly reprice larger moves.
