Integration and approximation

An integral aggregates small contributions, as with continuous payments. A Taylor expansion approximates a value change using slope and curvature.

The starting point

A company receiving income continuously needs to add many tiny payments. If c(t) is the annual rate of payment at time t, then over a short interval Δt the company receives roughly c(t) × Δt. An integral is the limit of these sums as the intervals shrink.

When the payment stream must be valued today, multiply each payment by its discount factor before adding it. A different approximation, Taylor expansion, answers a local sensitivity question. For a small change h, keep the slope term f′h and, for a better fit to a curved function, the term ½f″h².

Theory and method

Composite trapezoidal rule and Richardson error estimation

Numerical quadrature · Fundamental theorem of calculus

Treasury knows a seasonal cash-generation rate at observation dates. How could it estimate the integral and diagnose a curvature error?

A project supplies annualized daily revenue rates, while its lender wants accumulated cash between dates. Multiplying the final rate by the whole period treats earlier activity as if it occurred at that higher rate. A trapezoidal approximation gives each interval the average of its two endpoint rates, equivalent to linear interpolation there. Comparing two grids can estimate the remaining integration error when the rate is sufficiently smooth and a consistent error expansion applies.

How the result is built

Th = Σ h[f(tⱼ)+f(tⱼ₊₁)]/2, Iext ≈ T(h/2) + [T(h/2)−Th]/3

f is a discounted cash-flow rate in thousands of euros per year, t dates in years, h equal grid spacing, Th the trapezoid estimate in thousands of euros, and Iext the refined extrapolated integral.

  1. Approximate the rate between adjacent dates with a straight line. The area under that line is width times the average endpoint height.
  2. For a smooth function the leading global trapezoid error on an equal grid is proportional to h². Halving h reduces it by roughly a factor of four.
  3. Subtract the coarse estimate from the fine estimate. One third of that difference estimates the further correction from the fine grid to the limiting integral.

When the reasoning holds

  • A smooth rate and consistent grid support the second-order cancellation. Discontinuous loan drawdowns or known lump sums should be booked separately.
  • The sampled quantity must be the correct integrand. For present value, multiply payment rate by discount factor at each sampled date before integrating.
Terms in this lesson
Discount factor
The price today of one unit of currency payable at a particular future date under specified risk assumptions.
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Convexity
Curvature. In fixed income it describes how a bond’s price sensitivity to yield changes as the yield moves.
Discount rate
Rate used to express a future payment in today’s money, reflecting time and the relevant risk.
How to read the notation
Integral · ∫
An ordinary time integral adds a rate across small time intervals. A constant payment flow of €3 per month for four months gives €12. When the final term is dW instead of dt, the increments being accumulated are random. The stochastic-calculus lectures show how that changes the rules.
A change · Δ
ΔS commonly means the new asset price less the old price. A move from €50 to €53 gives ΔS = €3. In options, delta can also name a sensitivity. A delta of 0.6 means a €1 rise in the underlying predicts about a €0.60 rise in the option, for a small move.
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.
Time and random increments · dt, dW
Over a short interval Δt, a standard Brownian increment has mean zero and standard deviation √Δt. If a year is one time unit, a quarter-year increment has standard deviation 0.5. A drift term scales with elapsed time itself.

All notation examples

First example

A service pays at a rate of 4 + 2t thousand euros per year for one year. With zero interest the average rate is (4 + 6)/2 = 5, so total receipts are €5,000. With positive discounting, later receipts receive less weight.

Adding a continuous stream

Consider a machine that generates cash throughout a day instead of paying a single amount at the end. If the payment rate at time t is c(t) euros per year, a short period Δt generates about c(t)Δt euros. Multiplying by a discount factor D(t) places that contribution at today’s date.

Adding contributions over a partition and making it progressively finer gives the integral of c(t)D(t). A payment rate must be multiplied by an interval measured in matching units. A monthly rate integrated over years needs a conversion before the numbers describe currency.

Calculate and approximate an integral

With constant continuously compounded annual rate r and a constant payment rate c, present value over T years is ∫ c exp(−rt) dt = c[1 − exp(−rT)]/r. The antiderivative follows because differentiating exp(−rt) multiplies it by −r. As r tends to zero the expression tends to cT.

If payment or discount curves are available only at grid points, use strips. A left sum applies the first value over each interval. A trapezoid uses the average of the endpoint values. A trapezoid is exact on a linear integrand but can have error on a curved one.

Use Taylor’s formula for a local change

Taylor expansion approximates a function near one input. For a twice differentiable price, the second-order approximation is P(x + h) ≈ P(x) + P′(x)h + ½P″(x)h². Unlike summing a flow, this asks how one value changes when its argument moves.

For a bond with positive convexity, the quadratic term raises the estimated price under either sign of h. Applying Taylor expansion across a contractual kink can fail even for a moderate shock because the relevant slope can change abruptly on the other side.

Method

Choose the correct approximation

  1. Identify the unknown

    If you need total receipts over time, set up the integral. If you need tomorrow’s price after a shock to today’s inputs, write a local Taylor change.

  2. Track units

    Discounted euros per year times years give euros. In a Taylor term, price per squared unit of shock times squared shock also gives euros.

  3. Check the error

    For integration halve the time step. For a price expansion compare the result with direct repricing, especially around exercise or default thresholds.

Worked case

A declining stream of receipts

A short contract generates cash at €120,000 per year continuously for half a year. Assume certain receipts and a constant annual continuously compounded discount rate of 8%.

Working

  1. The undiscounted amount is €120,000 × 0.5 = €60,000. The integral for present value is 120,000 × [1 − exp(−0.04)]/0.08.
  2. Using exp(−0.04) ≈ 0.960789 gives about €58,815.84. Placing all receipts at the final date would give 60,000 × 0.960789 ≈ €57,647.
  3. A one-strip trapezoid averages initial and final discount factors and multiplies by €60,000, yielding about €58,823.68. Its small excess illustrates the effect of discount-factor curvature.

Interpretation

The continuous formula assumes even payment throughout the period. If the customer actually pays only on an invoice due date, use that payment schedule.

A common mistake

Use the first month’s annual cash-production rate as the sum of the first month’s receipts.

How to correct it

An annual rate has units of euros per year. Multiply by the year fraction and integrate any variation inside that fraction. A payment arriving at the boundary can require separate treatment.

Quick check
A price has slope −400 and second derivative 2,000 with respect to a rate. The rate rises by 0.01. What is its second-order change?

The slope contributes −400 × 0.01 = −4. The curvature contributes ½ × 2,000 × 0.01² = +0.10. Their sum is −3.90 in the original price units.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A project produces cash at annual rate 6t thousand euros over 0 ≤ t ≤ 2 with zero interest. Separately a marked position has slope −30 and curvature 140 per squared unit of its rate input.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Find total project cash and show why taking the terminal rate times two is wrong.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    For a +0.02 shock, find the position’s first- and second-order estimated changes.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

The integral is [3t²] from 0 to 2 = €12,000. The terminal rate is €12,000 per year, reached only at the end. Its average over the two years was €6,000 per year.

Part B 4 marks

The slope change is −30 × 0.02 = −0.60. Adding ½ × 140 × 0.02² = 0.028 gives −0.572. The two questions involve distinct operations even though both require consistent units.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Treasury knows a seasonal cash-generation rate at observation dates. How could it estimate the integral and diagnose a curvature error?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would first convert the rates to euros per year and check for point-in-time payments. For the smooth quadratic example, the one-interval estimate is €6,000 and the two-interval estimate €4,500. The expected quadratic error pattern gives a further −€500 correction. I would verify the pattern with another grid before using it on an unfamiliar project.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if a €2,000 contractual fee arrives exactly at the final date on top of the continuous flow?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Add its €2,000 cash or discounted value as a separate dated item. Treating an instantaneous fee as the terminal rate invents an amount accruing over the preceding interval and makes the answer depend artificially on the grid.
