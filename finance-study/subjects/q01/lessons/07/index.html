Multivariable calculus

A gradient collects first-order sensitivities and a Hessian collects curvature and cross-effects. Match their dimensions to the inputs they describe.

The starting point

A US share held by a euro investor changes value when either its dollar price or the exchange rate changes. With multiple inputs, a gradient collects the local slopes obtained by moving one input at a time. Its entries describe the first-order change in value for a chosen set of small shocks.

The Hessian is the table of second derivatives. Its diagonal records each input’s own curvature. Its off-diagonal entries capture interactions, where changing one input alters sensitivity to another. Full repricing or a second-order approximation can reveal a joint effect that a sum of separate first-order changes misses.

Theory and method

Euler allocation of portfolio volatility

Marginal risk contribution · Euler’s homogeneous function theorem

Why can a long holding have a negative contribution to portfolio volatility?

A hedging asset can gain when the rest of a portfolio falls. Adding a little more may then reduce overall volatility, even if the asset itself is volatile. Volatility is homogeneous of degree one in all positions, so Euler’s theorem decomposes total volatility exactly into position times marginal change. This produces economically meaningful negative contributions for hedges while the entire portfolio’s volatility remains nonnegative.

How the result is built

RCᵢ = wᵢ(Σw)ᵢ/σp, ΣᵢRCᵢ = σp

wᵢ is asset i’s signed exposure, Σ its covariance matrix in squared returns, σp = √(wᵀΣw) positive portfolio volatility, (Σw)ᵢ its covariance with the portfolio, and RCᵢ its component contribution in return-volatility units.

  1. Differentiate wᵀΣw with respect to the i-th holding, obtaining 2(Σw)ᵢ for a symmetric matrix.
  2. The square-root derivative divides this by 2σp. Multiplying by wᵢ gives the component contribution.
  3. Summing wᵢ(Σw)ᵢ/σp yields variance divided by volatility, which is exactly σp. The identity allocates the actual total.

When the reasoning holds

  • Positive portfolio volatility is required for the usual gradient formula. At a zero-risk point it is undefined as written.
  • The derivative varies one position while others remain fixed. A funded switch between two holdings has a marginal effect equal to the difference of their risk gradients.
Terms in this lesson
Derivative
A contract whose value depends on something else, such as a currency, an interest rate or a share price.
Revenue
Amount earned by selling goods or services. The customer may pay later.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
Equity
The owners’ claim after liabilities are deducted from assets. It also means ownership shares.
How to read the notation
A partial derivative · ∂V/∂S
Read it as “how quickly V changes as S moves, holding the other inputs fixed.” If a €0.10 increase in S raises V by about €0.04, the local sensitivity is about 0.4 per euro.
Transpose and multiplication · wᵀr
A transpose turns a column into a row. Multiplying that row by a column pairs their entries and adds the products. Weights (0.75, 0.25) multiplied this way by returns (4%, 8%) produce a portfolio return of 5%.
Subscripts · S₀ and S₁
A small index names the time or item. If S₀ = €40 and S₁ = €42, the price moves from €40 at time 0 to €42 at time 1. A subscript i can label a company instead of a date.
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.

All notation examples

First example

A $100 share at €0.90 per dollar is worth €90. At $105 and €0.92 per dollar it is worth €96.60. The original slopes explain €0.90 × 5 + $100 × 0.02 = €6.50. The remaining €0.10 is the product 5 × 0.02.

Differentiate one input at a time

A firm’s euro revenue can depend jointly on export volume, dollar price and the euro value of one dollar. A partial derivative changes one input while holding the others fixed. The gradient stacks these partial slopes in the order used by the input vector, making each sensitivity’s unit explicit.

For a small shock vector h, the first-order change is the dot product ∇fᵀh. This adds the local effect of each shock. The gradient itself depends on the starting point. If output price rises, the firm can become more exposed to the currency even with unchanged export volume.

Curvature and interactions

The Hessian is a square matrix of second partial derivatives. An off-diagonal entry tells how sensitivity to one input changes when the other input changes. With continuous second derivatives the two orders of differentiation agree. That symmetry matters when implementing a quadratic approximation.

For a small simultaneous shock, add ½hᵀHh to the gradient estimate. For two inputs, expanding the expression gives ½H₁₁h₁² + H₁₂h₁h₂ + ½H₂₂h₂². The cross term appears once because its two symmetric matrix entries meet the factor one half.

Locate what the approximation holds fixed

If a desk reports a currency delta while assuming the dollar share price stays unchanged, it has defined a partial sensitivity. A historical shock observation may move both inputs. Regressing euro value on exchange rates through history can include changes associated with equity markets, so it need not reproduce the partial derivative.

A scenario can preserve economically meaningful links by specifying several simultaneous shocks. Direct repricing provides a check where it is inexpensive. A second-order approximation can be exact for a bilinear payoff but can omit higher-order terms when a payoff involves a third changing factor.

Method

Attribute a joint movement

  1. Specify the value function

    Write V as a function of its independent numeric inputs, including currency quotation direction. Name any fixed quantities such as the number of shares.

  2. Calculate local terms

    Evaluate the full gradient and relevant Hessian entries at the initial observed inputs. Multiply each by matching shocks in original units.

  3. Compare with full repricing

    Subtract the original value from the final value. Reconcile the result to first-order, curvature and interaction contributions.

Worked case

Translation of an export invoice

An exporter expects to sell 100 identical units. Unit price falls from $50 to $48 and euros per dollar rise from €0.80 to €0.85. Assume quantities stay fixed, with no other revenues or costs.

Working

  1. Value is V(P, E) = 100PE. The original value is €4,000 and the final value €4,080. The net improvement is €80.
  2. At the start, sensitivity to dollar price is 100 × 0.80 = 80 and sensitivity to exchange rate is 100 × 50 = 5,000. First-order contributions are −160 and +250, explaining +€90.
  3. The mixed partial derivative is 100. The cross contribution is 100 × (−2) × 0.05 = −€10, giving the exact €80 movement.

Interpretation

A treasurer comparing separate price-only and currency-only scenarios would need the cross term to reconcile the simultaneous case. Its sign follows from shocks pointing in opposite directions.

A common mistake

Multiply each off-diagonal Hessian element by the shocks and then count both contributions at full weight.

How to correct it

Write the entire expression ½hᵀHh or its expanded two-input form. For continuous second derivatives, the two symmetric cross terms together contribute H₁₂h₁h₂ once.

Quick check
A dollar price falls by $3 as euros per dollar rise by €0.04. What joint term is missing from the first-order change for one share?

Euro value is dollar price times euros per dollar, so the cross term is the product of the two changes. Their opposite signs give (−3) × 0.04 = −0.12 euro.

Written practice · 9 marks

Independent exam problem

A claim equals f(x, y) = x²y for positive inputs, initially x = 2 and y = 3. Both rise, by 0.1 and 0.2 respectively.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate the gradient and first-order change.

  2. Part B · 5 marks

    Find the second-order correction and compare with exact repricing.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

The gradient is (2xy, x²) = (12, 4). Multiplying by shocks gives 12 × 0.1 + 4 × 0.2 = 2.0.

Part B 5 marks

H₁₁ = 2y = 6, H₁₂ = 2x = 4 and H₂₂ = 0. The correction is ½ × 6 × 0.1² + 4 × 0.1 × 0.2 = 0.11. Exact change is 2.1² × 3.2 − 12 = 2.112. The remaining 0.002 is h₁²h₂.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why can a long holding have a negative contribution to portfolio volatility?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

The sign comes from covariance with the rest of the portfolio and the sign of our holding. Here the long hedge contributes −2.5 percentage points of volatility, while the first asset contributes +20. Their sum is 17.5 points. I would present standalone volatility separately because it answers how risky the asset would be in isolation.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If both weights double while the covariance matrix is fixed, what happens to these contributions?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Portfolio volatility and each component contribution double. The marginal gradients stay unchanged along a positive scaling of all weights. This is precisely the degree-one homogeneity that permits Euler allocation.
