Functions and logs

Work from a price table to a function. Log returns add across time. Convert them back to compounded simple returns before reporting an investor’s gain.

The starting point

A share bought for €100 and sold for €110 has grown by a factor of 1.10. Subtracting one gives its simple return, 10%. Taking the natural logarithm of the factor gives its log return, about 0.0953. Both describe the same change from different starting scales.

Across consecutive periods the growth factors multiply. Logarithms turn that multiplication into addition. To express a sum of log returns as the investor’s total percentage gain, exponentiate the sum and subtract one. This reasoning assumes positive prices and that any cash distributions are included consistently.

Theory and method

Jensen’s inequality for log wealth

Geometric mean return · Volatility drag

A fund markets a risky strategy with mean monthly return zero. Can its typical compounded growth still be negative?

An investor who leaves money in a fund shares in every percentage loss on the capital actually accumulated. A 50% decline destroys half the account and needs a 100% rise to restore it. Logarithms convert consecutive growth factors into additive units, but the logarithm curves downward. Averaging log outcomes therefore assigns a greater penalty to downside than the average simple return suggests. This matters when turning a distribution of possible monthly returns into a long-horizon growth description.

How the result is built

E[ln G] ≤ ln E[G]

G is the positive gross return, ending wealth divided by starting wealth. ln is the natural logarithm. E averages outcomes using specified probabilities. Both sides are dimensionless log growth.

  1. For any point g greater than zero, concavity places ln G below its tangent ln g + (G−g)/g.
  2. Choose g equal to E[G]. Taking expectations eliminates the tangent’s linear term because E[G−g] is zero.
  3. For equal chances of gross returns 1.50 and 0.50, E[G]=1 while E[ln G]=½ln(0.75)≈−0.14384. The corresponding geometric growth factor is √0.75≈0.8660.

When the reasoning holds

  • Positive gross wealth is needed to take a real logarithm. A possible total loss gives log wealth of minus infinity and changes how growth must be reported.
  • A long-run realized-growth interpretation requires suitable repeatability or ergodicity. The inequality itself applies to a single specified distribution when expectations exist.
Terms in this lesson
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
How to read the notation
Natural logarithm · ln
The log return is ln(ending price ÷ starting price). From €100 to €105 it equals ln(1.05) ≈ 0.04879. Log returns add across successive periods. The ordinary percentage gain for this period is 5%.
Subscripts · S₀ and S₁
A small index names the time or item. If S₀ = €40 and S₁ = €42, the price moves from €40 at time 0 to €42 at time 1. A subscript i can label a company instead of a date.
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.

All notation examples

First example

A fund rises from €100 to €120 and falls to €96. Its two log returns sum to ln(1.20) + ln(0.80) = ln(0.96). The investor ends with 96 cents per euro invested, a 4% loss.

Choosing the object that grows

A share price is a price per unit. A wealth index follows the value of an investment, including distributions under an explicit reinvestment policy. A price chart that drops on an ex-dividend date can give a misleading impression of the shareholder’s loss. Define the investment first, then choose its return measure.

For an initial price P, end price Q and dividend D paid at the end, one share turns P into Q + D. Its gross return G = (Q + D)/P measures ending wealth per unit of beginning wealth. Its simple return is G − 1. The log return is ln(G) and is defined only for G greater than zero.

Deriving additivity across dates

Suppose distributions have been reinvested and W₀, W₁ and W₂ denote wealth on three dates. The first gross return is W₁/W₀ and the second W₂/W₁. In their product W₁ cancels, giving W₂/W₀. The logarithm of a product is the sum of the individual logarithms.

This cancellation requires one continuously linked investment. A large client deposit midway through the period changes wealth without earning a return. Split the history at the cash flow and calculate the change in the value of an invested unit on each side. Dollar-weighted investor performance can answer a different question.

Averaging across securities

For holdings set at the start of one period, portfolio gross return is the weighted arithmetic sum of individual gross returns. The logarithm belongs outside that sum. The logarithm is concave, so the weighted average of the assets’ log returns can differ from the log return on the portfolio.

Two equally sized positions that grow by 50% and shrink by 50% leave total wealth unchanged. Averaging their logs amounts to taking the log of their geometric mean. That value is negative because √(1.5 × 0.5) is less than one.

Method

Build a return bridge

  1. Build a wealth series

    Start from one currency unit, include distributions on a consistent timetable and separate external deposits and withdrawals.

  2. Chain across dates

    Multiply gross factors for the same investment. Add logs if the computation or model is expressed on the log scale.

  3. Report an investor outcome

    Convert the total log change L to exp(L) − 1. Label the period, currency and treatment of cash distributions.

Worked case

A share that pays a dividend

A share costs €40, finishes year one at €42 and pays €2 per share then. Reinvest the dividend immediately at €42 with fractional shares and no costs. All shares finish year two at €39 with no additional payout.

Working

  1. Year one turns €40 into €44, a factor of 1.10. Reinvestment leaves 44/42 shares, including the original holding.
  2. Year two multiplies wealth by 39/42. Ending wealth is €44 × 39/42 = €40.8571, which can also be obtained by valuing all the final shares.
  3. The two-year factor is 1.02143. The two log returns add to ln(1.02143) ≈ 0.02120, equivalent to a 2.143% compound simple return.

Interpretation

The quote alone fell from €40 to €39. Reconciling final share count to wealth reveals exactly how dividend treatment changed the result.

A common mistake

The arithmetic mean of monthly returns is the return an investor earns each month when the strategy is compounded.

How to correct it

Distinguish the sample average from the constant growth rate that reproduces terminal wealth. The latter is the geometric mean of gross returns minus one, subject to the positive-wealth assumption.

Quick check
A price rises from €80 to €100 and then falls to €80. What is its total log return?

The two factors are 1.25 and 0.80. Their product is 1, so their log returns add to ln(1) = 0. The simple percentages use different starting prices.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

An index rises from 200 to 240 and then drops to 210 without distributions. A second investor holds cash until the final subperiod.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate the index’s compound and total log return.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Can the second investor use the index’s 5% as a personal return on cash invested at 240? Explain and calculate.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Its growth factors are 1.20 and 210/240 = 0.875. They multiply to 1.05. The compound simple return is 5% and the log return ln(1.05) ≈ 0.04879. Award credit for both factors and conversion.

Part B 4 marks

No. With all funds invested only at 240, the return on that holding is 210/240 − 1 = −12.5%. Timing changes which factors apply to invested capital. Cash earns zero here if held without interest.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A fund markets a risky strategy with mean monthly return zero. Can its typical compounded growth still be negative?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Yes. I would ask whether the claim concerns one-period expected wealth or long-run compound growth on a continuously invested account. An equal chance of gaining or losing 50% produces mean simple return zero but geometric growth about −13.4% per period under repeated independent draws. The gap follows from the concavity of logarithmic wealth.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Would a portfolio holding half in each of two assets with these opposite returns earn −13.4% that period?

Compare with the follow-up answer

If one gains 50% exactly when the other loses 50% and the initial weights are equal, portfolio gross return is one in each state. Apply the logarithm after combining simultaneous portfolio holdings. Geometric averaging of separate asset outcomes answers a different question.
