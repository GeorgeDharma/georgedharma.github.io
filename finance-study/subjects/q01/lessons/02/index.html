Differentiation

A derivative is a local slope. Compare the derivative of a discounted payment with direct repricing after a small rate change.

The starting point

Imagine nudging an interest rate and watching a bond’s price move. A derivative is the slope at the starting rate. It answers how sensitive the price is to a small change. Multiplying this slope by the rate change gives an approximate price change.

A certain payment of F euros in one year has value P = F/(1 + r) at an annual effective rate r. Its slope is −F/(1 + r)². Rates enter as decimals. One basis point is 0.0001, so even a large-looking price derivative may imply a small price move.

Theory and method

Macaulay duration and modified duration

Discounted-cash-flow weights · Bond price elasticity

How can the weighted payment date of a bond determine its local percentage sensitivity to yield?

A pension team can receive the same total cash sooner or later. A yield rise discounts every future payment more heavily, and late payments accumulate more years of this effect. Macaulay duration combines payment dates using their shares of present value. Differentiating a common-yield price then relates this weighted date to modified duration. It converts a statement about the timing of promised euros into a local estimate of percentage price sensitivity.

How the result is built

Dmac = Σt·PVₜ/P, Dmod = Dmac/(1+y) = −P′(y)/P

t is years to payment under annual compounding, PVₜ its discounted euro value, P the sum of those values, y the decimal annual yield, and P′ price derivative per unit yield. Both durations have year units under this convention.

  1. For each payment Cₜ, PVₜ=Cₜ(1+y)⁻ᵗ. Differentiation gives −tPVₜ/(1+y).
  2. Sum the slopes and divide by total price, yielding a present-value-weighted average of dates divided by 1+y.
  3. Payments whose present values are €40 at one year and €60 at three years imply Dmac=(40+180)/100=2.2. At y=10%, Dmod=2.0, so +5 basis points predicts −0.10%.

When the reasoning holds

  • The displayed relation assumes annual discretely compounded common yield and fixed payments. Other quoting conventions modify the denominator.
  • A yield shock shifts the common discount yield. A twist in the term structure or an embedded prepayment option requires a more detailed sensitivity measure.
Terms in this lesson
Derivative
A contract whose value depends on something else, such as a currency, an interest rate or a share price.
Interest rate
The price of borrowing money or the return for lending it, quoted as a percentage over a stated period.
Bond
A loan that can be bought and sold. Its terms say when the borrower must pay interest and repay the amount borrowed.
Basis point
One hundredth of a percentage point. A rise from 4.00% to 4.01% is one basis point.
Percentage point
The difference between two percentages. A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises one percentage point.
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
How to read the notation
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.
Basis points and percentage points
A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises by one percentage point, or 100 basis points. Its increase relative to the original rate is (5 − 4) ÷ 4 = 25%.
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.
Subscripts · S₀ and S₁
A small index names the time or item. If S₀ = €40 and S₁ = €42, the price moves from €40 at time 0 to €42 at time 1. A subscript i can label a company instead of a date.

All notation examples

First example

A €105 payment is worth €100 at 5%. Its slope is approximately −95.24 per unit of rate. Raising the rate to 5.1% gives an estimated change of −95.24 × 0.001 = −€0.0952. Direct repricing gives €105/1.051 ≈ €99.905.

From a small move to a slope

A risk desk reprices a bond at yield y and at yield y + h. The difference quotient [P(y + h) − P(y)]/h is the average slope over that interval. If it converges as h tends to zero, its limit P′(y) measures the local reaction at the original yield.

The units of the derivative depend on the unit of the input. Expressing y as 0.04 instead of 4 changes derivative units by a factor of 100. Record price per one decimal unit of yield, per percentage point or per basis point before multiplying by a shock.

Differentiate a stream of payments

For fixed promised payments Cₜ at integer dates, P(y) = Σ Cₜ(1 + y)⁻ᵗ under annual compounding and a flat yield. Differentiating each term gives P′(y) = −Σ t Cₜ(1 + y)⁻ᵗ⁻¹. A late payment receives a larger time multiplier, although its present value may be smaller.

Dividing −P′ by P gives modified duration in years under this convention. For a 0.01 yield increase, the approximate percentage loss equals duration times 0.01. This sensitivity holds contractual cash flows fixed. A floating coupon, prepayment or default probability can change with interest rates.

Measure approximation error

A centered estimate uses [P(y + h) − P(y − h)]/(2h). For a smooth function the leading curvature effects cancel in the numerator, which usually improves accuracy over a one-sided estimate of comparable size. Extreme numerical precision can be lost if nearly identical prices are subtracted.

A derivative describes movement along the model’s chosen path. A single yield bump assumes every cash flow is subjected to the same yield change. An observed change in the yield curve can instead steepen it by raising long rates while short rates stay still.

Method

Trace the rate sensitivity

  1. Discount the payments

    Price each fixed payment using the stated compounding convention. Keep track of how many years separate it from the valuation date.

  2. Weight by maturity

    Multiply each discounted cash flow by its date and divide by 1 + y to obtain its contribution to the magnitude of the slope.

  3. Compare with a bump

    Multiply the slope by the yield shock written as a decimal. Reprice every payment at the new yield and record the difference.

Worked case

A two-year redemption

A bond pays €4 in one year and €104 in two years. Its flat annual yield is 4%. Estimate the loss for a 20-basis-point parallel increase and compare it with full repricing.

Working

  1. Present values are 4/1.04 and 104/1.04². They sum to €100. The separate slopes are −4/1.04² and −208/1.04³.
  2. Together they give a slope of approximately −188.61. A yield increase of 0.002 predicts a price change of about −€0.3772.
  3. At 4.2% the bond is worth 4/1.042 + 104/1.042² ≈ €99.624. The small positive difference from the tangent estimate is consistent with upward curvature.

Interpretation

A portfolio manager could describe a slope of −188.61 more intuitively as roughly 1.89 euro cents lost per €100 bond for a one-basis-point rise.

At the board

See the curvature that duration misses

A bond pays a single 100 in two years. Its price at annual effective yield y is 100/(1+y)². At 5% it is 90.7029 and its modified duration is 2/1.05 = 1.9048 years. The duration estimate holds the slope fixed.

Annual yieldExact priceDuration-only estimate from 5%
4%92.455690.7029 × (1 + 1.9048 × 0.01) = 92.4306
5%90.702990.7029
6%88.999690.7029 × (1 − 1.9048 × 0.01) = 88.9753

The exact prices lie above the straight-line estimate on both sides. For this ordinary fixed payment, price is convex in yield. A one-point fall gains about 1.7527 price units, while a one-point rise loses about 1.7033. Quoting “duration 1.9” without the initial price and the yield change would not give a cash loss.

A common mistake

A yield move from 4% to 4.2% is a 5% relative increase, so I should multiply duration by 0.05.

How to correct it

Duration uses the absolute change in decimal yield, here 0.002. A relative percentage increase measures the change compared with the original yield level and has a different denominator.

Quick check
A bond’s local price derivative is −240 and its rate rises by 25 basis points. What is the first-order price change?

Twenty-five basis points equals 0.0025. The local approximation is −240 × 0.0025 = −0.60. A larger move may require accounting for curvature.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A zero-coupon bond redeems €121 in two years at an annual yield of 10%.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Find its price, slope and modified duration.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Estimate the price effect of a 50-basis-point yield fall and explain the direction.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

Price is 121/1.1² = €100. The slope is −2 × 121/1.1³ ≈ −181.818. Dividing its negative by €100 gives modified duration 1.818 years.

Part B 3 marks

Multiply −181.818 by −0.005, obtaining +€0.909. A smaller denominator raises the present value of the unchanged future cash payment. Full repricing would also reflect curvature.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How can the weighted payment date of a bond determine its local percentage sensitivity to yield?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would value each cash payment, divide it by total bond price and weight its date by that fraction. The later €60 payment receives 60% weight and a three-year multiplier. Differentiating the same common-yield formula gives modified duration of two years here. That makes a five-basis-point increase worth about a ten-basis-point price decline locally.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If I quote yields continuously compounded, what happens to the relationship?

Compare with the follow-up answer

With P(y)=ΣCₜexp(−yt), differentiating gives −ΣtPVₜ. The percentage yield sensitivity equals the present-value-weighted payment date directly. Keep the same cash-flow basis and recalculate weights under the stated continuous yield.
