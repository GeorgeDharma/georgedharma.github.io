Linear systems

Solve for holdings that match target exposures. A singular or nearly singular system suggests redundant instruments or an unstable hedge.

The starting point

A risk desk can choose holdings to reach target sensitivities. Write one equation per risk and use the same unknown quantities in every equation. Solving the equations identifies a hedge that satisfies all modeled targets simultaneously. Additional costs, limits or unmodeled risks can still affect the choice.

If two instruments respond in exactly the same proportions, their sensitivity columns are linearly dependent. They cannot independently control two risks. Nearly proportional columns are also troublesome. Small measurement errors can then cause large changes in the hedge quantities, with offsetting positions concealing sizeable gross trades.

Theory and method

Singular value decomposition and minimum-norm hedging

Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse · Condition number

A hedging system produces huge offsetting positions from tiny target changes. How could you determine which hedge direction is causing it?

Two futures can respond almost identically to a market move. Their combined positions easily alter the common exposure, yet changing their small difference requires a large long-short pair. Singular value decomposition rotates the target and holdings into matched risk directions. Each singular value measures how much exposure one unit of holdings can generate in its corresponding direction. A small positive value amplifies errors specifically in that direction.

How the result is built

A = UΣVᵀ, x† = Σᵢ vᵢ(uᵢᵀb)/sᵢ for sᵢ > 0

A is the scaled exposure matrix, U and V contain orthonormal risk and holdings directions uᵢ and vᵢ, Σ has singular values sᵢ, b is the target exposure, and x† the minimum-Euclidean-norm least-squares holdings vector. ᵀ means transpose.

  1. Project b onto each risk direction uᵢ. A position along the matching vᵢ generates sᵢ units of that risk per unit of position.
  2. Divide each projected target by its singular value to find the required position. A zero singular value cannot support that risk direction.
  3. For already rotated singular values 8 and 0.2, a target (8,1) requires positions (1,5). A 0.10 error in the second target shifts the second holding by 0.50.

When the reasoning holds

  • Meaningful conditioning requires economically sensible row and column scales. Dollars, contract lots and arbitrarily scaled risk labels change numerical singular values.
  • The minimum-norm solution assumes that norm is an appropriate measure of position size. Borrow costs, liquidity and integer lots can favor another feasible hedge.
Terms in this lesson
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
First example

Contract A contributes (1, 1) per lot and B contributes (1, −1). A target of (8, 2) requires a + b = 8 and a − b = 2. Adding the equations gives a = 5, and substituting gives b = 3.

Match a whole exposure vector

Suppose an existing position loses under a rate rise and a currency fall. To cancel both sensitivities, choose hedge holdings x satisfying Ax = b, where columns describe candidate contracts and b is the negative of the existing exposure. If only one of the equations holds, some named risk remains.

The set of attainable target vectors is the column space of A. If a new column is a combination of others it adds another trading route to an old exposure, rather than another independent risk direction. A square system has a unique solution for every target precisely when its columns are independent.

Elimination and stability

Subtracting multiples of equations isolates one holding at a time. A small coefficient after elimination can reveal near dependence. An arbitrarily small nonzero determinant allows an exact mathematical solution, but obtaining a meaningful economic hedge can still require quantities so large that small price errors dominate.

Compare how much holdings change when an input moves by a plausible amount. A condition number formalizes the worst relative sensitivity in a chosen norm. Its size depends on units and scaling, so use sensible row and column scales when deciding whether a numerical problem is unstable.

Handle extra instruments or extra risks

If more instruments than independent restrictions are available, several hedges may match the target. Additional criteria such as trading cost, liquidity or gross position size choose among them. If more independent risks than instruments must be matched, an exact hedge might be impossible for this target.

Document the residual exposure and whether constraints such as long-only holdings bind. Least squares can minimize squared deviations after weighting the risks in financially meaningful units. A residual of one unit of a rare currency shock can have a very different consequence from one unit of a tiny yield shock.

Method

Diagnose the hedge

  1. Write the target

    State the signs of existing positions and the exposures a hedge must add. Verify the input columns describe one tradable unit each.

  2. Eliminate an unknown

    Use the equations to solve for holdings. Insert the answer back into every original row to confirm the intended contract quantities.

  3. Perturb and inspect

    Move one target or sensitivity by a realistic amount. Record changes to gross positions and trading cost as well as the resulting net risk.

Worked case

Two nearly identical hedges

Two instruments have exposure columns (1, 1) and (1, 1.01). A target exposure of (1, 1.10) is required. Fractional short positions are allowed for this numerical demonstration.

Working

  1. The equations are x + y = 1 and x + 1.01y = 1.10. Subtracting the first from the second gives 0.01y = 0.10, hence y = 10 and x = −9.
  2. If measurement error changes the target’s second component to 1.11, the new solution is y = 11 and x = −10. A one-hundredth change demands another pair of offsetting trades.
  3. If the second column’s second sensitivity were 1.00 instead, every feasible position with first component 1 would also have second component 1. This target would be unattainable.

Interpretation

Gross size matters. The exact original hedge involves 19 lots in absolute terms for a net first-row exposure of one, which raises implementation and estimation concerns.

A common mistake

A hedge with a mathematically zero residual always has negligible risk.

How to correct it

Its residual is zero for the estimated linear system. Re-estimate sensitivities, apply larger scenarios and check basis risk, liquidity and constraints before describing how much economic uncertainty was removed.

Quick check
Two available contracts have sensitivities (1, 2) and (2, 4). Can any combination generate the target (0, 1)?

The two columns describe the same risk direction. Offsetting them to zero on the first row also produces zero on the second. A contract sensitive to a different direction is needed.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

An analyst can trade contracts with exposure columns (2, 1) and (4, 2). A manager requests a hedge adding exposure (6, 3).

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Give two distinct solutions and explain why there are several.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Can these products generate (6, 4)? Name an operational criterion for choosing among hedges of the original target.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Three first contracts and zero second contracts give (6, 3). One first and one second contract also give (6, 3). The second column is twice the first, so only one independent equation restricts the two holdings.

Part B 4 marks

No. All attainable targets have first component twice the second, and 6 is not twice 4. For the original target, compare per-lot transaction cost, liquidity or position limits for the feasible holdings.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A hedging system produces huge offsetting positions from tiny target changes. How could you determine which hedge direction is causing it?

Compare with a spoken answer

I would scale risks and instruments, inspect the singular values, and project the target onto the weak left singular vectors. Here the small divisor 0.2 explains the large difference trade. I would stress that measurement and compare trading cost against a hedge allowing a transparent residual. The best numerical exact match may create too much practical exposure.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if the target includes a component along a zero singular value?

Compare with the follow-up answer

There is no exact portfolio for that component. The pseudoinverse reproduces the attainable projection and leaves a residual orthogonal to the column space. Report that residual in its economic units and consider a genuinely different hedge instrument.
