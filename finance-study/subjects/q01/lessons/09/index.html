Constrained optimization

Use Lagrange multipliers to impose a budget or exposure constraint. A multiplier measures the local sensitivity of the optimum to relaxing that constraint.

The starting point

A trader may have several ways to execute an order but must still fill the total size. A constrained optimization problem combines a goal, such as minimizing cost, with conditions such as total shares traded. At an interior solution a feasible transfer between venues cannot lower the total cost.

A Lagrange multiplier is the marginal value or cost of changing the constraint, for a stated sign convention. When total cost is minimized subject to x + y = b, write the Lagrangian as cost + λ(b − x − y). Then λ is the local increase in minimum cost per extra unit required.

Theory and method

Lagrangian dual function and weak duality

Shadow price · Lagrange multiplier

A solver proposes a €26 cost for a constrained hedge. How could a desk establish a lower bound without trusting the solver?

A feasible trade gives an upper bound on the minimum possible cost because the optimizer can always choose that trade. To obtain a lower bound, price the constraint with a multiplier and allow all holdings when minimizing the resulting Lagrangian. Every feasible trade pays its original cost once an equality constraint is met. The unrestricted minimum of the Lagrangian can therefore sit below every feasible trade’s cost. Maximizing such lower bounds provides a separate certificate.

How the result is built

g(λ)=infₓ L(x,λ) ≤ p*, L(x,λ)=C(x)+λ[b−aᵀx]

C(x) is euro cost of holdings x, aᵀx=b the required exposure, λ euros per unit of exposure, g the dual lower bound, and p* minimum feasible euro cost. An equality multiplier can have either sign.

  1. For C=x²+y² and x+y=6, L=x²+y²+λ(6−x−y). Minimize separately over x and y to obtain x=y=λ/2.
  2. Substitution gives g(λ)=6λ−λ²/2. At λ=5 this is a valid €17.50 lower bound.
  3. At λ=6 the lower bound is €18. The feasible plan (3,3) costs €18, closing the gap and proving optimality.

When the reasoning holds

  • A lower bound requires taking the global infimum of the Lagrangian, or independently bounding it correctly. An arbitrary local stationary value may fail.
  • For inequality constraints the multiplier sign must match the chosen constraint orientation. The equality example does not impose a nonnegativity restriction on λ.

All notation examples

First example

Suppose modeled execution cost is x² + 2y² and b = 6. Equal marginal costs require 2x = 4y. The allocation x = 4, y = 2 costs 24 units. Each venue’s marginal cost is 8, so requiring another 0.01 unit raises optimal cost by approximately 0.08.

An optimum has a feasible direction of movement

If two trading venues charge costs that rise with quantity, allocating an order entirely to the cheapest first unit can be expensive. Moving a small quantity from one venue to the other preserves total size. The optimum balances the gain on one side of this feasible move with its cost on the other.

Formally minimize C(x, y) subject to x + y = b. Set y = b − x and apply the chain rule to the one-variable cost. Its derivative is Cₓ − Cᵧ. At an interior optimum this vanishes, giving equal marginal costs. If a venue has reached capacity a marginal transfer may be infeasible.

Interpret a multiplier

Define L = C(x, y) + λ(b − x − y). Setting its derivatives in x and y to zero gives Cₓ = λ and Cᵧ = λ. The derivative with respect to λ reinstates the budget constraint. The sign convention here makes λ the extra optimized cost of requiring one more unit locally.

To see why, write the optimized cost as C(x(b), y(b)). Its derivative is Cₓx′ + Cᵧy′ = λ(x′ + y′) = λ, because x + y = b. This calculation assumes differentiability and local regularity. A discrete lot-size rule can instead cause jumps in the value function.

What to inspect at a boundary

If an upper bound on the cheaper venue binds, moving further into it is prohibited. Marginal costs need not match across the two venues. The difference between them can quantify the local value of relaxing the bound, provided the same constraints remain active.

Convexity can turn these local conditions into a global certificate. If transaction costs contain flat fees or discounts with jumps, there may be several qualitatively different allocations. Comparing a few discrete alternatives or solving the actual nonconvex problem then matters.

Method

Price an execution constraint

  1. Write the economic objective

    State whether C measures euros, basis points or another loss. Express the budget and any venue caps in matching trade units.

  2. Find an interior candidate

    Substitute the constraint or solve the Lagrange equations. Check the resulting quantities against all limits before accepting the candidate.

  3. Value a change

    Differentiate optimized cost or use the appropriate multiplier, then re-solve after a small perturbation to check sign and scale.

Worked case

Executing four blocks

Two venues charge modeled variable costs x² and 3y² euros for x and y lot-blocks. The desk must execute four blocks. Initially ignore capacities. Then impose a cap of two blocks at the first venue.

Working

  1. Interior marginal costs 2x and 6y agree when x = 3y. With x + y = 4 this gives x = 3, y = 1 and total cost 9 + 3 = €12.
  2. The budget multiplier is €6 per additional block locally. Substituting x = 3b/4 and y = b/4 gives cost 3b²/4. Its derivative at b = 4 is also 6.
  3. Under x ≤ 2 the constrained optimum is (2, 2) and costs 4 + 12 = €16. Moving a small quantity from the second venue to the first would save approximately 12 − 4 = €8 per extra unit of first-venue capacity.

Interpretation

A budget change and a capacity change refer to different constraints. Their multipliers answer different operational questions even when both have units of euros per block.

A common mistake

Equalize average cost, since equal average cost means the two venues are equally efficient.

How to correct it

A small order transfer is governed by marginal cost. A venue can have a lower average cost on previous units while charging more for the next unit.

Quick check
At a feasible interior allocation, venue A’s marginal cost is 9 and B’s is 6. Which small adjustment lowers cost?

Moving a small amount from A saves about 9 per unit and adds about 6 at B while preserving the required total. The net saving starts at about 3 per unit.

Written practice · 9 marks

Independent exam problem

Two desks contribute x and y units of hedge with cost 2x² + y². Required total is six units and quantities must be nonnegative.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Find the minimum-cost split and budget multiplier.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    If the second desk can provide at most three units, calculate revised cost and marginal benefit of relaxing this cap.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

Interior marginal costs satisfy 4x = 2y, or y = 2x. Hence x = 2, y = 4, cost 8 + 16 = 24 and multiplier 8 in cost units per additional hedge unit.

Part B 4 marks

The feasible choice minimizing cost is x = y = 3, at cost 27. Moving a small quantity from the first to the second reduces cost locally by 4 × 3 − 2 × 3 = 6 per additional unit of second-desk capacity.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A solver proposes a €26 cost for a constrained hedge. How could a desk establish a lower bound without trusting the solver?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would evaluate a dual lower bound and compare it with a separately checked feasible trade. With this quadratic objective λ=6 certifies €18 and the allocation (3,3) achieves it. A reported €26 minimum would be contradicted immediately. A small positive duality gap quantifies remaining objective uncertainty without relying solely on a solver’s success flag.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What does the same λ say about a one-unit increase in the exposure requirement?

Compare with the follow-up answer

It gives the local marginal cost, €6 per unit at b=6. The exact optimal cost function in this example is b²/2, so raising b to 7 adds €6.50. Distinguish the derivative from the full effect of a finite change.
