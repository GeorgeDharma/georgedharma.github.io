Convexity and numerical checks

Examine curvature, feasibility and optimality conditions including inequality constraints. Compare a solver result with a simple feasible benchmark and perturb the inputs.

The starting point

An optimizer’s answer must satisfy the original financial limits and improve the objective. In a convex minimization problem, the objective curves upward in the relevant sense and the line between any two feasible allocations remains feasible. Any local minimum is then global. Strict convexity on the feasible set can give uniqueness.

For a differentiable convex problem with standard regularity conditions, optimality can be checked through feasibility, stationarity, multiplier signs and complementary slackness. The last condition means an inequality’s multiplier can be nonzero only when the constraint binds. Also perturb input estimates to see whether small changes trigger impractically large trades.

Theory and method

Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions and Slater’s condition

Complementary slackness · Strong duality

A constrained optimizer reports a positive multiplier on an unused concentration limit. Which condition fails, and when would the conditions certify global optimality?

A portfolio manager pays a shadow cost for constraints that restrict the optimum. A cap with substantial headroom cannot deliver a first-order benefit if it is relaxed further. Complementary slackness pairs each inequality’s unused margin with its multiplier. Together with feasibility, stationarity and multiplier signs, it tests a candidate allocation and dual certificate. In a convex problem a valid certificate proves global optimality, while a qualification helps ensure one exists.

How the result is built

gᵢ(x) ≤ 0, λᵢ ≥ 0, λᵢgᵢ(x)=0, ∇f + Σᵢλᵢ∇gᵢ + Aᵀν = 0, Ax=b

x is the allocation, f the minimized differentiable convex loss, gᵢ convex inequality functions, λᵢ their multipliers, A and b affine equality data, ν equality multipliers, ∇ the gradient and ᵀ transpose.

  1. Check the allocation against every inequality and equality before inspecting losses. Set λ to zero for an inequality with strict slack.
  2. Combine objective and active-constraint gradients to calculate stationarity. A nonnegative multiplier signals the cost of tightening a bound under the chosen convention.
  3. For f=(w−0.8)² and 0.1≤w≤0.6, w=0.6 has slope −0.4. The upper gradient is +1 so λupper=0.4. The lower bound has 0.5 slack and multiplier zero.

When the reasoning holds

  • Convex objective and inequalities plus affine equalities make a KKT certificate sufficient. In nonconvex problems the same local conditions need not select a global optimum.
  • Under appropriate domain assumptions a point satisfying the affine equalities and every inequality strictly meets Slater’s condition, yielding strong duality and, in usual finite-optimum settings, applicable multiplier guarantees.
Terms in this lesson
Convexity
Curvature. In fixed income it describes how a bond’s price sensitivity to yield changes as the yield moves.
Benchmark
A reference chosen in advance for comparison, such as a market index or an equal-weight portfolio.
How to read the notation
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.
Summation · ∑
The symbol asks you to add the terms for the listed items. For weights of 0.6 and 0.4 and returns of 5% and 2%, ∑ wᵢrᵢ means 0.6 × 5% + 0.4 × 2% = 3.8%. A standalone capital sigma (Σ) can also name a covariance matrix in portfolio equations.
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.

All notation examples

First example

A fund minimizes squared distance (w − 0.70)² to a target weight with 0 ≤ w ≤ 0.60. Its best feasible weight is 0.60, with loss 0.01. At 0.50 the loss is 0.04. The upper limit prevents a further beneficial increase.

Prove a local answer is global

For a convex objective f, moving partway from x to y cannot cost more than the same weighted average of f(x) and f(y). For a convex feasible set that partway point remains an available choice. Suppose a local minimizer had a cheaper feasible competitor. Moving a sufficiently small distance toward it would already lower cost, contradicting local optimality.

Strict convexity goes further by putting an interior mixture strictly below the weighted costs for distinct points. It ensures at most one minimizer over a convex set. Existence also matters. An unconstrained function can approach its infimum indefinitely without attaining it.

Read the optimality conditions

For inequality limits gᵢ(x) ≤ 0 form L = f + Σλᵢgᵢ, adding equality terms if present. At a regular optimum the primal variables satisfy the limits, multipliers for inequalities are nonnegative, the Lagrangian is stationary in x and each product λᵢgᵢ is zero.

The product rule is complementary slackness. A strictly unused limit has zero multiplier. A binding limit can also have zero multiplier, so equality at the boundary alone does not establish an economic cost. For differentiable convex problems with appropriate constraint qualifications these conditions can certify global optimality.

Separate numerical tolerance from risk appetite

A solver’s stopping rule accepts small residuals. Scale each constraint and translate its residual back into economic units. A residual of 0.0001 may represent a minor rounding difference or a material position in a large portfolio. Risk appetite determines which deviations are acceptable in real implementation.

Stress uncertain expected returns and covariance estimates separately from solver accuracy. Tightening tolerances can verify that an allocation really solves the stated model. It cannot rescue a model whose inputs change the optimal allocations dramatically from one sample to the next.

Method

Audit an optimizer

  1. Find a feasible benchmark

    Use a simple compliant candidate and calculate its objective under the identical convention. A reported minimum worse than it needs investigation.

  2. Examine the certificate

    Check the signs, stationarity, constraints and complementary products. Convert budget and exposure discrepancies back to cash or positions.

  3. Perturb the economic inputs

    Re-run with a modest forecast or covariance change and record the change in holdings, turnover and realized risk estimates.

Worked case

Pricing a portfolio cap

A fund minimizes (w − 0.8)² subject to w ≤ 0.5 and w ≥ 0. Let the upper inequality be w − 0.5 ≤ 0 and the lower inequality be −w ≤ 0. Units of w are fractions of the portfolio.

Working

  1. At w = 0.5 the objective equals 0.09. The lower constraint has slack, so its multiplier is zero. The upper constraint binds.
  2. The objective slope is 2(0.5 − 0.8) = −0.6. Stationarity gives −0.6 + λ = 0, hence λ = 0.6 for the upper limit. All four optimality conditions are satisfied.
  3. Relaxing the cap by 0.01 predicts a loss reduction near 0.006. Exact loss at 0.51 is 0.29² = 0.0841, a reduction of 0.0059. The approximation is local.

Interpretation

The value function measures squared tracking loss. Reporting the multiplier as euro profit without a defined conversion from tracking loss would misstate its meaning.

A common mistake

A binding cap always has a strictly positive shadow price.

How to correct it

If an unconstrained optimum happens to equal the cap, its objective slope can already be zero. Check stationarity and perturbation, then report the marginal value in its proper units.

Quick check
A solver reports a weight of 0.62 under a hard cap of 0.60 because it fits the objective more closely. What is the appropriate conclusion?

Feasibility is part of the problem definition. A better unconstrained objective cannot compensate for a material hard-cap violation. Compare a corrected solution with feasible alternatives.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

Minimize (w − 0.35)² for a single portfolio weight subject to 0.2 ≤ w ≤ 0.7. Consider changing the upper cap to 0.30.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Give the first solution, constraint status and uniqueness argument.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Find the new optimum, objective and upper-limit multiplier under w − 0.30 ≤ 0.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

The unconstrained zero-loss point is w = 0.35 and lies strictly within both bounds. Both multipliers are zero. The squared loss is strictly convex, giving a unique minimizer.

Part B 4 marks

The best feasible weight is 0.30. Loss is 0.05² = 0.0025 and the objective slope is −0.10 there. Stationarity gives upper multiplier +0.10, measuring local loss reduction from relaxing the cap per unit of weight.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A constrained optimizer reports a positive multiplier on an unused concentration limit. Which condition fails, and when would the conditions certify global optimality?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

A positive lower-floor multiplier with half a unit of headroom contradicts complementary slackness. I would also check the solver’s sign convention and scaling. For a convex, differentiable formulation with affine equalities a feasible KKT certificate proves optimality. Slater’s condition helps ensure that such multipliers and zero duality gap are available under the usual assumptions.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Does a binding constraint force its multiplier to be strictly positive?

Compare with the follow-up answer

No. If the unconstrained target already lies exactly at the cap, the objective gradient can be zero there and λ can also be zero. Complementary slackness allows a zero multiplier on a binding constraint. A small perturbation helps interpret marginal economic value.
