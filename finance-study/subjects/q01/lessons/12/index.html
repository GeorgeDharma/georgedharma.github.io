Invert a market quote with a safeguarded root solver

Translate a market calibration into a signed residual with an admissible domain.

Theory and terminology

Safeguarded numerical root finding

bisection · Newton’s method · implied yield

What you should be able to explain

  • Translate a market calibration into a signed residual with an admissible domain.
  • Combine a guaranteed interval with Newton acceleration and distinguish price error from yield error.

Turn the quote into an equation

A conventional yield is a single discount rate that matches promised cash flows to an observed price. With annual end-of-year cash flows Cₜ and annual effective yield y, price is the sum of Cₜ/(1+y)ᵗ. The admissible domain is y greater than −1. Compare a dirty price, including accrued interest, with the same entitlement to cash flows used in the model.

Define the residual as computed price minus observed price. For positive future cash flows the derivative of each term is negative. The total price falls strictly with yield. Continuity and opposite signs at two candidate yields therefore establish existence, and monotonicity establishes uniqueness. Neither conclusion requires the current quoted yield as a starting guess.

Trade guaranteed progress for speed

Bisection evaluates the midpoint of a sign-changing interval and retains the half whose ends still have opposite signs. Record an exact zero immediately. Every accepted bisection halves the search width. Newton iteration uses local slope to jump towards the root and can converge much faster near a simple root.

Keep both ideas in one solver. Try a Newton proposal inside the domain and bracket when the slope is well determined. Fall back to a midpoint if the proposal is outside, barely shrinks the interval or has unreliable slope. Update the bracket from the accepted proposal’s sign. The exact halving bound applies to bisection steps, regardless of how often a solver proposes Newton steps.

Know what the output certifies

A residual in dollars answers a valuation question. A yield interval answers a rate question. For a flat price curve a tiny change in price can mean a large rate change. Quote precision ultimately limits the useful accuracy of an inferred yield. Test both with tolerances appropriate to the trade.

Sign changes in future cash flows can produce multiple admissible roots. For example a project with cash flows −100 today, +230 in one year and −132 in two has zero NPV at both 10% and 20%. Multiplying its equation by (1+y)² gives −100(1+y)²+230(1+y)−132=0. A solver report alone cannot select an economically unique IRR.

Method

Build the result

f(y)=P(y)−Pquote; y_new=y−f(y)/f′(y); width_n=(b−a)/2ⁿ

P and Pquote are prices per 100 face. y, a and b are annual decimal yields. f′ measures price change per unit decimal yield. n counts pure bisections of the original bracket.

  1. A continuous residual with f(a)f(b)<0 crosses zero inside [a,b]. With positive bond cash flows that root is unique.
  2. Approximate the residual by its tangent f(y)+f′(y)Δy. Setting this to zero yields the Newton adjustment −f(y)/f′(y).
  3. After n bisections the new interval has width (b−a)/2ⁿ. Its midpoint is at most half that width from any enclosed root. Price error is checked separately by repricing.
Worked case

Calibrate a two-year bond

A bond pays 4 after one year and 104 after two. Its dirty price today is 95 per 100 face. There is no stub period or accrued interest. Use an annual effective yield and an initial bracket of 4% to 8%.

Working

  1. P(4%)=100.000 and P(8%)=92.867. Residuals +5.000 and −2.133 bracket a solution.
  2. At 6%, the residual is +1.333. The root is above 6%. At 7%, it is −0.424. The root lies between 6% and 7%.
  3. At 7%, f′=−4/1.07²−208/1.07³=−173.284 per unit yield. Newton proposes 0.07−(−0.42405)/(−173.284)=0.067553. The proposal stays inside the bracket.
  4. Repricing at the unrounded proposal gives a residual about +0.00145 per 100. The new bracket ends there and at 7%. Continued solution gives y=6.7561%.

Decision and interpretation

Both the price and yield checks matter for a production report. With a whole-cent price quote, extra solver decimals are a measure of computational precision. The market quote still has whole-cent precision.

Quick check
A monotone bond's starting bracket is 80 basis points wide. After three pure bisections, what is the worst midpoint error?

Three halvings leave a 10-basis-point-wide interval. The midpoint lies at most 5 basis points from the enclosed root.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

A three-year zero-coupon bond pays 100 and quotes 87.63. Check whether [4%,5%] brackets its yield. How many pure bisections guarantee a midpoint within 0.1 basis points?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked solution
  1. Model prices are 100/1.04³=88.900 and 100/1.05³=86.384. Opposite residual signs and strictly positive redemption imply exactly one root in the interval.
  2. The original interval is 100 basis points wide. After n bisections midpoint error is at most 50/2ⁿ basis points. Eight steps give 0.1953 bp and nine give 0.0977 bp.
  3. Nine bisections suffice for the stated yield tolerance. Direct inversion gives (100/87.63)^(1/3)−1, about 4.50%. Match price tolerance to the quoted cent as a separate check.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How can a pricing team tell whether a reported yield actually solves the quoted bond price?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I reprice it using the same cash flows, calendar and compounding. I require a small price residual and a yield interval narrow enough for our use. A sign-changing bracket and a monotone price curve establish which solution we found.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Would a solver failure to find a sign change prove the quote is impossible?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Only the chosen interval was tested. For a monotone bond, check entitlement, accrued interest and compounding, then widen within the valid domain. For nonmonotone cash flows a root may even touch zero without changing sign.
