Dates and time zones

Distinguish order time, execution time, publication time and settlement. The date assigned to a record determines when a learner could have used it.

The starting point

A trade can be ordered on Monday, executed that afternoon and settled later. A news release may reach the public after that market closes. Recording all these events as Monday erases the sequence that a historical trading simulation needs.

Keep separate timestamps for the event, its publication and the time data became available. Convert to a common time zone while retaining the market’s local calendar for session boundaries. A simulation may use an observation only once it was available before the simulated decision.

Theory and method

Point-in-time as-of selection

Data vintage · Information set

How would you choose the value a backtest could have used at a past close when today’s database contains a later correction?

A market close is a time at which a simulated decision must already be fixed. A February economic number can have a first release in March and a correction in April. Filtering by February’s period label alone allows the April correction to enter a March simulation. Preserve both the observation period and the timestamp when each version became available to the simulated desk. Choose the newest version that had actually arrived before the decision cutoff.

How the result is built

SELECT value FROM releases WHERE series_id = ? AND period = ? AND available_at < ? ORDER BY available_at DESC, revision_seq DESC LIMIT 1

The three SQL parameters are series identity, observation period and the UTC decision cutoff, respectively. available_at is the desk’s known receipt time. revision_seq breaks genuine same-time ties by stable official version ordering.

  1. For a March 15 close at 15:00 UTC, a February observation of 101 arriving March 10 qualifies. The corrected 104 arriving March 18 is later and fails the cutoff.
  2. Run the query for March 20 at the same local instant and both versions qualify. Descending availability selects 104.
  3. Store the March 15 result’s version ID and market calendar alongside the numeric value. Re-running that historical snapshot must still return 101 after the correction enters the database.

When the reasoning holds

  • Timestamps must use a common comparable representation such as fixed-width ISO UTC with identical precision. Cross-zone strings with different offsets cannot be compared lexicographically without normalization.
  • The exercise uses a strict deadline, so a release exactly at auction cutoff is ineligible. If the order submission rule differs, use the genuine commitment time explicitly.
Terms in this lesson
Settlement
The final transfer of cash or securities needed to complete a transaction.
Look-ahead bias
Accidental use of information in a test before a decision-maker could have known it.
First example

Assume a Tokyo market closes at 15:30 local time on the day in question. A company releases earnings at 16:00 Tokyo time, or 07:00 UTC. A model using the announcement to simulate a trade at that day’s 15:30 close uses information from 30 minutes in the future.

Record at least two clocks

A retailer reports December sales in a press release in February. December identifies the period to which the information relates. February identifies when an investor could first read the release. A trading model using those sales must look at availability, whereas an economic chart may legitimately group the value by its December reference period.

An event can have order time, execution time, settlement date, official publication time, and actual system arrival time. A payment forecast may care about settlement while a historical strategy must respect both release and arrival if the data feed was delayed. Preserve the original timestamps and define the decision timestamp against which availability is tested.

Offsets and exchange calendars

A timestamp without an offset can be ambiguous, especially when clocks move back and the same local time appears twice. Store a time-zone-aware instant, typically also normalized to UTC, and the market time zone needed to assign trading sessions. A fixed offset does not necessarily hold over an entire year in markets observing daylight saving.

A date in UTC can correspond to the next calendar day in Asia or the prior one in the Americas. A market holiday and an unexpected early close both change the next executable session. Use the stated exchange calendar or explicit exercise schedule. Calendar-day arithmetic alone cannot identify an executable auction time.

Use point-in-time data

Revised economic numbers can differ from first releases. A row may have one observation period and multiple published versions. At each historical decision, choose the most recent version actually available then, with a tie rule for records arriving exactly at the decision cutoff. This is often called an as-of selection.

Look-ahead bias occurs when an analysis benefits from data impossible to know at the simulated time. The bias need not come from an obvious future return. An index constituent list determined later, a revised industry label or backfilled data vendor correction can supply future information just as easily.

Method

Reconstruct an information set

  1. Normalize times

    Convert local release and trading times to the same real instant using stated offsets. Retain the local exchange calendar.

  2. Apply availability

    Select only records whose release and required ingestion time precede the strategy cutoff. Order versions by when they actually became known.

  3. Assign a tradable decision

    Use the first eligible trading event under the declared decision rule, making holidays, early closes and exact cutoff handling explicit.

Worked case

A release between two closing auctions

On a hypothetical day, Market E closes at 16:00 local with offset UTC+1. Market W closes at 16:00 local with offset UTC−4. A release is public at 15:30 UTC and reaches the analyst at 15:35 UTC. Both markets next open on the following business day.

Working

  1. E’s close is 15:00 UTC and W’s close is 20:00 UTC. At E’s close the announcement has not occurred, much less arrived.
  2. At W’s close the release has been in the system for four hours and twenty-five minutes. A close-only rule could use it there, subject to a feasible order cutoff.
  3. For E, its next eligible close is on the following business day. Assigning the release to a shared date field would wrongly suggest simultaneous eligibility.

Interpretation

If W’s auction actually requires instructions before 15:20 UTC in a special session, its eligibility also changes. The relevant deadline is when the decision must be committed.

At the board

Match a European close to the correct UTC observation

A fictional data feed records a Paris observation at 16:30 local time on two sides of the March 2025 clock change. A second feed timestamps prices in UTC. Buying both at “16:30” joins different instants.

See the reproducible code
from datetime import datetime, timezone
from zoneinfo import ZoneInfo

paris = ZoneInfo("Europe/Paris")
for date in ["2025-03-28", "2025-03-31"]:
    local = datetime.fromisoformat(date + "T16:30").replace(tzinfo=paris)
    print(local.astimezone(timezone.utc).strftime("%H:%M UTC"))

The observations are 15:30 UTC and 14:30 UTC. Assign the documented source zone before converting. A date-only join still needs a session rule. If a release arrived after a market close, that close could not contain its information.

A common mistake

A series of revised sales is joined to trade dates using the period-end date as its availability date.

How to correct it

Join on release or ingestion timestamps and preserve vintages. Only then attach the reporting-period label for description.

Quick check
What is the earliest eligible session under a rule allowing trades only at a session close after publication?

The same-day close precedes publication on the stated timeline. The next available session close is the first one satisfying this rule.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A central bank’s reference month is March. A first estimate of 102 reaches a desk on 5 April at 14:05 UTC. A revision of 104 arrives on 2 May at 09:00 UTC. A market closes at 16:00 local UTC+2, with decisions immediately before the close.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Which value is available for the 5 April close and for the 6 April close, assuming both are trading days and no earlier March value exists?

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    What should an as-of query return for a 3 May decision? Explain why overwriting the April value causes a historical error.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Both closes occur at 14:00 UTC. On 5 April the first figure arrives five minutes too late, so no March estimate is eligible. On 6 April the desk can use 102.

Part B 4 marks

It should return 104, the latest release then known. Overwriting 102 would make the April simulation appear to know a revision released on 2 May, introducing look-ahead bias.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How would you choose the value a backtest could have used at a past close when today’s database contains a later correction?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would filter first on the desk’s availability timestamp and only then choose the newest eligible vintage. March 15 therefore uses 101 and March 20 uses 104. I would store the decision cutoff, time-zone convention and release identifier with each derived signal. That lets another analyst reproduce the March decision even after the provider has corrected its February numbers.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The correction was public at 14:58, but the desk ingested it at 15:02 for a 15:00 order cutoff. Which timestamp applies?

Compare with the follow-up answer

For a simulation of this desk’s executable process I would use actual system availability, 15:02. The correction misses the 15:00 cutoff even though another trader could have read it earlier. A separate study of publicly available information may use 14:58 if it explicitly models that information set.
