Spreadsheet references

Use relative and absolute references to copy calculations across periods. Check a total in a second way to expose a shifted range.

The starting point

A formula copied down a worksheet can either follow the row or keep pointing to a particular cell. A relative reference moves as the formula is copied. Dollar signs lock a row, a column, or both.

Use moving references for row-specific information and a fixed reference for a common assumption. Check the last copied formula and reconcile the total using an independent calculation. Two columns of plausible numbers can still contain a shifted reference.

Theory and method

Exposure-weighted aggregation using SUMPRODUCT

Weighted average · Relative and absolute references

A portfolio report shows an average default probability of 5%, yet its expected defaulted balance suggests 7.4%. How would you identify which denominator the spreadsheet used?

A portfolio has several borrowers with different balances. An average based on one row per account measures the default probability of a randomly selected account under the model. The proportion of the total euros expected to default instead gives each euro equal weight. A spreadsheet can calculate that quantity row by row or with a weighted sum. Reconcile both methods to see whether a formula copied down has locked the wrong cell.

How the result is built

=SUMPRODUCT(B2:B4,C2:C4)/SUM(B2:B4)

B2:B4 holds exposures in euros. C2:C4 holds decimal probabilities over the same horizon. SUMPRODUCT adds the exposure times probability for every matching row, and SUM returns gross exposure. The result is a decimal exposure-weighted rate.

  1. Rows are €10,000 at 1%, €10,000 at 5% and €80,000 at 9%. The equal-account mean is (1% + 5% + 9%)/3 = 5%.
  2. Row expected balances are €100, €500 and €7,200. Their sum is €7,800 on €100,000, giving 7.8%. A reported 7.4% still fails the detailed reconciliation.
  3. To show each contribution to the reported rate, use =B2*C2/SUM($B$2:$B$4) and copy down. The fixed denominator stays at €100,000 and the three contributions sum to 7.8%.

When the reasoning holds

  • Each probability concerns the same horizon, and balance at default is assumed equal to the stated exposure. Facilities drawn down before default would require revised exposures.
  • The report asks for expected defaulted principal. Estimating expected credit loss additionally needs recovery or loss severity and possibly timing.
How to read the notation
Summation · ∑
The symbol asks you to add the terms for the listed items. For weights of 0.6 and 0.4 and returns of 5% and 2%, ∑ wᵢrᵢ means 0.6 × 5% + 0.4 × 2% = 3.8%. A standalone capital sigma (Σ) can also name a covariance matrix in portfolio equations.

All notation examples

First example

Rows 2 and 3 contain prices of €80 and €120 in column B. Cell F1 holds a 5% fee. Enter =B2*$F$1 in C2 and copy down. Fees of €4 and €6 sum to €10, matching 5% of the €200 combined price.

Understand how a copied reference moves

A spreadsheet formula is written from a location. In C5, a reference to B5 points one column to the left in the same row. When copied down, that relative relationship stays the same. If a rate must always come from one fixed assumption, ordinary relative copying can silently direct later rows to an empty cell or another assumption.

A dollar sign anchors the component it precedes. $B5 fixes column B but lets the row move. B$5 fixes row 5 but lets the column move. $B$5 fixes both. A mixed reference is especially useful when a row supplies rates and a column supplies exposures across a two-dimensional scenario table.

Separate error detection from formatting

A beautifully formatted matrix can contain a shifted range. Check corner cells by reasoning independently about the expected result, then examine the formulas at the first, middle and final positions. When rows are added within a fixed summation range, check whether the range expands. A blank separator row can break an assumed contiguous region.

Reconcile totals another way. For exposures sharing one rate, sum the exposures and multiply once. If different rates apply, calculate each exposure times its own rate and sum those products, conceptually a weighted sum. A simple arithmetic average of rates multiplied by total exposure works only under suitable equal-weight or coincidental conditions.

Types and signs are also inputs

A displayed “5%” can be a numeric value of 0.05 formatted as a percentage or a text string imported from a report. Spreadsheets may coerce text in some functions and ignore it in others. Confirm numeric types and inspect the stored value before concluding a formula is wrong.

Use one sign convention for receipts, expenses and balances, and label amounts in euros, thousands or millions. A reconciliation between a €2m statement and a 2,000 numeric input in thousands is legitimate only after conversion. Avoid rounding every intermediate value to the displayed decimals unless the contract requires it. Formatting a result and changing its stored precision are separate actions.

Method

Build a two-way fee table

  1. Lay out the axes

    Put principal amounts in column A and rates in row 3. Explicitly mark the units and check rates are stored as numbers.

  2. Anchor deliberately

    Enter =$A4*B$3 at B4. The exposure column and the rate row are fixed while the relevant exposure row and rate column shift.

  3. Test the corners

    Compare the smallest and largest exposure-rate combinations with independent multiplication, then check each column total against aggregate exposure times its rate.

Worked case

A guarantee-fee matrix

A4 contains €20,000, A5 €30,000 and A6 €50,000. B3 stores 1%, C3 1.5% and D3 2%. The insurer wants a full-year fee matrix without compounding or fixed charges.

Working

  1. The B4 formula =$A4*B$3 produces €200. Copied down and right to D6 it becomes =$A6*D$3, yielding €1,000.
  2. The entire 1.5% column should total €100,000 × 0.015 = €1,500. The three rows produce €300, €450 and €750.
  3. If D6 mistakenly uses =$A$4*D$3, it returns €400 by repeatedly using the first exposure. A column total or bottom-corner check catches the substitution.

Interpretation

Weighted sums become necessary when each contract has a different rate within a single portfolio rather than when one scenario rate applies to an entire column.

A common mistake

The analyst locks both coordinates of each reference, then copies one identical result everywhere.

How to correct it

Identify which axis should follow each copied formula. Inspect D6 after copying from B4 and verify both its row-specific exposure and column-specific rate.

Quick check
After copying the formula from C2 to C3, which formula should appear?

The relative price reference moves from B2 to B3. Both parts of the fixed fee reference keep pointing to F1.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A10 and A11 hold exposures €8,000 and €12,000. B9 and C9 store stress loss rates 4% and 7%. A fee-free scenario grid begins at B10.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Write the formula to copy across and down, its formula in C11, and its result there.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Provide an independent check for column C and diagnose an erroneous formula =$A$10*C$9 copied down that column.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

At B10 use =$A10*B$9. At C11 it becomes =$A11*C$9 and yields €12,000 × 0.07 = €840.

Part B 4 marks

Expected column C total is (€8,000 + €12,000) × 0.07 = €1,400. The erroneous anchor uses €8,000 twice, yielding €560 + €560 = €1,120 and understating the total by €280.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A portfolio report shows an average default probability of 5%, yet its expected defaulted balance suggests 7.4%. How would you identify which denominator the spreadsheet used?

Compare with a spoken answer

I would calculate the expected defaulted euros independently before accepting either report. The three rows yield €7,800 on €100,000, so the balance-weighted result is 7.8%. The simple 5% answers an account-count question. The claimed 7.4% still needs tracing, perhaps to a shifted range or an omitted balance. I would inspect both the first and the last copied formula.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If the €80,000 account splits into eight €10,000 contracts at the same probability, what happens to each average?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The exposure-weighted rate remains 7.8% because balances and probabilities on the same euros are unchanged. The equal-contract mean becomes (1 + 5 + 8×9)/10 = 7.8%. The number of rows has changed, exposing why the two original measures described different units.
