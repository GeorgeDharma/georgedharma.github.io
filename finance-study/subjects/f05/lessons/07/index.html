Prices and returns

Separate dividends, splits and currency conversion. Agree whether a series records price changes alone or total investor returns.

The starting point

A share can distribute cash or split into more shares while its quoted price changes. For an investor, the relevant calculation concerns total wealth across all shares and cash received. A price series alone may miss part of the return.

For one share without a split, total return over one period is (closing price + cash dividend − starting price) divided by starting price, before fees and taxes. With a split, update the number of shares held. To report in another currency, value both the starting and ending wealth using their respective exchange rates.

Theory and method

Total return decomposition with exchange-rate interaction

Local-currency return · Currency translation return

A foreign asset earns 10% in local currency and its currency falls 8% against the investor’s home currency. Why does the investor earn less than 2%?

A home investor owns a changing amount of foreign wealth and must value it at a changing exchange rate. The currency loss applies to the full local ending wealth, including the local investment gain. Multiplying the two growth factors captures that interaction. If the foreign asset paid dividends, include them in local terminal wealth under the stated reinvestment or conversion timing. Then multiply by the appropriate currency ratio to obtain the home outcome.

How the result is built

r_home = r_local + r_FX + r_local r_FX.

r_home is decimal investor return in home currency, r_local the local total return including stated distributions and r_FX the percentage change in home currency per one unit of foreign currency. Subscripts label components.

  1. Start with €100 of translated wealth. Local assets grow by factor 1.10. One foreign currency unit then buys only 0.92 times its initial euros.
  2. Home growth is 1.10 × 0.92 = 1.012, giving 1.2%. The components are +10%, −8% and the interaction 0.10 × (−0.08) = −0.8 percentage points.
  3. The simple sum of local and currency percentages overstates ending euro wealth by €0.80 per €100. An independent opening-to-closing wealth reconciliation confirms the discrepancy.

When the reasoning holds

  • Both local return and exchange-rate change cover exactly the same horizon. A dividend converted earlier needs its own dated FX rate and may require separate cash tracking.
  • The FX quote uses home currency per foreign unit. A quote in the opposite direction must be inverted before using its return.
Terms in this lesson
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
How to read the notation
Subscripts · S₀ and S₁
A small index names the time or item. If S₀ = €40 and S₁ = €42, the price moves from €40 at time 0 to €42 at time 1. A subscript i can label a company instead of a date.
Natural logarithm · ln
The log return is ln(ending price ÷ starting price). From €100 to €105 it equals ln(1.05) ≈ 0.04879. Log returns add across successive periods. The ordinary percentage gain for this period is 5%.
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.

All notation examples

First example

An investor pays €100 for one share. In a two-for-one split it becomes two shares, each finishing at €52. The investor collects a €1 dividend on each new share. Final wealth is 2 × (€52 + €1) = €106 and total return is 6%.

Reconstruct an investor’s wealth

The quoted closing price is a price per share, while the investor owns a number of shares and may receive cash distributions. Investor wealth can change because price moves, share count changes in a split, dividends arrive or the exchange rate changes. Begin with one shareholding and follow each component to the reporting date.

A price return excludes cash dividends but should still account for splits when measuring an investor’s price performance. A total return includes distributions under the specified reinvestment assumption. If the data supplier’s adjusted series already embeds a dividend or split, applying the event again double counts it. Read the field definition and check a known event manually.

Convert currencies as growth factors

Suppose a home investor records the home-currency value of one foreign unit as e. Starting wealth in home currency equals starting foreign wealth times e₀. Ending wealth equals ending foreign wealth times e₁ when distributions are converted at the end in this simplified case. The home growth factor is local total-return factor times e₁/e₀.

Adding local and currency percentage changes is only an approximation because their gains interact. If the exchange rate is quoted in the inverse direction, invert it before applying this rule. A dividend converted immediately at an earlier date uses its own exchange rate and cannot always be combined into a single end-date conversion.

Chain a time series consistently

Daily simple returns combine multiplicatively. For positive beginning and ending wealth, log return is the natural logarithm of the growth ratio. Log returns add over consecutive periods, while simple returns do not. Either representation must use a consistent total-versus-price basis, split treatment and currency throughout the chain.

Large apparent losses on an ex-split date or gains around a data vendor transition deserve inspection against raw events. Distinguish a genuine price move from a change in instrument identifier, quote currency or decimal scale. Maintain both raw event data and calculated adjustments so the claimed return can be reproduced.

Method

Audit a total return

  1. Trace share count

    Update units for splits or consolidations and value all units using the correct post-event price.

  2. Add distributions

    Identify dividends, their applicable shares, and whether they are reinvested or held as cash. Decide when their currency converts.

  3. Compare in home units

    Measure opening and closing total wealth on a consistent currency basis and reconcile local growth with the currency factor.

Worked case

A foreign share splits

A euro investor holds one share trading at $80 when $1 buys €0.90. It splits two-for-one. At period-end both new shares trade at $43 and pay $1 each in cash, converted then. At that point $1 buys €0.85.

Working

  1. Starting wealth is $80 × €0.90 = €72. Ending foreign wealth is 2 × ($43 + $1) = $88, a local total return of 10%.
  2. Ending wealth in euros is $88 × €0.85 = €74.80. Home return is €74.80/€72 − 1 ≈ 3.89%.
  3. Equivalently 1.10 × (0.85/0.90) − 1 ≈ 3.89%. The dollar’s weakening reduces part of the foreign-currency gain.

Interpretation

A per-share comparison of $43 versus $80 would miss the extra share. The local price-only return on the two shares was $86/$80 − 1 = 7.5%.

A common mistake

A dividend is added to a total-return index whose published methodology already reinvests that distribution.

How to correct it

Trace one dividend event and compare the raw price, distribution and adjusted factor. Apply the corporate action once at the correct stage.

Quick check
Ignoring the dividends but treating the split correctly, what is the price-only return?

The two shares together are worth €104 at the end. Compared with the original €100, the price-only gain is 4%.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A home-currency investor buys two shares at £30 when £1 = €1.20. Each pays £1 at period-end and finishes at £32, with £1 then worth €1.10. Distributions convert at period-end and there are no fees.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate local price return, local total return and final home-currency wealth.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Calculate home total return and verify it using growth factors. Explain whether simply adding the two percentage changes gives the exact value.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Local price return = 32/30 − 1 ≈ 6.67%. Local total return = 33/30 − 1 = 10%. Ending foreign wealth is 2 × £33 = £66, worth €72.60.

Part B 4 marks

Initial wealth was 2 × £30 × €1.20 = €72. Home return €72.60/€72 − 1 ≈ 0.833%. Factor check 1.10 × (1.10/1.20) − 1 ≈ 0.833%. Adding percentages omits the interaction term.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A foreign asset earns 10% in local currency and its currency falls 8% against the investor’s home currency. Why does the investor earn less than 2%?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would translate terminal wealth directly. A local factor of 1.10 multiplied by a currency factor of 0.92 gives 1.012, or 1.2% home return. The missing 0.8 percentage points in the simple addition is the currency loss on the local gain itself. A performance report should label its FX quotation and dividend treatment so the decomposition reconciles to actual home wealth.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The vendor supplies the opposite quote, foreign units per euro, which rose by 8%. Is the home-per-foreign fall exactly 8%?

Compare with the follow-up answer

No. The correct home-currency factor is 1/1.08, approximately 0.925926. With the 10% local gain the home factor is 1.10/1.08, giving about 1.8519%. Inverting a rate is different from simply changing the sign of its percentage change.
