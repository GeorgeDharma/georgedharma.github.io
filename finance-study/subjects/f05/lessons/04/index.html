Tabular operations

Filter rows, group observations and calculate changes. Inspect the original rows contributing to a reported total.

The starting point

A payments table contains one row per transfer. To answer how much a bank sent in euros last month, first identify the eligible rows, then combine their amounts. Filtering selects observations. Grouping brings observations with a shared identifier together for calculation.

Choose the grouping key and the unit before summing. Mixing currencies silently changes the meaning of a total. Keep a count of the contributing rows and inspect a few individual transactions, especially when two groups appear unexpectedly identical.

Theory and method

Time-weighted average balance

Stock–flow distinction · Balance-day weighting

A borrower used its full line for one day and a much smaller balance for nine days. Why is averaging the two balance records an incorrect interest base?

A balance row describes how much principal is outstanding at a point, while an interest calculation charges for each day that amount remains in use. Event tables often record a balance only when it changes. Giving every event equal weight makes a short-lived spike count as much as a long-lived base balance. Reconstruct the interval covered by each row and multiply the amount by its exposure days before calculating the average or interest.

How the result is built

Average balance = Σ(balance × days in force)/total days. Simple interest = Σ(balance × days in force) × annual rate/day-count base.

Balances are outstanding euros, days are actual whole days on a stated half-open interval, rate is annual decimal interest and the day-count base is 360 or the stated contractual alternative.

  1. Day 1 carries €100,000, then days 2 through 10 carry €10,000. The two event rows average €55,000 if weighted equally.
  2. Exposure-days are €100,000 × 1 + €10,000 × 9 = €190,000-days. Divide by ten for a time-weighted €19,000 balance.
  3. At 7.2% simple actual/360 interest is €190,000 × 0.072/360 = €38. A SQL grouping can sum balance × days_in_force by account after the intervals are built.

When the reasoning holds

  • Intervals must be nonoverlapping, gap-free and cut at every genuine balance or rate change. Missing a transaction can incorrectly extend the previous balance.
  • Each day has one applicable accrual balance under the stated convention. Intraday loans or alternative value dates require finer intervals or different rules.

First example

A table has euro transfers of €40 and €60 from branch A, one €30 transfer from B, and one $20 transfer from A. Filtering to euros and grouping by branch gives A €100 and B €30. Their €130 total reconciles to the three eligible rows.

Decide what one row represents

A payments table may contain one row per invoice, transfer, card authorization or settlement line. Calling every row a sale before establishing its grain can duplicate activity. A cancelled card authorization and its later replacement, for instance, do not necessarily represent two completed cash receipts.

Record the key that identifies a transaction, the currency, the sign convention and which status indicates completion. Filtering a month by booking date can answer a different question from filtering by settlement date. State the operational event before counting records so a colleague can reproduce the same population.

Aggregate correctly across currencies and weights

A group-by operation partitions rows according to one or more keys, such as branch and currency, then calculates a summary within each group. Summing signed amounts gives net flow. Summing positive receipts separately from refunds gives gross inflow and outflow. A count of rows is different from a count of distinct customers or transaction IDs.

A portfolio’s weighted rate is the sum of exposure times rate divided by total exposure. A simple mean of group averages generally misweights groups with different sizes. For currencies, translate at a specified rate and date before adding different units, or report distinct subtotals. Do not interpret a global total that adds euros and dollars directly.

Changes need a coherent baseline

A period-on-period percentage change is (new−old)/old under the usual base convention. If the old value is zero, this percentage is undefined even when the euro change is informative. With a negative starting value, a percentage change can be hard to interpret, so show the absolute movement and sign as well.

Filtering before grouping can change denominators. Suppose only branches with sales in both months remain. This describes continuing branches and omits newly opened ones, whereas network growth includes them. Retain an auditable row count or set of contributing IDs behind an aggregate to make this population choice visible.

Method

Build an auditable summary

  1. Filter by a stated definition

    Select completed transactions with the right event date and currency. Keep refund signs and document canceled rows.

  2. Group and aggregate

    Sum by branch and currency and count unique identifiers. For weighted rates keep both numerator and denominator. On a normalized SQL table, SELECT branch, currency, SUM(amount) AS net FROM payments WHERE status = 'cleared' GROUP BY branch, currency returns one net amount per branch and currency.

  3. Reconcile to the detail

    The sum of comparable subtotals must match the filtered total. Verify any period comparison uses the intended set of branches.

Worked case

Cleared card receipts

In June the record shows A1 branch A +€120 cleared, A2 branch A −€20 cleared refund, B1 branch B +€90 cleared, B2 branch B +€40 canceled, and B3 branch B +$50 cleared. Use settlement status and separate currencies.

Working

  1. Euro-cleared entries are A1, A2 and B1. Their gross positive receipts are €210, refund amount €20, and net €190.
  2. Branch A nets €100 and B €90 in euros. The $50 is recorded in a dollar subtotal. The canceled €40 is excluded.
  3. If the approved rate is €0.90 per dollar and a single reporting currency is required, B3 adds €45, giving €235 combined net reporting euros.

Interpretation

A previously reported €200 comparable euro-only total would fall 5% to €190. Comparing €235 with that €200 without converting the prior dollar activity would mix definitions.

At the board

Find the duplicated exposure before summing it

Suppose the loan table has one row per facility and the collateral table has one row per asset pledged to a facility. Facility A has a balance of 100 and two assets. Facility B has a balance of 60 and no collateral row. An unrestricted left join produces these rows.

See the reproducible code
WITH security_count AS (
  SELECT facility_id, COUNT(*) AS n
  FROM collateral
  GROUP BY facility_id
)
SELECT l.facility_id, l.balance, COALESCE(s.n, 0) AS items
FROM loans AS l
LEFT JOIN security_count AS s
  ON s.facility_id = l.facility_id;

A direct sum after the raw join reports 260. The source loan ledger totals 160. Grouping the many side first gives two output rows, A with two items and B with zero. SUM(DISTINCT balance) would only happen to work here. Different facilities can have identical balances.

A common mistake

The average rate across branch summary rows is used as the rate on all loans.

How to correct it

Obtain the branch exposures and weighted numerator. A €1m branch should not count the same as a €100m branch in an exposure-weighted calculation.

Quick check
If branch A’s euro total rises from €100 to €125 on the same basis, what is the percentage change?

The difference of €25 is compared with the starting total of €100. The currency filter and the group definition must be consistent across periods.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

Two branches hold €100,000 of loans at 2% and €300,000 at 6%. A third row is a canceled €50,000 loan and must be excluded.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Calculate the weighted rate and compare with the simple average of the two branch rates.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    A previous month had zero loans in a new branch that now holds €40,000. What change should its report show?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

Annual amounts are €2,000 and €18,000. Total €20,000 on €400,000 gives 5%. The equally weighted branch average of 4% gives the smaller branch disproportionate influence.

Part B 3 marks

Report a €40,000 increase and disclose a zero prior balance. Ordinary percentage growth relative to the prior month is undefined. Network-level growth can still be computed if its total prior balance was positive.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A borrower used its full line for one day and a much smaller balance for nine days. Why is averaging the two balance records an incorrect interest base?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would weight the balances by the number of days they were actually outstanding. The €100,000 line balance persisted for one day, while €10,000 persisted for nine, giving €19,000 across the ten-day window. Interest on those balance-days is €38. I would retain each source event, its effective date and the next change date so the billed accrual can be reproduced.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if the high balance lasted two days and the ten-day window stayed fixed?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The low balance then lasts eight days. Exposure-days become €100,000 × 2 + €10,000 × 8 = €280,000-days. The average rises to €28,000 and interest rises to €56. The two observed balance values have remained identical while the economic charge has changed.
