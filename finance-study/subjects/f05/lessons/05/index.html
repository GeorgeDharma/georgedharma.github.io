SQL and joins

Identify the keys that distinguish records. Joining two tables with repeated keys can silently multiply balances or transaction volumes.

The starting point

A loan table identifies the borrower on each facility, while a second table records that same borrower’s industry. A join brings the industry label onto each loan by matching borrower identifiers. A key is the column or combination of columns identifying a record at the intended level.

Check whether the lookup table has one row per match key. Multiple matches are legitimate in some analyses, but can multiply amounts unintentionally. Check record counts, unmatched identifiers and the sum of principal both before and after attaching a descriptive label.

Theory and method

Pre-aggregation of one-to-many joins

Join cardinality · Independent child tables

One loan has two guarantors and three repayments. Why does joining all three tables at once produce six rows, and how would you calculate total repayments safely?

A loan can have several guarantors and several cash repayments. Joining both lists on the same facility ID pairs every guarantor with every repayment even though the two child records describe different things. A replicated cash amount still looks like an ordinary number to SUM. Establish the target grain, then aggregate each child list to that grain independently before attaching its results to the loan. Keep row-level details in their own tables for later drill-down.

How the result is built

WITH p AS (SELECT loan_id, SUM(amount) AS paid FROM payments GROUP BY loan_id), g AS (SELECT loan_id, COUNT(*) AS n FROM guarantees GROUP BY loan_id) SELECT l.id, COALESCE(p.paid, 0) AS paid, COALESCE(g.n, 0) AS guarantees FROM loans AS l LEFT JOIN p ON p.loan_id=l.id LEFT JOIN g ON g.loan_id=l.id

loans has one row per id; payments records settled amounts in euros per loan_id; guarantees records one verified agreement per loan_id. COALESCE supplies the documented zero when no applicable child record exists.

  1. One loan has confirmed repayments €10, €20 and €30 plus two guarantors. A naive two-child join creates 3 × 2 = 6 pairs.
  2. Summing across those pairs counts €10, €20 and €30 twice, reporting €120. Each guarantor also appears three times, inflating an ordinary COUNT.
  3. The payment subquery returns one loan row with €60. The guarantor subquery returns one loan row with count two. Both join one-to-one onto the parent, preserving the true totals.

When the reasoning holds

  • Payment rows must already be free of duplicate transaction IDs and expressed in one reporting currency. Pre-aggregation will faithfully sum duplicated or mixed-unit source data.
  • An absent child represents a confirmed zero in this example. If the feed has not yet arrived, mark incomplete coverage instead of automatically setting missing to zero.
Terms in this lesson
Principal
The amount borrowed or invested before interest is added.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
First example

Borrower X has loans of €40 and €60. A borrower lookup incorrectly contains two identical X rows. A join on X produces four rows and an apparent total of €200. The original loans total only €100.

Keys describe an economic relationship

Loans have facility IDs, borrowers have customer IDs and payments have transaction IDs. A facility may make many payments and one customer may own several facilities. Identify the tables’ grains before choosing a join. An account name typed differently in two source systems is a weak key even if the visible labels look similar.

A primary key uniquely identifies a row in its table. A foreign key refers to a row in another table. For example many facilities can refer to one borrower. A borrower lookup intended to label facilities should contain at most one applicable borrower row per matching key at the date in question.

What joins do to unmatched records

An inner join returns matching pairs and drops rows lacking a match. A left join retains every row of the left-hand table and supplies missing right-hand fields when no match exists. Neither choice alone guarantees correct totals. An inner join can silently remove a facility whose borrower sector has not yet arrived.

When a loan legitimately links to multiple collateral records, multiplying rows can be correct at collateral grain. Summing loan principal after that expansion still repeats amounts. Aggregate collateral to one per loan or report exposure from the facility table. SELECT DISTINCT can hide duplicates accidentally and can also delete different real rows sharing identical displayed amounts.

Time is part of some keys

A customer can move from one industry or risk band to another. A lookup with multiple historical rows per customer is valid, yet joining only on customer ID matches several eras simultaneously. Use the observation date with a nonoverlapping effective-date interval, commonly start ≤ date < end, to identify the correct record.

Check for overlapping validity intervals and missing intervals for each entity. Assigning today’s sector or credit rating to an earlier decision can also leak later information into a historical analysis. The database relationship and the economic date definition must agree before aggregation.

Method

Protect a balance across a join

  1. Check uniqueness

    Group the lookup by the intended complete match key and investigate counts above one. For dated keys test validity intervals. For a single-date lookup, SELECT borrower_id, COUNT(*) FROM labels GROUP BY borrower_id HAVING COUNT(*) > 1 lists repeated borrower keys.

  2. Join and audit

    Retain source IDs, compare counts and principal sums before and after the join, and record unmatched identifiers separately. After resolving repeated keys, SELECT f.loan_id, f.principal, b.sector FROM facilities AS f LEFT JOIN labels AS b ON f.borrower_id = b.borrower_id attaches a sector while retaining unmatched loans.

  3. Repair at the correct grain

    Resolve duplicate lookup records or summarize child records. Avoid indiscriminate deduplication of economically distinct facilities.

Worked case

Adding sectors to a loan book

Facilities L1 and L2 belong to customer X and carry €60 and €40. L3 belongs to Y and carries €50. A sector lookup has two current X rows and no Y row. Original portfolio balance is €150.

Working

  1. A naive inner join returns L1 twice and L2 twice while dropping L3. Its apparent principal is €200 across four rows.
  2. A naive left join returns the four X rows plus unmatched L3, giving €250 across five rows. Neither operation by itself fixes repeated X labels.
  3. Resolve X to one verified current record and use a left join. The output then contains L1, L2, L3 exactly once and €150, with Y sector explicitly unknown.

Interpretation

The simultaneous removal and duplication can partially offset in totals. Always check individual source-ID multiplicities as well as portfolio aggregates.

At the board

Find the duplicated exposure before summing it

Suppose the loan table has one row per facility and the collateral table has one row per asset pledged to a facility. Facility A has a balance of 100 and two assets. Facility B has a balance of 60 and no collateral row. An unrestricted left join produces these rows.

See the reproducible code
WITH security_count AS (
  SELECT facility_id, COUNT(*) AS n
  FROM collateral
  GROUP BY facility_id
)
SELECT l.facility_id, l.balance, COALESCE(s.n, 0) AS items
FROM loans AS l
LEFT JOIN security_count AS s
  ON s.facility_id = l.facility_id;

A direct sum after the raw join reports 260. The source loan ledger totals 160. Grouping the many side first gives two output rows, A with two items and B with zero. SUM(DISTINCT balance) would only happen to work here. Different facilities can have identical balances.

A common mistake

A report balances in euros, so the join is accepted without checking IDs.

How to correct it

Count distinct and repeated facility IDs and audit unmatched values. An omitted €50 loan and duplicated €50 loan can cancel in totals.

Quick check
What is the most useful repair if one industry label per borrower is intended?

Resolve why the lookup has repeated keys and retain one verified record per borrower. Dividing by two would damage borrowers with only one match.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A portfolio has loans A €30, B €70 and C €40. The intended borrower label table matches A once, B twice and C zero times.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Show row count and summed exposure for an inner join and a left join.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Specify a repair that preserves every loan while making missing information visible, with two validation checks.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Inner join emits 1 + 2 = 3 rows, totaling €30 + 2 × €70 = €170. Left join adds C once, giving four rows and €210. Original exposure is €140.

Part B 4 marks

Verify and resolve the duplicate label for B, then left-join one valid label per borrower. Retain C with an unknown label. Require one row per original loan and €140 total, and produce an unmatched-ID list containing C.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

One loan has two guarantors and three repayments. Why does joining all three tables at once produce six rows, and how would you calculate total repayments safely?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would aggregate payments by loan ID and separately count guarantees by loan ID. Then I would left-join those one-row-per-loan summaries onto the facility register. The correct answer is €60 across three payments and two guarantors. A six-row join is an intermediate set of artificial pairs and would double the cash total. I would reconcile both source IDs and euros before signing off.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Would SUM(DISTINCT amount) fix the issue if the true payments were €20, €20 and €30?

Compare with the follow-up answer

It would wrongly collapse the two real €20 transfers and return €50 instead of €70. Separate payment identities define distinct transactions. First resolve genuine duplicate IDs, then sum every valid transaction before joining to guarantors.
