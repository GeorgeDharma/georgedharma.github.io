Model structure

Separate source data, assumptions, calculations and outputs. A change to a single assumption should flow through the model consistently.

The starting point

A sales forecast is easier to check when the reader can trace a result back to its inputs. Distinguish the original record of last year’s sales, the assumed growth rate, the calculation that combines them, and the resulting forecast.

Give each input a name, a unit and one place to change it. Formulas elsewhere should refer to that input. Preserving the original data also allows someone to tell a changed assumption apart from a correction to the underlying source.

Theory and method

Driver-based modelling and price–volume variance analysis

Revenue bridge · Sensitivity analysis

Revenue increased by €5,200. How would you trace how much came from price, how much came from volume, and what happened where both changed?

A service firm increases its price just as more customers place orders. A model multiplies quantity by price. Changing both at once means some of the additional units also earn the higher price, creating an interaction. Decide which comparison you want before attributing the increase. A three-part bridge isolates the base-price volume effect, the base-volume price effect, and their overlap. This prevents a reviewer from assigning the same overlap to both changes.

How the result is built

ΔR = (Q₁−Q₀)P₀ + Q₀(P₁−P₀) + (Q₁−Q₀)(P₁−P₀).

R means euro revenue, Q units sold and P euros per unit. Subscripts 0 and 1 indicate base and actual periods. ΔR is actual revenue less base revenue.

  1. The base is 200 orders at €40, giving €8,000. Actual volume is 240 and price €55, producing €13,200 and a €5,200 change.
  2. The 40 additional orders at the old price contribute €1,600. Applying the €15 price rise to the original 200 orders adds €3,000.
  3. Their interaction adds another 40 × €15 = €600. All three sum to €5,200. Expanding (Q₀+ΔQ)(P₀+ΔP) proves the identity.

When the reasoning holds

  • Units correspond to comparable products and revenues are recognized on a consistent basis. A mix change concealed within one average price can call for a segment-level bridge.
  • This is an exact attribution convention, not causal evidence that a price increase caused volume growth. Simultaneous demand shifts can affect both.
Terms in this lesson
Revenue
Amount earned by selling goods or services. The customer may pay later.
Cash buffer
Money kept available to meet upcoming bills or unexpected payments.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
First example

Last year’s units sold were 800, assumed growth is 5%, and the price per unit is €20. Forecast units are 800 × 1.05 = 840. Forecast revenue is 840 × €20 = €16,800. Changing only growth to 10% should produce 880 units and €17,600.

Start from the decision and its units

A lender asks how far an exporter’s sales can fall before cash runs short. Building a revenue chart immediately may look productive, but the question concerns available cash after dated collections and payments. Begin by naming the decision, required output, currency, frequency and reporting horizon. A clean model makes these choices visible.

Distinguish an observed input, a chosen assumption, an intermediate calculation and a decision output. Last month’s invoiced units come from a system of record. Next month’s collection percentage is an assumption. Expected receipts are calculated. Whether a cash buffer is breached is the decision output. Each category needs a different source of verification.

One direction of calculation

Arrange the normal flow from records and assumptions through calculations to outputs. When a result is copied back into an assumption cell, subsequent calculations may quietly use a stale result. Circular logic occurs when an output feeds an input that determines the same output. Some financial equations legitimately need a simultaneous solution, but that dependence should be explicit.

Write balances as opening amount plus inflows less outflows equals closing amount. Keep a stock such as month-end cash separate from a flow such as monthly sales. Adding twelve closing balances does not measure annual cash received. A total that is dimensionally implausible can reveal an architectural mistake before any formula is inspected.

Scenarios are coherent sets of assumptions

A downside case might combine slower customer payments with higher material costs. Store those changes together under a named assumption set while preserving the original observed history. Running the same calculation logic against each scenario makes output differences attributable to the changed assumptions.

A sensitivity changes one or more defined inputs systematically to study how the output responds. A model should represent what happens when a threshold is crossed, such as a mandatory credit withdrawal. Testing only a small favorable change may hide a discontinuity. Identify which cash flows and contractual rules are outside your simplified model.

Method

Design a traceable cash model

  1. Describe the source table

    Write one row per period with dated opening records and units. Keep source values intact and label the version used.

  2. Write the cash identity

    Use collections, payments and opening cash to derive closing cash. Store scenario terms once so a changed assumption updates every dependent output.

  3. Build a decision check

    Compare projected cash with the required minimum and calculate the sales or collection threshold, then replicate one month manually.

Worked case

Cash required for a book fair

A seller opens with €3,000, expects to invoice 500 orders at €20, pays €5,600 in cash costs and must retain €1,000. Only a fraction c of this month’s sales is collected by month-end. Assume no other flows.

Working

  1. Invoiced sales total €10,000. Closing cash is €3,000 + €10,000c − €5,600. Sales alone cannot determine liquidity.
  2. At c = 50%, close is €2,400 with €1,400 headroom above the minimum. At c = 30%, close is €400 and headroom is −€600.
  3. Solve €3,000 + €10,000c − €5,600 ≥ €1,000. Collections must reach at least 36% of current invoices.

Interpretation

If old invoices are collected too, they need their own line. Including them inside the current-sales collection percentage would make the assumption hard to interpret.

A common mistake

To remove a negative cash result, someone overwrites the calculated closing balance with the minimum required.

How to correct it

Keep the formula, show the funding gap and add a separately justified funding source if one actually exists. A cash requirement by itself does not create money.

Quick check
Which change makes this model easiest to review?

A single assumption change flows through the calculations while the observed sales of 800 stay available for checking.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A clinic has €2,000 opening cash, 300 appointments billed at €40, cash costs of €9,800 and minimum cash of €1,200. It collects 70% of bookings by month-end, with no other receipts.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Find closing cash and required collection rate to hit the minimum.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Identify an input to preserve as an observed record and one assumption to vary in a downside. Explain how to test the model at the threshold.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

Billing €12,000. At 70%, closing cash = €2,000 + €8,400 − €9,800 = €600. Required rate solves €12,000c ≥ €9,000, so c ≥ 75%.

Part B 3 marks

Preserve opening cash tied to a dated bank record. Vary the collection share or justified bookings assumption. At 75%, the model must give exactly €1,200 and changing the rate down by one point should reduce cash €120.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Revenue increased by €5,200. How would you trace how much came from price, how much came from volume, and what happened where both changed?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would reconstruct revenue from the raw order and pricing inputs, then reconcile the change to €1,600 of volume at the old price, €3,000 of price on the original orders and €600 from applying the price rise to the extra orders. That makes the interaction visible. I would separately investigate product mix and demand before using this accounting attribution as a forecast.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Management wants only price and volume rows. Can you still reconcile exactly?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Yes. I can apply the original price to volume changes for €1,600 and the new volume to price changes for 240 × €15 = €3,600. Those two total €5,200. I would label the ordering explicitly because this version assigns the €600 interaction to the price row.
