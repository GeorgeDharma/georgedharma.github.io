Case 1 of 4 · 10 marks

The population behind a fraud flag

A processor has 5,000 merchants, of which 4% have fraudulent activity. A rule flags 80% of fraudulent merchants and 5% of the rest. Review costs €20 per flagged merchant, and a successful detection prevents €80 in losses. Assume every true flagged review succeeds.

Case 2 of 4 · 10 marks

Two cash exposures and downside risk

Two economic states are equally likely. Exposure A pays €10 or €30, while B pays €30 or €10 in those same respective states. A proposed book holds one unit of A and two units of B. Ignore discounting.

Case 3 of 4 · 10 marks

Processing speed in paired branches

Sixteen independent branches record individual before-to-after changes in processing time. The mean change is −2 minutes and the standard deviation of paired changes is 4 minutes. Use a 95% t critical value of 2.131. Software was introduced at the same time as extra staff in all branches.

Case 1 · 10 marks

The population behind a fraud flag

Part A 4 marks

Construct flag counts and compute fraud probability conditional on a flag.

There are 200 fraudulent merchants and 4,800 genuine ones. The system flags 160 frauds and 240 genuine merchants, 400 in all. Predictive value is 160/400 = 40%. Award for base counts, correct two flagged counts and conditional value.

Part B 3 marks

Evaluate reviewing all alerts and derive the break-even saving per detection.

Cost 400 × €20 = €8,000. Benefits 160 × €80 = €12,800, giving €4,800 net. Break-even benefit is €8,000/160 = €50 per true detection. Award for costs, net and break-even.

Part C 3 marks

The processor carries this rate to an unverified sample consisting only of reviewed urban alerts. What can that sample establish?

It may estimate the conditional fraud proportion among those reviewed urban alerts if observations and outcomes are complete. It cannot establish base prevalence for all merchants or the false-negative rate because no unflagged group is observed. Changing population composition can also change predictive value. Award one for each distinction.

Case 2 · 10 marks

Two cash exposures and downside risk

Part A 4 marks

Calculate means, standard deviations and covariance for A and B.

Both means are €20 and both standard deviations €10. Deviations are −10/+10 and +10/−10. Covariance is (−100 − 100)/2 = −100 square euros, giving correlation −1. Award for means, deviations/dispersion, covariance and sign.

Part B 3 marks

Calculate the proposed book’s state payoffs, mean, variance and probability of missing a €55 obligation.

Payoffs are €70 or €50. Mean is €60, variance is 100 square euros and probability of falling below €55 is 0.5. Award for state payoffs, moments and shortfall probability.

Part C 3 marks

Check the variance with covariance and propose a zero-variance combination with positive holdings.

Variance of A + 2B is 100 + 4 × 100 + 4 × (−100) = 100. One unit of each pays €40 in both states, thus variance zero. This follows from matching deviation magnitudes, not from the means alone.

Case 3 · 10 marks

Processing speed in paired branches

Part A 4 marks

Calculate the standard error and 95% confidence interval for the mean paired change.

Standard error = 4/√16 = 1 minute. Interval is −2 ± 2.131 = [−4.131, +0.131] minutes. Award for the paired standard deviation, standard error, formula and endpoints.

Part B 3 marks

Does a two-sided 5% test reject no mean change, and does this demonstrate that the change is economically irrelevant?

Zero lies within the corresponding interval, so the matched test does not reject. Effects close to a four-minute reduction remain compatible with the data and might matter economically. Detection and practical importance are different.

Part C 3 marks

How does the staffing change affect attribution, and how many independent branches would approximately halve the standard error if dispersion stayed fixed?

Staffing and software changed together, so the before–after estimate cannot isolate software effects. Standard error scales as one over the square root of n, requiring 64 independent branches to halve it, with the same dispersion and design.

Case 4 · 10 marks

An audit of a forecasting claim

Part A 4 marks

Fit a line with an intercept and find the residual at x = 2.

Mean x = 2 and mean y = 3. Sum of cross-products is 1 + 0 + 2 = 3, sum of squared x deviations is 2. Slope is 1.5 and intercept is zero. At x = 2 fitted value is 3, so observed minus fitted is −1 thousand euros. Award for centered sums, slope, intercept and residual.

Part B 3 marks

Interpret the slope’s units and give a plausible confounder that limits causal interpretation.

The slope is €1,500 per 100 customers, or €15 per customer as a fitted association. Product mix, shop location or season might increase both foot traffic and revenue independently. A positive slope does not identify revenue caused by one induced visit.

Part C 3 marks

Apply Bonferroni and outline a valid follow-up forecast evaluation.

Threshold is 0.05/12 ≈ 0.00417. The smallest p of 0.013 fails it. Freeze the selected specification and performance measure and evaluate on previously unused observations, preserving time order where required.
