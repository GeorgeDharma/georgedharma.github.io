Confounding and dependence

A common driver can produce an association without a direct causal effect. Serial dependence can make naive standard errors too small.

The starting point

An analyst notices that both borrowing and sales rise during the summer. More borrowing might support sales, but seasonal demand can drive both. A variable influencing both the predictor and outcome can confound their observed relationship.

A second issue concerns precision. Successive observations can share the same shocks. Positive serial dependence can make consecutive months less informative than the same number of independent observations. Compare like seasonal periods and choose uncertainty estimates appropriate to the dependence and design.

Theory and method

Direct standardization and Simpson’s paradox

Case-mix adjustment · Stratification

One lender has lower default rates in every risk band but a higher overall default rate. Could both observations be correct?

A lender serving mostly high-risk businesses can have better outcomes within every comparable group and still show a higher headline default rate. Each headline averages group results using that lender’s own customer mix. To ask how the lenders compare on a common population, choose a common set of weights and apply both lenders’ group-specific rates to it. The resulting contrast removes measured mix differences and leaves other sources of confounding to investigate.

How the result is built

Standardized rateⱼ = Σg w*g rⱼg, with common target weights summing to one.

j identifies the lender, g the risk band, r the observed default proportion within a lender and band, and w* the chosen target population share. Rates and weights are fractions for the same outcome horizon.

  1. Lender A defaults are 1% in low-risk and 9% in high-risk borrowers. B has 2% and 10%. A has 80% high-risk customers and B just 20%.
  2. A’s actual aggregate is 0.2×1% + 0.8×9% = 7.4%. B’s is 0.8×2% + 0.2×10% = 3.6%.
  3. Apply 50% weights to each band for both lenders. A then gives 5% and B 6%. The common-mix difference favours A by one percentage point.

When the reasoning holds

  • Bands must mean the same thing at both lenders and be observed before treatment or decision. Different rating definitions can make comparison misleading.
  • The common target requires support in every used band. Unmeasured differences within bands and outcome dependence can remain after standardization.

First example

A bank reports monthly lending during 12 months, but its lending limit is set only once per quarter. All three months in a quarter share that policy. There are four distinct policy settings, even though the spreadsheet has 12 rows.

A comparison needs a counterfactual

A bank compares the profits of firms receiving loans with those denied loans. The financed firms may have been stronger even before the loan. A causal effect asks how the same firms would have performed under different financing. Only one of those outcomes is observed, so identifying a credible comparison group requires an argument.

Confounding occurs when a factor influences both treatment or exposure and the outcome. Baseline credit quality can affect both loan approval and future profits. Adjusting for measured pre-loan quality can improve comparability, but unmeasured customer demand or owner experience may remain. More data of the same selected type do not automatically remove that problem.

Comparing changes across groups

A differences-in-differences calculation subtracts the change in an untreated group from the change in a treated group. Its causal interpretation requires that, absent the treatment, their outcome trends would have been comparable. It also needs attention to simultaneous changes, shifting group composition and spillovers between groups.

Plot multiple pre-treatment periods to see whether trends were different before the intervention. Similar pretrends strengthen plausibility but cannot prove the unobserved future counterfactual. If an unrelated factory closure affects only the comparison region at the same time, subtracting its decline could exaggerate the apparent treatment effect.

Dependence changes standard errors

Observations from the same company or month can share shocks. Even if a point estimate is calculated correctly, an independence-based standard error may be too small with positive within-group correlation. Block or cluster-aware procedures preserve such dependence under appropriate assumptions and need enough independent clusters to support inference.

Distinguish bias from precision. Correcting standard errors for clustering changes estimated uncertainty and does not by itself remove omitted-variable bias. Adding a good control can help comparability and still leave correlated errors. A strong empirical design addresses both the credibility of the comparison and the way uncertainty is calculated.

Method

Audit an empirical claim

  1. Name the comparison

    Define treated and comparison units, timing, assignment process and the alternative outcome the study is trying to approximate.

  2. List common causes and shocks

    Identify variables that could affect both assignment and outcomes. For changes over time, identify concurrent group-specific events.

  3. Match errors to design

    State the likely independent units or time blocks and test robustness using credible group definitions, pretrends and placebo periods.

Worked case

A new invoice platform

Average collection time in adopting branches falls from 40 to 30 days. Comparison branches improve from 36 to 32 days over the same period. The bank chose early adopters partly because they had unusually poor collection times.

Working

  1. The simple after-minus-before change among adopters is −10 days. Comparison branches also change by −4 days.
  2. The differences-in-differences estimate is −10 − (−4) = −6 days, an improvement beyond the common change if parallel counterfactual trends are credible.
  3. Selection after exceptionally bad performance creates a risk of regression toward more typical results. Check several earlier periods, baseline trends and whether other collection policies changed.

Interpretation

Repeated invoices within one branch share management and customers. Basing precision solely on the total number of invoices would likely overstate independent information.

A common mistake

Cluster-aware errors are presented as proof that platform adoption caused the estimated difference.

How to correct it

They address uncertainty under a dependence pattern. Causality still depends on assignment and the plausibility of the counterfactual trends.

Quick check
Which change mainly tackles the common seasonal driver in the borrowing and sales example?

Seasonal comparisons and demand controls directly address a shared cause. Dependence-aware errors address uncertainty and alone cannot remove confounding.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

Approved shops increase monthly sales from €50,000 to €58,000. Comparison shops increase from €42,000 to €47,000. A city festival happened only near approved shops during the follow-up month.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Compute the difference in changes and state the assumption required for a causal interpretation.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Explain how the festival and repeated daily observations affect two different aspects of the evidence.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Approved change is €8,000 and comparison change €5,000, leaving €3,000. Causality requires similar untreated trends and no differential concurrent shock driving that contrast.

Part B 4 marks

The festival can bias the €3,000 effect upward by independently boosting treated sales. Daily records from a shop can be correlated, affecting precision. Compare similar festival exposure or periods, and model uncertainty using independent shop or geography units as appropriate.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

One lender has lower default rates in every risk band but a higher overall default rate. Could both observations be correct?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Both observations fit. A lends more heavily to the high-default band. Its overall 7.4% exceeds B’s 3.6%, while applying a common 50/50 mix gives A 5% against B 6%. I would publish both. The raw figures describe actual portfolio outcomes, and the standardized figures answer a measured case-mix question. Neither comparison alone identifies what changing lender would cause for a given borrower.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Would using 80% high-risk weights for both lenders change the direction of the standardized contrast here?

Compare with the follow-up answer

At 80% high risk, A is 7.4% and B is 0.2×2% + 0.8×10% = 8.4%. A remains one point lower because the within-band differences are each exactly one point. When band-specific differences vary in sign or size, the chosen target weights can change the overall comparison.
