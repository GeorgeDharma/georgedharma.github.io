Covariance and correlation

Two exposures can offset when they move differently. Covariance carries units, while correlation standardizes the strength of their linear co-movement.

The starting point

Two assets that rise and fall together may offer little protection when held in combination. Examine each return relative to its own average, then multiply the two deviations in each outcome. Averaging those products gives covariance.

Positive covariance means deviations often have the same sign. Negative covariance means they often offset. Dividing covariance by the two standard deviations gives correlation, a unit-free measure between −1 and +1 when both standard deviations are positive. Zero correlation alone need not imply independence.

Theory and method

Minimum-variance hedge ratio

Linear projection hedge · Covariance hedge

How much of a market index should a desk sell to minimize variability in a position when the two price changes are correlated but do not match exactly?

A trading desk owns a position that often falls when an index falls. Selling index exposure can offset some of that common movement. Too small a hedge leaves shared risk in place, while too large a hedge creates new index exposure in the opposite direction. Choose the quantity that minimizes the variance of the combined price changes. The best hedge is shaped by both the degree of co-movement and the volatility of the hedge instrument itself.

How the result is built

h* = Cov(X,H)/Var(H) = ρσX/σH for hedged change X − hH.

X is the unhedged position’s euro P&L over the horizon, H the euro P&L of one long hedge unit, h units sold, ρ their correlation and σ the respective standard deviations. Cov and Var are covariance and variance.

  1. Expanding Var(X−hH) gives Var(X) + h²Var(H) − 2hCov(X,H). Differentiate in h and set 2hVar(H) − 2Cov(X,H) = 0.
  2. Suppose σX = €12, σH = €8 and ρ = 0.60. Covariance is 0.60 × 12 × 8 = 57.6 square euros. Hedge-unit variance is 64.
  3. The optimal h is 0.9. Residual variance is 144 + 0.9² × 64 − 2 × 0.9 × 57.6 = 92.16, so residual standard deviation is €9.60.

When the reasoning holds

  • Position and hedge P&Ls use the same dates, horizon and currency and are in the declared unit sizes. Changing the hedge contract size changes h.
  • The objective minimizes variance with no material transaction costs or exposure limits. Costs or unreliable historical covariance can justify a smaller practical hedge.
Terms in this lesson
Correlation
A measure of how closely two series move together in a linear pattern.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
How to read the notation
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.
Summation · ∑
The symbol asks you to add the terms for the listed items. For weights of 0.6 and 0.4 and returns of 5% and 2%, ∑ wᵢrᵢ means 0.6 × 5% + 0.4 × 2% = 3.8%. A standalone capital sigma (Σ) can also name a covariance matrix in portfolio equations.
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.

All notation examples

First example

Two equally likely outcomes give asset A returns of +10% or −10% and asset B the exact opposite. Each averages zero, and their correlation is −1. A half-and-half portfolio earns 0% in either outcome before costs.

Co-movement relative to each average

Suppose the same weather shock helps a cold-drink seller and hurts a hot-drink seller. To measure their joint movements, subtract each seller’s own expected profit in each state, multiply the two deviations and average these products using the state probabilities. This produces their covariance.

When both deviations tend to share a sign, positive products dominate. Covariance depends on units, so measuring one account in cents instead of euros changes the numerical value. Correlation divides covariance by the two standard deviations, cancelling unit scaling when both are positive. It describes linear co-movement and does not establish causality.

Derive a portfolio’s variance

A weighted portfolio return is w times return A plus (1−w) times return B. Its deviation from the portfolio mean is the weighted sum of the two deviations. Squaring that sum produces the two weighted squared terms plus twice their product. Taking expectations gives the portfolio variance formula.

In symbols, variance = w²σA² + (1−w)²σB² + 2w(1−w)ρσAσB. Here σ denotes standard deviation and ρ correlation. This shows why portfolio variance cannot be calculated as a simple weighted average of the two individual variances. The joint behaviour term can add risk or offset it.

Limits of the diversification argument

For a fixed pair of long positions and given individual volatilities, a lower correlation reduces the portfolio variance. Perfect negative correlation can remove all variability with a suitable choice of weights, but equal weights work exactly only when the weighted magnitudes of the two movements match. With unequal risks the hedge ratio must change.

A sample correlation from ordinary times can change in a crisis. Zero correlation says linear co-movement is absent in the data or model. Nonlinear dependence can remain. A bank depending on the same source of emergency funding for both positions can face a joint risk that past price correlation alone misses.

Method

Combine two risks

  1. Match units and dates

    Use standard deviations from the same return horizon and units, then write all weights on one wealth base.

  2. Compute covariance

    Multiply correlation by the two standard deviations. Include twice the product of weights times covariance in the variance.

  3. Interpret the square root

    Convert variance to standard deviation. Compare with perfect positive or negative co-movement to understand what the correlation term changed.

Worked case

A two-currency receivable portfolio

A company puts 60% of an exposure in asset A with annual volatility 10% and 40% in asset B with 20%. Their same-horizon correlation is −0.50. Treat the weights as fixed for the period.

Working

  1. Covariance is −0.50 × 0.10 × 0.20 = −0.010. The own-risk contributions are 0.60² × 0.10² = 0.0036 and 0.40² × 0.20² = 0.0064.
  2. The cross term is 2 × 0.60 × 0.40 × (−0.010) = −0.0048. Variance is 0.0052 and volatility is approximately 7.21%.
  3. If correlation rose to +0.50 with all else fixed, variance would be 0.0148 and volatility about 12.17%. Individual volatilities have not changed.

Interpretation

A negative correlation reduced the joint dispersion in the example but did not establish that each loss will be offset in every state.

A common mistake

The correlation of −0.5 is entered as the covariance in a return variance calculation.

How to correct it

First convert correlation to covariance using both asset volatilities. The standardization that makes correlation unit-free must be undone before adding variance terms.

Quick check
If B instead earns exactly A’s return in every outcome, what happens to that half-and-half portfolio?

Each half now experiences the same return. Weighting two identical movements by one half each reproduces one full movement.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

An equal-value portfolio contains two assets with volatilities of 12% and 8%. Their correlation is zero.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Find portfolio volatility and compare it with the case where correlation is +1.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    With correlation −1, find a long-only weight in the first asset that eliminates variance. Explain why half in each falls short.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Variance at zero correlation is 0.25 × 0.12² + 0.25 × 0.08² = 0.0052, giving 7.21%. At +1 volatility is 0.5 × 12% + 0.5 × 8% = 10%.

Part B 4 marks

Set w × 0.12 = (1−w) × 0.08, giving w = 0.40. With equal weights the weighted volatilities are 6% and 4%, so an unmatched 2% remains.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How much of a market index should a desk sell to minimize variability in a position when the two price changes are correlated but do not match exactly?

I would sell 0.9 index units using the stated P&L scales. The hedge covariance is 57.6 and the hedge variance 64, making their ratio 0.9. That reduces standard deviation from €12 to €9.60. Interviewer’s follow-up

If the covariance changes to zero with both volatilities unchanged, what does the variance-minimizing model recommend?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The optimal linear hedge becomes zero. Selling any positive amount would add h²Var(H) without an offsetting covariance benefit. This conclusion is about this variance objective and this hedge. A nonlinear payoff might still help under particular tail scenarios.
