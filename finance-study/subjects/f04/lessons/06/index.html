Sampling distributions

Estimates change across samples. The standard error measures variation of an estimator under its sampling assumptions.

The starting point

A survey of 25 shops estimates their average daily sales. A second survey of another 25 shops is unlikely to find exactly the same average. Imagine repeating the sampling method many times and collecting the estimated means. Their distribution is a sampling distribution.

The standard error describes how much the estimate varies under the sampling procedure. For independent, similarly distributed observations the estimated standard error of the mean is sample standard deviation divided by the square root of sample size. Dependence changes that calculation.

Theory and method

Finite population correction

Simple random sampling without replacement · Survey standard error

An auditor randomly inspects 80 of only 100 invoices. Should the uncertainty calculation treat this the same as inspecting 80 out of a million?

An auditor choosing different invoices without replacement learns a great deal about a small register when most of it has been inspected. The next draw cannot repeat invoices already seen. This introduces a dependence that reduces uncertainty about the mean of this particular fixed population. A finite population correction accounts for the shrinking share of the register that remains unknown. Estimating the mean of future invoices is a different target and does not receive the same automatic benefit.

How the result is built

Estimated SE(mean) = (s/√n) × √(1−n/N).

SE is estimated standard error, s the sample standard deviation computed with denominator n−1, n the number sampled and N the fixed population count. Units are those of an individual invoice amount.

  1. For a simple random sample of 80 out of 100 invoices, let s = €40. The uncorrected standard error is 40/√80 ≈ €4.47.
  2. The correction is √(1−80/100) = √0.2 ≈ 0.4472. The estimated finite-population standard error is exactly €2.00.
  3. If all 100 invoices were measured without error, their population mean would be known. The factor becomes zero, matching that boundary case.

When the reasoning holds

  • The target must be the specified fixed register. Sampling 80 current invoices still leaves uncertainty about next year’s invoice-generating process.
  • The design is simple random sampling without replacement with reliable recorded values. Preferential selection or mismeasured invoices can add error beyond the correction.
Terms in this lesson
Standard error
An estimate of how much a statistic would vary across repeated samples under the assumed sampling process.
How to read the notation
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.

All notation examples

First example

A sample of 25 shops has a sales standard deviation of €100. The estimated standard error of its mean is €100 / 5 = €20. With 100 independent shops and the same standard deviation, it falls to €10.

Sampling a mean repeatedly

Suppose a warehouse manager randomly chooses 25 invoices each week to estimate mean payment delay. Individual delays vary for many reasons. The sample mean also varies between random samples because each contains a different mix of delays. The distribution of those estimated means is the sampling distribution of the estimator.

A sampling distribution is a conceptual tool even when only one sample is observed. Its standard deviation is the standard error. A standard error expresses precision of the estimated mean, not the spread of delays an individual future invoice might experience. Predicting one invoice generally requires allowing for individual variability in addition to uncertainty about the mean.

Why the square root appears

For independent observations with common variance σ², the variance of their sum is nσ². Dividing their sum by n to form an average divides variance by n², leaving σ²/n. Taking the square root gives σ/√n. In practice s, the sample standard deviation, often replaces the unknown population σ.

Quadrupling independent sample size therefore halves the estimated standard error if the underlying spread is unchanged. A few high-variance observations can keep estimates imprecise despite a large headcount. Increasing n does not cure systematic measurement errors, missing units or an estimator that targets a different quantity from the requested one.

When independence fails

Successive months from the same shop may have correlated sales because one contract affects several months. The variance of a sum then includes pairwise covariance terms in addition to individual variances. Positive covariance can make observations provide less independent information than the simple count suggests.

The central limit theorem can justify a roughly normal distribution for averages under suitable conditions such as independent observations with finite variance and adequate sample size. Strong dependence, heavy tails or very small samples can undermine that approximation. There is no universal sample threshold that makes all designs reliable.

Method

Plan a precision target

  1. Estimate spread

    Use a representative pilot or comparable historical data to estimate the standard deviation of individual outcomes.

  2. Choose target precision

    Under independence, solving s/√n ≤ target gives n ≥ (s/target)². Round upward because part of an observation is unavailable.

  3. Check information quality

    Ask whether repeated records share a customer, branch or date shock. More independent units can be preferable to repeated records under identical conditions.

Worked case

A branch wait-time study

A pilot sample suggests payment processing times have a standard deviation of 18 minutes. Management wants a standard error at most three minutes for the average. Assume independently sampled comparable payments for the first calculation.

Working

  1. The target requires n ≥ (18/3)² = 36. At 36 independent observations the estimated standard error is three minutes.
  2. At 81 independent observations the corresponding standard error is 18/9 = two minutes, not 18/81 minutes.
  3. If all 81 records come from one outage day, the customer wait-time data may still be numerous, but the study has poor coverage of normal day-to-day conditions.

Interpretation

Collecting samples across days, branches or workflow types can address the actual decision. A larger total within one unusual condition does not necessarily do so.

A common mistake

A small standard error is used as evidence that a single customer’s waiting time will be close to the average.

How to correct it

The standard error measures uncertainty in the mean estimate. Use the distribution of individual waits or a prediction interval to discuss a single future customer.

Quick check
What does the €20 measure in the original sample?

The €100 standard deviation measures variation among shops. The €20 standard error describes uncertainty from estimating their population mean using a sample of 25.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A random sample of 64 independent loans yields an observed standard deviation of €240 in annual service cost. Sampling conditions can remain the same as sample size grows.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate the estimated standard error of the mean and the minimum sample size for an estimated standard error of €15.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    The same 64 loan IDs appear four times because records were copied. Would a naive calculation using n = 256 be valid?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

The current estimate is €240/8 = €30. Achieving €15 requires (240/15)² = 256 independent loans, four times the current size.

Part B 4 marks

No. Copies repeat the same outcomes exactly and provide no new independent loans. After removing duplicates the information remains 64 observations and the estimated standard error remains €30.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

An auditor randomly inspects 80 of only 100 invoices. Should the uncertainty calculation treat this the same as inspecting 80 out of a million?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would apply the finite population correction if the question is the mean of these hundred invoices and the 80 were randomly selected without replacement. The estimated standard error falls from roughly €4.47 to €2 because only twenty invoices remain unobserved. I would use a different sampling argument for future transactions or a selected high-risk subset.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If all 100 invoices are inspected but the software converts every dollar value using a rate 2% too high, is total uncertainty really zero?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Only the sampling uncertainty about the recorded register vanishes. A systematic conversion error remains and can bias even a complete census. I would reperform the conversion with independently verified rates and describe that measurement issue separately from the finite sampling adjustment.
