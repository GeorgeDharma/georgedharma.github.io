Confidence intervals

Connect an estimate with a range produced by a repeated-sampling procedure. An imprecise estimate can include economically important gains and losses.

The starting point

An analyst estimates a return advantage of 2 percentage points from a sample. The estimate would change if another sample were drawn. A confidence interval pairs the estimate with a range showing how precise the chosen procedure is.

Under a normal approximation, an estimated 95% interval is the estimate plus or minus about 1.96 standard errors. In repeated sampling, approximately 95% of intervals built this way cover the true parameter under the assumed model. The actual interval from one sample has fixed endpoints.

Theory and method

Confidence interval for a difference of independent means

Welch standard error · Confidence bound

A pilot saves €3 per case on average at one branch group. How would you decide whether the savings exceed a €2 implementation cost?

A point estimate of €3 could arise with wide uncertainty if the branch samples are small. Compare the treated and comparison mean under the sampling design, then use a lower confidence bound that reflects uncertainty in both means. The business threshold is €2. A finding compatible with a true saving of €1 could still look positive on average while failing to establish that implementation pays for itself.

How the result is built

SE(difference) = √(sA²/nA + sB²/nB); lower bound = estimated difference − c × SE.

sA and sB are the sample standard deviations in euros per case; nA and nB are independent sample counts; c is the appropriate critical value for the chosen confidence procedure, supplied in this exercise.

  1. Suppose the estimated baseline-minus-pilot cost difference is €3. Each group has 100 independent records with standard deviation €5. SE = √(25/100 + 25/100) ≈ €0.7071.
  2. For a prespecified one-sided 95% normal approximation use c = 1.645. The lower savings bound is €3 − 1.645 × €0.7071 ≈ €1.84.
  3. The estimated net benefit is €1 per case after the €2 fee. Its lower bound is approximately −€0.16. The data support a positive point estimate but leave the break-even decision uncertain.

When the reasoning holds

  • For independent groups, mean estimator variances add. Matched cases or common shocks require covariance or a paired analysis.
  • The multiplier must match a prespecified one-sided or two-sided question and sampling approximation. Small samples may require an appropriate Welch t critical value.
Terms in this lesson
Percentage point
The difference between two percentages. A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises one percentage point.
Standard error
An estimate of how much a statistic would vary across repeated samples under the assumed sampling process.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
How to read the notation
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.

All notation examples

First example

With an estimate of 2 percentage points and a standard error of 1.5 points, the interval is 2 ± 2.94 points, or about −0.94 to +4.94. It admits a modest disadvantage as well as a meaningful advantage.

From estimation error to a range

A point estimate can be close to the true mean or far away in any one sample. A confidence procedure chooses a margin using a model of the estimator’s variation. For an approximate two-sided 95% normal interval, use estimate ± 1.96 times its standard error. The multiplier controls how much of the reference distribution is included.

When observations are independent and normally distributed but their variance is estimated from the same small sample, a Student t multiplier with n−1 degrees of freedom is appropriate for the population mean. Its wider tails allow extra uncertainty. Use any critical value supplied in a question and state the population or design assumptions.

Interpret confidence after observing data

The confidence level describes the repeated-sampling performance of the procedure. Imagine repeating the entire design many times and constructing a new interval each time. Under the model, roughly the stated proportion cover the fixed true parameter. The endpoints in this particular observed sample have already been calculated.

An interval containing zero for an incremental profit effect is compatible with no effect and potentially meaningful gains or losses. This does not demonstrate that the effect is exactly zero. Conversely, a tightly estimated small positive effect may rule out zero yet have little economic significance after implementation costs.

Construct the right interval for a difference

If the same companies are observed before and after a policy, form a difference for each company. A confidence interval for the mean of those paired changes uses the standard deviation of the differences. Treating before and after as unrelated groups throws away the pairing and can misstate uncertainty.

For independent groups, the estimated standard error of their difference can be formed by adding their separate estimated variances of means and taking a square root. That calculation assumes independent groups. Within-company pairs require the covariance between pre- and post-values. Always specify which observations are independent sampling units.

Method

Report a defensible interval

  1. Define the parameter

    State whether estimating a level, a paired mean change or a difference between independent means. Keep measurement units visible.

  2. Calculate uncertainty

    Obtain the standard error from the relevant sampling design and multiply by the correct reference critical value.

  3. Interpret the bounds

    Explain what decisions the range supports or leaves uncertain. Name a design problem, such as selection, that an interval will not correct.

Worked case

A paired delivery-time evaluation

Nine independent depots record their own change in processing time after a workflow update. Negative changes are improvements. Mean paired change is −4 minutes and the sample standard deviation of those nine changes is 6 minutes. Assume approximately normal paired differences and use t = 2.306 for eight degrees of freedom.

Working

  1. The standard error of the mean change is 6/√9 = 2 minutes. The 95% margin is 2.306 × 2 = 4.612 minutes.
  2. The interval runs from about −8.61 to +0.61 minutes. While the estimate suggests faster processing, a slight deterioration also falls within this range.
  3. A promise of improvement of at least five minutes is unsupported because the interval includes effects smaller than that and even a positive delay.

Interpretation

The setup has no untreated comparison group. Even a narrower interval for the mean change would not by itself attribute that change uniquely to the workflow update.

A common mistake

Someone takes a 95% confidence interval for a mean to predict the range containing 95% of individual outcomes.

How to correct it

Identify the target printed next to the interval. Individual outcomes vary around the mean and need their own predictive distribution.

Quick check
If the standard error falls to 0.5 points while the estimate stays 2, what is the approximate interval?

The new half-width is 1.96 × 0.5 = 0.98 percentage points. It is the standard error, along with the chosen confidence level, that determines the width.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

Sixteen independently sampled loan officers show an average paired increase in cases completed of 3 per week, with standard deviation 4 for their individual paired changes. Use t = 2.131 for 15 degrees of freedom.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate the 95% interval for the mean change.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Can management claim an increase of at least four cases, and does the interval alone establish that new software caused the change?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Standard error is 4/√16 = 1. Margin 2.131, giving 0.869 to 5.131 additional cases per week. Round to 0.87 to 5.13.

Part B 4 marks

No to both. Values below four are still included. A repeated-period comparison without a credible counterfactual cannot rule out other simultaneous changes, even if the mean increase differs from zero.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A pilot saves €3 per case on average at one branch group. How would you decide whether the savings exceed a €2 implementation cost?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would report the estimated €1 net benefit and the uncertainty around it. Independent samples produce a difference standard error of around 71 cents. The prespecified lower gross bound is €1.84, so the lower net bound is about minus 16 cents. That supports further measurement or a decision accepting uncertainty, but it does not support a 95% lower-bound claim that costs are covered.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Suppose a new contract reduces the fixed fee to €1.50 without changing the data. What conclusion changes?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The evidence on gross savings is unchanged. Subtracting €1.50 gives a point net benefit of €1.50 and a lower net bound near €0.34. The same prespecified confidence calculation now provides a positive lower bound relative to the revised economic hurdle.
