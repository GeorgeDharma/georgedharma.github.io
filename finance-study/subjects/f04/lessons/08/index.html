Tests and multiple comparisons

Compare an observed result with a null model. Testing many rules increases the chance that an apparently striking result occurs by luck.

The starting point

An investment researcher tests many signals and reports whichever one looks strongest. Even completely uninformative signals can occasionally appear persuasive by chance. A test must be interpreted in light of how many opportunities there were to find an apparent success.

Under a null hypothesis, a p-value measures the probability of a result at least as extreme as the observed one under the specified test. It does not give the probability that the null is true. Repeated selection can undermine the interpretation of the smallest p-value.

Theory and method

Holm’s step-down procedure

Familywise error rate · Sequentially rejective Bonferroni

Your team tested four ideas. Can it control the probability of any false rejection while retaining more discoveries than a single common cutoff?

A group of analysts tries several signals and would like to report the ones passing the same research family. Ordinary individual p-values no longer control the chance of at least one false alarm across that family. Holm’s procedure starts with the smallest p-value and initially demands strong evidence. Each time a hypothesis passes, fewer untested hypotheses remain, so the next comparison can use a less severe threshold. The first failure stops additional rejections.

How the result is built

Order p₍₁₎ ≤ ⋯ ≤ p₍ₘ₎. Test p₍ᵢ₎ ≤ α/(m−i+1) until the first failure.

m is the number of hypotheses in the declared family, i their rank starting at one, p each valid individual p-value and α the desired familywise error rate. Rejections apply only to the ordered hypotheses that pass before stopping.

  1. At α=0.05, sort four p-values into 0.009, 0.014, 0.031 and 0.040. Compare 0.009 with 0.05/4 = 0.0125 and reject the first.
  2. Compare 0.014 with 0.05/3 ≈ 0.01667 and reject the second. Compare 0.031 with 0.05/2 = 0.025 and stop on failure.
  3. Do not reject the fourth even though 0.040 is below 0.05. The stopping rule is part of what controls the family error probability.

When the reasoning holds

  • The p-values must be valid for their individual null hypotheses and the declared family must include all relevant searches. Leaving out failed variants can invalidate the guarantee.
  • Use ranked order and stop at the first failure. Holm allows dependence between tests; searching new rules after seeing this result creates a new selection problem.
Terms in this lesson
Standard error
An estimate of how much a statistic would vary across repeated samples under the assumed sampling process.
How to read the notation
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.

All notation examples

First example

With 20 independent tests of true null hypotheses at a 5% threshold, the chance of at least one false rejection is 1 − 0.95²⁰, about 64%. Real tests can be correlated, so this exact calculation depends on independence.

Construct a null comparison

A trading team estimates whether a signal improves average returns after costs. Before looking at results, state the null value and the alternative. The null might specify zero mean improvement. A two-sided alternative allows both gains and losses. The test statistic measures the observed deviation from the null in standard-error units.

Under suitable conditions z = (estimate − null)/standard error can use a standard normal reference. A two-sided p-value is the probability under the null model of a statistic at least as extreme in either direction. Choosing one-sided testing only after learning the sign wrongly changes the question and invalidates the nominal error rate.

False alarms versus missed effects

A Type I error rejects a true null. The significance level sets its probability under the specified testing procedure and assumptions. A Type II error fails to reject a false null at a particular alternative. Power is one minus that error probability. Low precision or a small true effect can leave a study with low power.

Reducing the rejection threshold can reduce false positives while making a real effect harder to detect at fixed sample size. A p-value is not the probability the hypothesis is false, nor the probability the strategy will make money next year. Profitability also depends on effect size, fees, capacity, implementation and stability beyond the sample.

Searching many ideas

Testing twenty rules and announcing only the best makes selection part of the experiment. For m independent tests of true nulls at level α, the chance of at least one false rejection is 1−(1−α)ᵐ. This number can be much larger than α. Dependence changes the exact family probability.

A simple conservative procedure sets each test’s cutoff to α/m, the Bonferroni rule. It controls the probability of one or more false rejections at most α even without independence when the individual p-values are valid. Holding out a genuine second sample for a frozen rule gives a separate check, provided the analyst does not continue tuning on it.

Method

Plan a test before looking

  1. Write the claim

    State null, direction, test unit, horizon and inclusion rule before using results to select among alternatives.

  2. Compute and disclose

    Show the estimate, standard error, statistic, uncertainty interval and the number of examined variants, including rejected ideas.

  3. Protect a fresh check

    Prespecify a multiplicity adjustment or freeze the chosen rule before evaluating unused data. Explain remaining costs and risks separately from statistical evidence.

Worked case

Six proposed lending alerts

An analyst tries six equally planned alerts against a family significance level of 5%. The smallest observed p-value is 0.012 for a rule estimating an €8 gain per account, before €10 implementation costs per account.

Working

  1. The unadjusted 0.012 is below 0.05, but the Bonferroni threshold is 0.05/6 ≈ 0.00833. No alert meets it if 0.012 is the smallest.
  2. Even taking the selected €8 estimate at face value, an €8 gross gain less €10 cost is a €2 estimated net loss per account.
  3. If all six tests were independent true nulls, the probability of at least one unadjusted 5% rejection is 1−0.95⁶ ≈ 26.49%.

Interpretation

A holdout analysis should test one fixed definition and estimate net effects. Choosing another alert after seeing the holdout would turn that data into part of model development.

A common mistake

Failure to reject is reported as proof that the effect is unimportant.

How to correct it

Check the confidence interval and the decision-relevant effect size. A study can be too imprecise to distinguish no gain from a sizeable useful gain.

Quick check
An original forecasting rule is selected using all the historical observations. What is the cleanest next check?

Keeping the second sample independent of rule selection gives the rule a fresh opportunity to succeed or fail. Retuning after seeing that sample would use up this independence.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A desk tests 10 forecasting rules and plans 5% familywise error control. The best rule has mean gain 0.6 percentage points, standard error 0.25 points and two-sided unadjusted p = 0.016. Costs are 0.7 points.

  1. Part A · 4 marks

    Calculate the standardized statistic and apply the Bonferroni rule.

  2. Part B · 4 marks

    Calculate estimated net gain and give a valid independent check.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 4 marks

Statistic = 0.6/0.25 = 2.4 relative to a zero null. Threshold is 0.05/10 = 0.005. The p-value 0.016 exceeds it, so this selection does not pass the prespecified family threshold.

Part B 4 marks

Net estimate is 0.6 − 0.7 = −0.1 percentage points. Freeze the rule, costs and decision thresholds before evaluating untouched later data, and report the uncertainty of the new net estimate.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Your team tested four ideas. Can it control the probability of any false rejection while retaining more discoveries than a single common cutoff?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would report the whole ordered family. The first two pass their thresholds, the third fails 0.025, and the fourth remains unrejected. Holm controls the chance of at least one false rejection at the chosen level under valid component tests. I would still report effect sizes and implementation costs because passing the statistical rule does not establish a useful trading gain.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if one analyst originally tested eight extra failed variants before reporting these four?

Compare with the follow-up answer

I would reconstruct the actual research family or set aside an untouched validation sample with a frozen specification. Running Holm on only the four surviving choices ignores prior selection. Without the missing tests or a valid separate design, the claimed 5% familywise control is unsupported.
