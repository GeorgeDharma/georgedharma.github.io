Conditional probability

Revise a probability when new evidence arrives. A useful warning indicator can still produce many false alarms if default is initially rare.

The starting point

A bank’s software flags some borrowers for review. Even a useful flag may identify many customers who repay. To interpret a flag, start with the base rate, the fraction of borrowers who would default before you see the warning.

Conditional probability changes the relevant group. The probability of a warning among defaulters uses all defaulters as the denominator. The probability of default among warned borrowers uses all flagged borrowers instead. Confusing these fractions can dramatically overstate individual risk.

Theory and method

Bayes’ theorem in odds form

Likelihood ratio · Posterior odds

A credit alert is four times as likely among borrowers who default. Does seeing the alert mean default is now more likely than repayment?

A timely alert can be diagnostically useful even when most flagged borrowers repay. An analyst should compare how likely the alert is in each underlying group and how common default was before observing it. Convert the prior probability into odds, multiply by the alert’s likelihood ratio and convert back to a probability. This separates what the new evidence contributes from what was already known about the borrower population.

How the result is built

Posterior odds = prior odds × LR; odds = p/(1−p); posterior p = odds/(1+odds).

p denotes a default probability. LR is the probability of the alert under default divided by the probability of the alert under repayment. Odds express default chances relative to repayment chances.

  1. Starting default probability is 10%, giving prior odds 0.10/0.90 = 1/9.
  2. Suppose the alert appears in 80% of eventual defaults and 20% of repayments. The likelihood ratio is 0.80/0.20 = 4.
  3. Posterior odds are 4/9. The corresponding probability is (4/9)/(1+4/9) = 4/13, about 30.77%. Repayment remains more likely.

When the reasoning holds

  • The likelihoods must be credible for this population and prediction horizon. A warning calibrated for distressed firms may behave differently for new retail borrowers.
  • Successive likelihood ratios can be multiplied directly only with suitable conditional independence or correct conditional estimates. Two alerts copied from one event add little evidence.
Terms in this lesson
Probability of default (PD)
The chance that the borrower meets the specified default definition over the stated horizon.
How to read the notation
Conditional probability · P(A | B)
Read the vertical bar as “given.” If four of 100 late-paying customers default, P(default | late payment) in that group is 4%. To find P(late payment | default), start with the group of customers who defaulted.
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.

All notation examples

First example

Out of 1,000 borrowers, 20 default. The system flags 15 of those and flags 85 of the 980 who repay. It catches 15/20 = 75% of defaults, but only 15/(15 + 85) = 15% of flagged borrowers default.

Partition the evidence

A bank sees a late payment and wants to know the chance of eventual default. One way to avoid reversing conditional probabilities is to build a table with actual default status as rows and warning status as columns. Each cell is a joint count or joint probability of both events occurring.

For any events A and B with P(B) positive, P(A given B) = P(A and B)/P(B). Multiply a prior probability of A by P(B given A) to obtain the joint probability. Then divide by the overall probability of the evidence. That denominator must include all ways the evidence can arise, including warnings from customers who repay.

Sensitivity, specificity and predictive value

Sensitivity is the share of actual defaults flagged. Specificity is the share of nondefaults correctly left unflagged. The false-positive rate is one minus specificity. Positive predictive value measures the share of flagged cases that actually default. This fraction depends on prevalence, the underlying rate of default.

Moving the same warning rule to a population with a different base rate can change its predictive value even if sensitivity and specificity stay stable. Predictive value can also change because customer behaviour shifts and the rule itself performs differently. Distinguish these mechanisms when interpreting a change in an alert dashboard.

From updated probability to action

A forecasted probability helps make a decision only together with the consequences of the alternatives. A manual review that costs €50 may be worthwhile if it can prevent a large expected loss. The same review may be wasteful for tiny accounts. State what the review would actually change, its effectiveness and whom its cost applies to.

When several warnings are combined, their likelihoods can only be multiplied directly under suitable conditional independence assumptions. Two alerts derived from the same missed payment may add little information beyond the first. A joint count or a model of the second warning given both the first warning and default status is stronger evidence.

Method

Use natural frequencies

  1. Start with a cohort

    Choose a convenient population size and allocate it into defaults and nondefaults using the initial base rate.

  2. Apply the rule

    Fill the alert and no-alert counts separately within each group using sensitivity and false-positive rates.

  3. Condition and decide

    Use only flagged rows or columns as the denominator for the relevant post-alert question. If making a decision, attach explicit costs and benefits.

Worked case

Support tickets at an online lender

Among 10,000 comparable accounts, 2% will default. An alert catches 80% of these and flags 10% of nondefaults. A review costs €10 per alert and, by assumption, prevents €100 of loss on every true flagged default and none otherwise.

Working

  1. There are 200 defaults and 9,800 nondefaults. Alerts include 160 defaults and 980 nondefaults, or 1,140 in total.
  2. Conditional default probability among alerts is 160/1,140 ≈ 14.04%. An alert is informative relative to the prior 2% even though most alerts concern nondefaults.
  3. Reviewing all alerts costs €11,400 and saves €16,000, an expected net benefit of €4,600 under the stated effectiveness.

Interpretation

If only half the losses on true defaults could be prevented, savings would be €8,000 and this particular review policy would have a negative expected net return.

At the board

Read a credit-screening result in both directions

In a fictional historical cohort, 40 of 1,000 borrowers defaulted. A warning system flagged 30 of those 40 and flagged 96 of the 960 other borrowers. A flag sends a case to manual review.

Later defaulted301040

The system detected 30 / 40 = 75% of the defaults. A reviewer with a flagged case wants the reverse probability, 30 / 126 = 23.8%. Among unflagged cases, 10 / 874 = 1.14% defaulted. All three answers are correct for different populations. Threshold changes alter missed defaults and review workload. Do not carry this percentage into a new population without checking its base default rate.

A common mistake

The false-positive rate is quoted as the probability that a flagged borrower will repay.

How to correct it

The false-positive rate conditions on nondefaults. The second statement conditions on alerts. Build the full table to identify the requested denominator.

Quick check
How many defaults occur among people the system did not flag?

Five of the 20 defaults were missed. The unflagged group contains these five along with the unflagged borrowers who repaid.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A population of 2,000 merchants has a 5% true fraud rate. A rule flags 70% of frauds and 10% of genuine merchants. Each review costs €12 and averts €40 of loss for every detected fraud.

  1. Part A · 5 marks

    Find the number flagged, true positives and predictive value.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Calculate the expected net benefit of reviewing every flag and the break-even amount saved per true detection.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 5 marks

There are 100 frauds and 1,900 genuine merchants. Flags include 70 frauds and 190 genuine, total 260. Predictive value is 70/260 ≈ 26.92%.

Part B 3 marks

Reviews cost 260 × €12 = €3,120. Savings are 70 × €40 = €2,800, net −€320. Break-even savings per detection are €3,120/70 ≈ €44.57.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A credit alert is four times as likely among borrowers who default. Does seeing the alert mean default is now more likely than repayment?

Compare with a spoken answer

I would raise the default estimate but would still regard repayment as the more likely outcome. The likelihood ratio is four, so prior odds of one to nine become four to nine. That corresponds to roughly 31% probability. I would use that probability alongside exposure, possible loss and intervention cost before deciding whether to stop a loan or trigger a review.

Interviewer’s follow-up

A second independent alert has a likelihood ratio of three. What changes if it is conditionally independent given default status?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Under that independence assumption the new odds are (4/9) × 3 = 4/3, so probability is 4/7, about 57.14%. If both alerts arise from the same late instalment, I would need the likelihood of the second given the first before using that multiplication.
