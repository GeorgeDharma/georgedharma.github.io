Is one approval or response rate really higher?

Test equality of two independent population rates under the stated normal approximation.

In this lecture

  1. Define the success and who could have it
  2. One rate under the null, two when estimating
  3. Interpret evidence and practical size separately
  4. Rebuild the calculation
  5. Start with counts so the denominators remain visible
  6. Two randomly assigned reminder groups
  7. Independent exam problem
  8. Explain it in an interview
Theory and terminology

Two-sample inference for proportions

pooled z test · difference in proportions

What you should be able to explain

  • Test equality of two independent population rates under the stated normal approximation.
  • Give an interval for the absolute rate difference and recognize when the design breaks its assumptions.

Define the success and who could have it

A response rate needs a defined denominator and follow-up window. For a reminder experiment, count one eligible person in one assigned group and define response within, for example, seven days. Comparing a seven-day rate in one group with a 30-day rate in another changes the quantity being measured.

Give the order of subtraction. A change from 12% to 18% is six percentage points and a 50% relative increase. Those descriptions use different denominators. In a budget forecast six extra responses per hundred eligible people is usually easier to convert to staffing requirements.

One rate under the null, two when estimating

Each individual's outcome is binary. If people are independent within and across groups, the variance of a sample proportion is p(1 − p)/n. Variances add when subtracting independent estimates. Under the null that the population rates are identical, all observations can inform one estimate of p. Pool the counts, so a larger group contributes appropriately.

For an interval estimating the difference itself, allow the two rates to differ and estimate each group's variance separately. The normal approximation needs enough expected successes and failures in each group under the null for the test. A common classroom condition is at least ten of each. For the interval check observed successes and failures in each group.

Interpret evidence and practical size separately

For a two-sided 5% test of equality, compare the magnitude of the standardized gap with approximately 1.96. A small p-value measures inconsistency with the equality model and the sampling assumptions. It is not the chance that the effect is false. An interval shows how precisely the gap was estimated.

If the same customers receive both treatments, the two proportions are paired and their covariance matters. If an entire branch receives one treatment, people within a branch may move together. Replication then needs to come from independent branches, not merely more individual records. Two independent arms with rare events may require an exact or other small-sample procedure.

Method

Build the result

z = (pA − pB)/√[p(1 − p)(1/nA + 1/nB)]. Interval = (pA − pB) ± 1.96√[pA(1 − pA)/nA + pB(1 − pB)/nB].

pA and pB are observed fractions, nA and nB their group counts, and pooled p is total successes divided by total observations. z has no units. The approximate 95% interval is in fractions of the eligible population.

  1. Define and calculate the two observed rates in the planned order.
  2. Pool successes and observations under equality, calculate the null standard error and divide the observed gap by it.
  3. For the interval recompute the standard error from the two separate rates. Multiply the endpoints by 100 to report percentage points.
Worked case

Two randomly assigned reminder groups

Of 400 independently assigned people receiving reminder A, 72 respond within seven days. Of 400 assigned reminder B, 48 respond. Everyone has the same follow-up period and no individual appears twice.

Working

  1. Rates are 0.18 and 0.12. The observed difference A minus B is 0.06, or six percentage points.
  2. Under equality the pooled rate is 120/800 = 0.15. Each arm has 60 expected successes and 340 expected failures.
  3. Null standard error is √[0.15 × 0.85 × (2/400)] = 0.02525. z = 2.376 and the approximate two-sided p-value is 0.0175.
  4. The unpooled standard error is 0.02516. The interval is 0.06 ± 1.96 × 0.02516, or 1.07 to 10.93 percentage points.

Decision and interpretation

The sample supports a higher rate for A under this design. If each additional response is expensive to handle, assess both interval endpoints in the staffing decision.

Quick check
For the equality test, group A has 20 successes in 100 and B has 30 in 300. What is the pooled rate?

Pool counts before dividing, giving (20 + 30)/(100 + 300) = 0.125. The unweighted average of 20% and 10% gives equal importance to groups of different sizes.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

A lender randomly assigns 300 clients to each of two new-statement formats. Of the first group 45 enroll in digital billing, versus 30 in the second. Give the two-sided 5% decision and approximate 95% interval for first minus second.

Reveal the worked solution
  1. Observed rates are 15% and 10%. Their difference is five percentage points, and the pooled fraction is 75/600 = 0.125.
  2. The null standard error is 0.02700, producing z = 1.852. Its magnitude is below 1.96 and the two-sided p-value is about 0.064.
  3. The interval uses standard error 0.02693. It runs from −0.28 to 10.28 percentage points. The experiment does not establish equality or rule out a useful improvement.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A new reminder raises the observed response rate from 12% to 18%. Is six percentage points too large to attribute to assignment variation alone?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Compare the gap with its sampling error under equal underlying rates. In two independent groups of 400 the gap is about 2.38 standard errors, giving a two-sided p-value near 0.017.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Would a binned extract with ten reminders per client count as ten independent people?

Compare with the follow-up answer

No. Aggregate to the defined client outcome or model repeated observations with their dependence. Treating reminders as independent can make the standard error too small.
