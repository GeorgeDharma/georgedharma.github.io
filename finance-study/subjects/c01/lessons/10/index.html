Comparing countries

Compare a commodity exporter and an importer using dated series. Separate descriptive indicators, accounting identities and causal explanations.

The starting point

To compare countries, put each indicator on a common basis. Use the same period, definition and units. A current-account figure scaled by GDP compares net external flows with the size of the economy. It answers a different question from the dollar amount of financing needed.

A movement that coincides with an oil-price change can have several explanations. A country also imports other goods, pays foreign investors and receives transfers. Build a comparison across dates, identify which components moved and distinguish measured relationships from a causal claim.

Theory and method

Difference-in-differences and comparative macroeconomic analysis

Parallel trends · Percentage-point change · Counterfactual

An exporter improved its current-account share by four points after a commodity shock while an importer deteriorated by two. What could make the six-point contrast causal?

A country analyst can observe what happened to the exporter and another economy. The missing observation is what would have happened to that same exporter without the exposure being studied. A difference-in-differences comparison uses the contrast economy's change to approximate that unobserved change. It removes fixed level differences and shared time shocks under assumptions. Commodities that affect both economies differently, contemporaneous policy and denominator changes can complicate the economic interpretation.

How the result is built

DiD = (yᴬ₁−yᴬ₀) − (yᴮ₁−yᴮ₀)

y is the measured outcome, here current account as a percentage of GDP. A and B are comparison economies and 0 and 1 are dates. The contrast is in percentage points. DiD denotes difference-in-differences.

  1. Measure the same ratio on comparable annual data. If A moves from −5% to −1% and B from −1% to −3%, their changes are +4 and −2 points.
  2. Subtract the comparator's change to get +6 points. Under parallel counterfactual trends A's predicted untreated outcome is −5 − 2 = −7%.
  3. Compare the observed −1% with that hypothetical −7%. Then test exposure, earlier trends, spillovers and co-occurring policy changes before assigning a causal story.

When the reasoning holds

  • Parallel untreated trends must be economically credible. An exporter and importer can both react directly to the same commodity shock, making the contrast an exposure differential rather than a clean untreated effect.
  • Outcome definitions, timing and denominator measurement must be consistent. A sudden GDP collapse can raise a deficit share even if its dollar value is unchanged.
Terms in this lesson
Percentage point
The difference between two percentages. A rate rising from 4% to 5% rises one percentage point.
Exchange-rate regime
How authorities manage their currency, ranging from a fixed link to a market-driven rate.
Counterfactual
What would probably have happened without the event, decision or policy being studied.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
First example

In one year an exporter records a $6bn surplus on $300bn GDP, or +2%. An importer records a $4bn deficit on $100bn GDP, or −4%. Their balances differ by 6 percentage points of GDP. The difference alone cannot isolate an oil-price effect.

Start with a measurable comparison

“Which country is more exposed?” is too broad to test. Decide whether the outcome is the size of external borrowing relative to income, the amount of debt due within a year, the buying power of exports or the change in consumer prices. Two indicators can support different answers without contradicting each other.

For each country list the series definition, reporting unit, time span, denominator and observation date. Ratios such as current account to GDP require both series in compatible currency units over the same period. Converting one nominal series at a year-end rate and the other at an annual average can manufacture a change.

Distinguish levels, changes and revisions

A deficit of four percent of GDP is a level for the period. A move from minus four to minus two improves the balance by two percentage points. The magnitude of the deficit halves, a separate relative-percent description. Explicitly state the sign used so an improvement cannot be mistaken for a larger deficit.

Historical observations can be revised and some exchange rates or trade prices can be measured with error. Keep the dataset vintage if possible. If a period is missing, say which conclusions can still be drawn rather than silently substituting a value from another year.

Evaluate an explanation against alternatives

Suppose an oil exporter improves after oil prices rise. An importer could serve as a comparison, but the two economies may also differ in policy, exchange-rate regimes and other exports. A before-and-after difference measures the pattern and becomes evidence about causality only under additional assumptions.

Ask what would have happened to the exporter without the price shock. A credible comparison needs similar pre-shock trends or a reason for its counterfactual, and a test for other simultaneous shocks. Use trade components and initial exposure to investigate the mechanism rather than relying on country labels alone.

Method

Make a small comparative data table

  1. Align

    Use the same annual period, denominator and sign convention. Identify nominal versus inflation-adjusted series and any gaps or breaks.

  2. Compute

    Show each balance divided by its GDP and write the single-country change in percentage points before comparing changes.

  3. Interpret

    Specify a mechanism, an alternative and one feasible observation that would distinguish them. State whether the result is descriptive or depends on further assumptions.

Worked case

Two economies around a commodity shock

In year 1 exporter A records a current account of −6 on GDP 200 and importer B records −3 on GDP 150. In year 2 the figures are +2 on 200 and −6 on 150 respectively. These are illustrative data in common annual units.

Working

  1. A starts at −3% and ends at +1%, an improvement of 4 percentage points. B starts at −2% and ends at −4%, a deterioration of 2 points.
  2. The change in A minus the change in B is 4 − (−2) = 6 percentage points. The levels in the final year differ by 5 points.
  3. A commodity-price rise could fit the opposite moves. A separate reduction in A's import demand or a rise in B's profit payments abroad could fit them as well. Examine quantities, prices and income transfers to investigate.

Interpretation

The six-point difference is a change in a gap, not the final gap or an established causal treatment effect. Each answers a different question for a country analyst.

A common mistake

Treating the country with the larger dollar deficit as the one with the larger deficit share.

How to correct it

Divide each deficit by compatible GDP and also report the dollar liability when the financing amount matters.

Quick check
What would best help investigate whether an oil-price rise explains the difference?

The exposures test how sensitive each country should be to oil and the components reveal other changes that might explain the result.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

In year 1 A has a −4 balance on GDP 80 and B −6 on GDP 200. In year 2 A has −2.4 on GDP 80 and B −10 on GDP 250. Both countries report current account receipts less payments.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate the two within-country changes.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    How did A's balance change relative to B's?

  3. Part C · 2 marks

    Give a test for an explanation based on a change in imported fuel prices.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

A moves from −5% to −3%, improving 2 percentage points. B moves from −3% to −4%, deteriorating 1 point.

Part B 2 marks

The comparative change is +2 − (−1) = +3 percentage points. The result describes the change in the gap between the two ratios.

Part C 2 marks

Measure initial net fuel-import exposure for each country and separate fuel price and volume effects in the trade balance. Compare pre-shock patterns and other simultaneous changes before assigning causality.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

An exporter improved its current-account share by four points after a commodity shock while an importer deteriorated by two. What could make the six-point contrast causal?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would report six percentage points as the observed contrast and say what would make it causal. Without the studied exposure, both countries would need to have followed comparable changes. I would examine pre-trends and separate the commodity price and volume effects, alongside policy and GDP changes. If the importer is itself harmed by the same shock, six points measures their relative exposure and can overstate the exporter's isolated gain.

Interviewer’s follow-up

The exporter introduced an import ban in the same quarter. How would you investigate?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Disaggregate imports by affected product, compare with other similarly exposed exporters without the ban and test pre-policy trends. If the ban changes the same outcome at the same time without a credible comparison, describe the six-point differential and acknowledge that the mechanisms cannot be separately identified.
