Trade and terms of trade

A change in export prices alters the income obtainable from the same production. Follow the effect through import demand, public revenues and private borrowing.

The starting point

A country’s terms of trade compare the prices it receives for exports with the prices it pays for imports. If its export prices rise relative to import prices, each unit exported can purchase more imports even if production volume stays unchanged.

A gain benefits the owners of the exports first. The effect on households and government depends on who earns the proceeds, how taxes work and whether the price change lasts. A temporary commodity boom can encourage long-lived borrowing against income that later disappears.

Theory and method

Terms of trade and the Marshall–Lerner condition

Income terms of trade · J-curve

A currency depreciates and the goods trade deficit initially widens. Does that refute the argument that depreciation can improve trade?

A ferry company pays for imported fuel under a shipment contract. The dollar invoice immediately costs more local currency after depreciation, even before the company can reduce the order. Foreign buyers may also need time to switch toward domestic goods. Relative traded prices, physical quantities and the trade balance therefore follow different paths. The Marshall–Lerner result concerns quantity responses under particular pricing and starting-balance assumptions. A short-lived initial deterioration is compatible with a later improvement.

How the result is built

ToT = Pₓ/Pₘ ; local balanced-trade Marshall–Lerner test: abs(ηₓ) + abs(ηₘ) > 1

ToT is the terms of trade using comparable export and import price indices Pₓ and Pₘ. ηₓ and ηₘ are export and import demand elasticities, dimensionless percentage quantity responses to percentage relative-price changes. abs denotes the absolute magnitude.

  1. Start with balanced trade and suppose export prices are fixed in home currency while import prices are fixed in foreign currency. Depreciation initially raises the home cost of each import.
  2. Export volume can grow as foreign customers face lower prices. Import volume can fall as domestic buyers face higher prices. Compare both quantity gains with the import-price effect.
  3. For a small depreciation under the stated assumptions, elasticities of 0.7 and 0.5 sum to 1.2 and support a local eventual improvement. Elasticities of 0.2 and 0.3 sum to 0.5 and do not.

When the reasoning holds

  • The simple threshold assumes initially balanced trade and the specified price pass-through. Initial imbalances or foreign-currency export pricing change the weighted condition.
  • Quantities must have time to adjust. Fixed shipping contracts or production limits weaken short-run responses and can generate a J-curve.
Terms in this lesson
Revenue
Amount earned by selling goods or services. The customer may pay later.
How to read the notation
Expected value · E[X]
Multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. With equal chances of €10 and €30, E[X] = €20. A single outcome can differ from the average.

All notation examples

First example

A country sells copper and imports machinery. Both price indices start at 100. Copper rises to 120 and machinery to 110. Its terms-of-trade index becomes 120/110 × 100 = 109.1. The gain is about 9.1% despite a 20% rise in the copper price.

Separate export prices from trade volumes

Suppose the country sends one tonne of cocoa abroad in exchange for imported machinery. If the cocoa price rises relative to the machine price, each tonne commands more machines. Terms of trade measure this price relationship without requiring a change in tonnes exported.

Use a price index for the exports and another for the imports, with compatible base periods. Divide the export index by the import index. To report a base-100 terms-of-trade index, multiply the resulting ratio by 100. Prices and quantities then answer distinct questions.

Link the price gain to purchasing power

A better relative export price can raise the purchasing power of national production even when real GDP volume changes little. Gross benefits depend on export quantities as well as the price movement. If a storm halves output while raising the export price, the country can still have lower export receipts.

Who receives the additional income matters. Producers can be foreign-owned, royalties can flow to government, and imported inputs can become more expensive. Trace the part of the gain actually available to residents before discussing household consumption or public borrowing capacity.

Distinguish the current account response

A lasting price gain might raise desired consumption and investment, including imports. A temporary gain might be saved abroad to smooth spending after prices fall. Different expectations about the persistence of the change can therefore produce different current-account paths even with identical observed prices today.

A commodity producer's response also depends on extraction constraints, fiscal rules and currency denomination. An improved price ratio alone does not establish a trade surplus. To calculate the trade balance, multiply actual prices by actual quantities on each side.

Method

Price a trade bundle

  1. Compute relative prices

    Use the same base date. Divide export prices by import prices and label the ratio or index units.

  2. Measure buying power

    Multiply export volume by export price, then divide by import price to find how many imports the revenue can buy.

  3. Calculate net trade separately

    Deduct the actual import bill from actual export receipts. Compare the amount with the earlier period and explain any difference with the index.

Worked case

A crop shortage during an export boom

Initially a country exports 100 sacks at $4 each and imports 50 tools at $8 each. Later the export price rises to $5, the tool price rises to $9 and only 72 sacks can be harvested. Import volume remains 50 tools.

Working

  1. The export price index is 125 and the import price index is 112.5. Terms of trade rise to 125/112.5 × 100 = 111.11.
  2. Originally exports earned $400 and bought 50 tools. The new harvest earns 72 × $5 = $360 and buys 40 tools.
  3. The import bill is now 50 × $9 = $450. The trade balance changes from zero to −$90 even as the terms of trade improve.

Interpretation

The smaller harvest overwhelms the improvement in the price received per sack. A policy maker deciding on import finance needs quantities and settlement dates in addition to relative prices.

A common mistake

Reading a 10% terms-of-trade gain as a 10% increase in export revenue.

How to correct it

Specify what is held fixed. Relative prices measure imports per export unit. Revenue requires the export quantity and its own price.

Quick check
Suppose export prices stay at 120 and import prices then rise to 120. Where is the index?

Both price indices are now 120. Their ratio is one, so the terms-of-trade index returns to 100.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

An economy initially exports 120 units at 10 and imports 100 units at 12. Later export prices fall to 9, import prices fall to 10, exports stay at 120 and imports rise to 115.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate the percentage change in the terms of trade.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Find the new trade balance.

  3. Part C · 2 marks

    Explain how export purchasing power and the trade deficit can both rise.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

The initial ratio is 10/12. The new ratio is 9/10. The growth factor is 0.90/(10/12) = 1.08, so the relative price improves 8%.

Part B 2 marks

Export receipts are 120 × 9 = 1,080. Import spending is 115 × 10 = 1,150. The new balance is −70 compared with zero initially.

Part C 2 marks

New receipts can buy 1,080/10 = 108 import units, up from 100. Actual import volume rose to 115, more than the receipts can finance.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A currency depreciates and the goods trade deficit initially widens. Does that refute the argument that depreciation can improve trade?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would check when quantities can change. If import contracts are fixed, the local invoice can rise today while export demand responds only later. Under balanced initial trade and conventional pass-through, the Marshall–Lerner result tests whether the combined eventual elasticities exceed one. It does not promise that every country or the first quarter improves. I would ask for invoice currencies, volume data and the original trade balance.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Both demand elasticities appear high, but factories are already at capacity. Is that sufficient evidence of an export surge?

Compare with the follow-up answer

No. Foreign demand may increase while domestic producers lack capacity to fill orders. A quantity forecast needs export supply, delivery times and pricing assumptions. The textbook elasticity mechanism can overpredict immediate improvement under these constraints.
