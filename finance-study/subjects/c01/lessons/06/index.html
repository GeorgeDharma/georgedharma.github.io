Exchange-rate regimes

Under a peg, the central bank may buy or sell reserves to maintain the rate. Under a float, the exchange rate helps absorb shocks.

The starting point

An exchange rate puts a price on one currency in another. Under a peg, the central bank undertakes to keep that price at or near a specified level. Under a float, the market price can move as households and firms change their currency purchases.

To defend a domestic currency under selling pressure, the central bank can supply foreign currency and buy its own. This uses foreign reserves. Under a float a weaker domestic currency raises the local cost of imports and foreign-currency debt, even as domestic production may become cheaper for foreign customers.

Theory and method

Central-bank balance-sheet approach to a peg

Sterilized intervention · Monetary base

If a central bank buys dollars to hold down its currency, can it prevent the domestic banking system's reserves from increasing?

A central bank paying local money for foreign currency acquires a foreign asset and initially credits domestic banks' reserve balances. If it also sells a domestic security, buyers pay it with domestic money, which can reverse the reserve injection. The two operations change the composition of the central bank's assets. Following both sides of each transaction shows how it can stabilize domestic liquidity for a while even while accumulating or losing foreign reserves.

How the result is built

ΔB = ΔNFA + ΔNDA with other balance-sheet items fixed

B is domestic base money, NFA net foreign assets and NDA net domestic assets, all expressed in local currency. Δ means a change over the transaction window. Changes here exclude valuation gains and unrelated government balances.

  1. Purchase $3m at 5 home units per dollar. Net foreign assets rise 15m home and paying domestic banks creates 15m of reserve balances.
  2. Sell domestic government securities for 15m home. Domestic assets decline 15m and buyers' banks surrender 15m in reserve balances.
  3. Combine both sides. The monetary base changes by +15 − 15 = 0 while the foreign asset holding still increases by $3m.

When the reasoning holds

  • The simplified balance sheet holds government deposits, capital and other liabilities fixed. Accounting revaluations of foreign assets need their actual counterpart.
  • Sterilization needs eligible domestic instruments and can create a carry cost when domestic borrowing yields exceed earnings on foreign reserves.
Terms in this lesson
Exchange-rate regime
How authorities manage their currency, ranging from a fixed link to a market-driven rate.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Foreign exchange (FX)
The conversion of one currency into another. Always state how the rate is quoted.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Interest rate
The price of borrowing money or the return for lending it, quoted as a percentage over a stated period.
Revenue
Amount earned by selling goods or services. The customer may pay later.
First example

At a peg of 5 local units per dollar, firms demand $30m and private sellers offer $22m. To meet the gap at the peg, the central bank sells $8m and receives 40m local units.

Why a peg consumes or accumulates reserves

Think of the central bank as a dealer promising to buy or sell foreign currency at a stated price. If importers demand more foreign currency than exporters and investors supply, defending the price requires the dealer to supply the shortfall out of usable reserves or other foreign funding.

Under buying pressure on the domestic currency the flows reverse. The central bank may purchase incoming foreign currency and issue domestic money. A peg can therefore create both reserve shortages and expansion of the domestic money supply, depending on the direction of market pressure.

Distinguish intervention from sterilization

When the central bank buys dollars with newly created local currency, domestic bank reserves can increase. Sterilization is a second transaction intended to offset the domestic liquidity effect, for example by selling domestic securities to absorb bank reserves. It can change domestic liquidity while leaving the original foreign exchange purchase in place.

The central bank cannot indefinitely sell usable foreign assets it does not have. Borrowing reserves or changing interest rates may buy time, but each has an associated cost. Published gross reserves may include funds already pledged or operationally unavailable.

What a float adjusts and what it leaves exposed

In a float the exchange rate may change until willing purchases and sales balance. A depreciation makes a fixed foreign price more expensive in local currency. It also raises local-currency revenues per unit of foreign sales if foreign prices and volumes are unchanged.

The adjustment can be uneven across sectors. An exporter with domestic costs and foreign receipts may benefit, while a domestic utility owing foreign debt can face higher debt service. The consequences depend on pricing contracts, how quickly volumes respond and how much foreign-currency debt is hedged.

Method

Calculate the cost of maintaining the rate

  1. Identify the quote

    A peg of 4 local units per dollar fixes the cost of one dollar. Keep reserves in dollars and changes in domestic money in local units.

  2. Measure private imbalance

    At the peg, subtract private foreign-currency supply from demand. A positive gap calls for a central-bank sale if no other funding appears.

  3. Track reserve capacity

    Deduct sales from freely usable reserves and test a minimum buffer. Keep a separate column for any domestic liquidity operation.

Worked case

A central bank's operating buffer

The peg is 4 local units per dollar. Monthly private demand for dollars is 45m and private supply 33m. Gross reserves are $62m, including $14m pledged. An additional $12m must remain as an operating buffer.

Working

  1. Usable unpledged reserves are 62 − 14 = $48m. Reserves available above the buffer are $36m.
  2. The market gap is 45 − 33 = $12m per month. The bank sells $12m and receives 48m local units per month if it fills the entire gap.
  3. Three such months use $36m and leave $12m unpledged. A fourth identical sale would breach the policy buffer unless the authorities obtain foreign funding or the imbalance changes.

Interpretation

The three-month result assumes stable private flows. A credibility shock could increase dollar demand immediately, so it is a conditional arithmetic result.

A common mistake

Treating all published reserves as immediately available to meet private withdrawals.

How to correct it

Identify pledged assets, settlement constraints, the chosen buffer and any committed foreign credit line before estimating coverage.

Quick check
If that $8m gap persists for three periods, what happens to reserves assuming no other flows?

The bank sells $8m of its foreign assets each period. The local currency it buys is a different side of the transaction.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

A country pegs at 7 local units per dollar. In one week private dollar supply is $28m and demand is $34m. It begins with $40m freely usable reserves and has no other flows.

  1. Part A · 2 marks

    Give the amount and direction of intervention.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Find ending reserves and the immediate domestic liquidity effect absent sterilization.

  3. Part C · 3 marks

    Identify an offsetting domestic operation and explain whether it restores the dollars.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 2 marks

The central bank sells $6m. It receives 6 × 7 = 42m local units to supply dollars at the peg.

Part B 2 marks

Reserves fall to $34m. The domestic money paid to the bank drains 42m local units from the market under the simplified settlement assumptions.

Part C 3 marks

The bank could buy domestic securities and pay out 42m local units to re-inject the liquidity. This offsets the domestic effect. It does not reverse the $6m fall in foreign reserves.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

If a central bank buys dollars to hold down its currency, can it prevent the domestic banking system's reserves from increasing?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Yes. I would trace the initial credit to domestic banks and then an equal, opposite liquidity operation. Buying $3m at 5 creates 15m home units; selling 15m of securities withdraws them. That leaves base money unchanged and foreign assets higher. I would check available domestic securities, the cost of sterilization and subsequent pressure on the peg before assuming the same policy can run indefinitely.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If the bank is instead selling dollars and has only $2m freely usable, does buying domestic securities restore dollar reserves?

Compare with the follow-up answer

No. Domestic purchases can replace local liquidity drained by a dollar sale. The bank still needs actual foreign assets, incoming foreign receipts, international borrowing or a policy adjustment to meet the next demand for dollars.
