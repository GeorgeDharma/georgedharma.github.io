Borrowing across dates

Foreign borrowing can move consumption forward or finance productive investment. Sustainability depends on future income relative to obligations.

The starting point

Borrowing from abroad lets residents use resources now and pay for them later. This can smooth consumption after a temporary bad harvest, or pay for an investment that raises future production. The lender receives a claim on future resources.

In a two-date model with certain cash flows, compare the resources gained today with the discounted future repayment. A country must also be able to transfer the promised currency on the payment date. Refinancing delays that test and introduces uncertainty about the next loan.

Theory and method

Intertemporal current-account model

Permanent income hypothesis · Consumption smoothing

Why can a temporary export-income shock produce a current-account deficit while an equally large permanent shock does not?

An islander chooses meals in two years and can borrow or lend abroad. A lost harvest today leaves lifetime resources almost intact when the next harvest is normal. Borrowing allows part of that burden to be spread over both years. If both harvests permanently fall, borrowing does little to replace the lost lifetime resources. Comparing the two income paths clarifies why the persistence of an external shock can matter as much as its size in year one.

How the result is built

C₀ + C₁/(1+r) = Y₀ + Y₁/(1+r) ; CA₀ = Y₀ − C₀ in the endowment example

C and Y denote consumption and endowment income. Subscripts 0 and 1 are the two dates, r the world interest rate. CA₀ is the first-date current account. There is no investment, government or initial net foreign wealth.

  1. Start at incomes of 100 in each period, r = 0, and equal-period preferences that favour equal consumption. The feasible smooth path consumes 100 twice.
  2. A temporary fall gives incomes 80 and 100. Total resources are 180, so optimal equal consumption is 90 and the first-period current account is −10.
  3. If incomes are instead 80 and 80, equal consumption is 80 and the current account is zero. Both examples lose 20 of first-period income.

When the reasoning holds

  • The numerical example assumes equal weights, diminishing marginal utility and zero interest, which make equal consumption optimal across two dates.
  • Borrowing must be accessible. If lenders cap borrowing at 4, the smoothly chosen consumption of 90 is infeasible in the temporary-shock example.
Terms in this lesson
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Principal
The amount borrowed or invested before interest is added.
Revenue
Amount earned by selling goods or services. The customer may pay later.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
First example

A mill borrows 100 dollars for one year at 5% to install a machine. The machine earns an extra 112 dollars after costs next year. After repaying 105, the mill keeps 7. If the extra receipts were 101, it would need 4 from another source.

Write a budget for two dates

A country can consume more resources today than it produces if it sells assets or borrows from abroad. A promise to repay is a future claim on resources. Draw a timeline with one column for each date and put principal and interest together in the repayment column.

In a two-period economy with no opening debt and no investment, consumption today plus tomorrow's consumption discounted to today equals today's output plus discounted future output if there is no final wealth. A one-unit increase in consumption today reduces feasible future consumption by one plus the borrowing rate.

Match the use of funds with the repayment source

Borrowing for a temporary income shortfall can smooth living standards even if no machine is built. Borrowing for new machinery can increase the future output available for repayment. Assess the value of the project from the extra cash it creates relative to its cost and the return required on alternative uses.

A persistent shortfall is different. If export revenues repeatedly fall below promised payments, rolling over old principal can postpone a cash shortage without improving underlying capacity. Liquidity asks whether a payment can be met when due. Solvency asks whether feasible future resources can cover the obligations over time.

Respect dates, currencies and risk

Cash earned after a deadline cannot pay a loan at maturity without interim finance. A foreign-currency loan requires that currency, or local cash that can be converted on acceptable terms. A positive expected surplus across scenarios can coexist with default in a bad scenario.

The simple intertemporal model assumes access to borrowing and lending at the specified rate. If borrowing is restricted, the model may describe a preferred consumption path that the country cannot finance. A debt-service schedule exposes such constraints more clearly than a lifetime average.

Method

Draw a two-date resource table

  1. Today

    Record production, consumption, investment and net borrowing at the start date. Check whether positive borrowing supplies the shortfall.

  2. Repayment date

    Add future output and project cash, deduct principal and interest, and show the remaining resources available to consume.

  3. Stress the promise

    Reduce or delay the future receipt. Identify the exact shortfall and whether a liquid asset, new income or extra borrowing would fill it.

Worked case

An irrigation investment with a late harvest

An island produces 90 now and would produce 110 next year. It borrows 20 at 10% in foreign currency and spends the entire loan on equipment. Output and receipts in this example can be exchanged one-for-one for the payment currency.

Working

  1. Today's consumption stays at 90. The added equipment, rather than extra consumption, absorbs the 20 foreign resources.
  2. Next year's loan service equals 20 × 1.10 = 22. If additional harvest receipts are 28 before maturity, next year's consumption can reach 110 + 28 − 22 = 116.
  3. The project's incremental present value is −20 + 28/1.10 = 5.45. If the six-unit share of the harvest arriving before maturity were the only cash earmarked for this loan, there would still be a 16-unit liquidity gap at maturity.

Interpretation

The project can be productive overall while its payment schedule requires a bridge loan or access to other receipts. Separating total return from the due date captures both issues.

A common mistake

Using the project's gross sales as the repayment source even though costs and payment dates are unknown.

How to correct it

Subtract incremental operating costs, specify when customers pay and state which currency can actually be delivered.

Quick check
Which fact most directly supports repayment of a one-year dollar loan funding a new project?

Repayment depends on the amount, currency and timing of available resources. Employment and purchase cost alone do not establish them.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

A country expects resources of 80 today and 110 next year. It wants to consume 95 today, invests nothing, has no opening assets and can borrow at 8%. Assume all values are in the loan currency.

  1. Part A · 2 marks

    How much must it borrow, and what is due next year?

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Calculate next year's highest feasible consumption with zero final debt.

  3. Part C · 3 marks

    Suppose a storm reduces next year's output to 90 and essential spending is 78. Quantify the problem.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 2 marks

Borrow 95 − 80 = 15. Principal plus interest equals 15 × 1.08 = 16.2.

Part B 2 marks

Deduct 16.2 from 110, leaving 93.8. Today's increase of 15 has shifted 16.2 of resources out of next year's consumption.

Part C 3 marks

After essential spending only 12 remains for a 16.2 payment. The immediate gap is 4.2. Refinancing could cover the deadline, but the interest and future primary resources determine whether that solution lasts.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why can a temporary export-income shock produce a current-account deficit while an equally large permanent shock does not?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would ask whether future export earnings are expected to recover. With two equal periods, zero interest and free borrowing, losing 20 today but recovering next year makes households spread the loss, consuming 90 in both years and borrowing 10. Losing 20 in each period instead lowers sustainable annual consumption to 80. A government assessing its reserves also needs to test a binding lending limit or a slower recovery.

Interviewer’s follow-up

How would you recognize an overoptimistic consumption-smoothing narrative?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Compare projected future net export receipts with the scheduled foreign-currency repayments. If recovery repeatedly shifts later, or borrowers must renew debt merely to pay interest, the assumed temporary shock may be persistent and the planned consumption path infeasible.
