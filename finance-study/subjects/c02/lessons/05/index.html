Foreign Exchange and Multinational Financial Management

Balance-sheet exposures

Transaction exposure concerns an identified payment. Translation affects group reporting. Economic exposure concerns future competitiveness, prices and market share.

The starting point

A company can face several currency risks at once. Transaction exposure arises from a specific foreign-currency receipt or payment. Translation exposure arises when group accounts express a foreign operation’s financial statements in the reporting currency.

Economic exposure concerns future business conditions. A competitor producing in a newly cheaper currency may cut prices, even if your company invoices every customer in its own currency. To identify the exposure, ask whether the currency movement changes a contracted cash amount, a reported balance, or a future business opportunity.

Theory and method

IAS 21 functional currency and monetary-item remeasurement

Transaction exposure · Translation of a foreign operation · Economic exposure

A subsidiary keeps local books but invoices most sales in dollars. Why is identifying its functional currency the first step in explaining its FX results?

A company may present group statements in euros, keep invoices in dollars and pay staff locally. The functional currency reflects the primary economic environment in which it operates. A monetary receivable fixes an amount of currency to collect. Changes in its functional-currency value can produce an exchange difference even before payment. Translating the net assets of a foreign operation for consolidated reporting is a different exercise with a different exposure and potentially different presentation.

How the result is built

FX difference on a simple foreign monetary receivable = Q(S₁ − S₀)

Q is the fixed foreign amount owed. S₀ is transaction-date functional currency per foreign unit and S₁ the closing or settlement rate. The expression gives the change in the functional-currency carrying amount between those dates, before any designated hedge.

  1. Determine functional currency from the economic environment and relevant indicators. A convenient reporting choice alone does not settle it.
  2. Record the initial receivable at transaction-date spot. With $100,000 and €0.92 per dollar, the initial euro amount is €92,000.
  3. At year-end €0.88 per dollar gives €88,000, a €4,000 exchange loss for the simple euro-functional receivable. A later settlement at €0.90 produces another €2,000 gain relative to year-end.

When the reasoning holds

  • The example assumes a plain monetary receivable with no hedge designation or special net-investment treatment. Different items or relationships can affect presentation.
  • Evidence about prices, costs and financing is needed to establish functional currency. A foreign operation's reporting translation must be evaluated separately from a single invoice.
Terms in this lesson
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Settlement
The final transfer of cash or securities needed to complete a transaction.
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
First example

A euro-reporting group expects a contracted $1m receipt. At €0.95 per dollar that is €950,000. At €0.90 it is €900,000. The €50,000 loss on the invoice is transaction exposure. A change in the reported euro value of its foreign factory is a separate issue.

Distinguish a payment contract from a reporting currency

A French parent can keep euro accounts while a foreign subsidiary pays wages in pesos and makes sales in pesos. The subsidiary's functional currency reflects its main economic environment. The group chooses a presentation currency for consolidated statements. They can differ even though the subsidiary's factories and customers do not change.

A foreign-currency customer invoice has a defined amount and date. It can change value in the business's functional currency between sale and collection. A foreign subsidiary's net assets can change value when translated for group reporting even if there is no planned sale or dividend.

Trace exposure through a cash-flow map

Start with contracts and identify who owes which currency on what date. Net same-currency payments against receipts only if timing and legal access permit. Next identify variable sales, purchasing costs and competitor reactions that can change the future stream of operating cash.

A company quoting local prices can still have economic exposure. A competitor might lower its local price after a currency depreciation reduces its own costs. The effect then runs through order volume and margins. It cannot be measured from the face value of a single invoice.

Choose a measure appropriate to the decision

To forecast liquidity on an invoice, translate dated cash flows at alternative settlement rates or executable hedge rates. To investigate reporting volatility, specify the accounting assumptions and which balance-sheet amount is being translated. To assess competitiveness, build an operating scenario for prices, units and costs.

A translation change may become cash relevant if a loan covenant uses reported net worth or if the subsidiary is about to distribute assets. The accounting label alone is insufficient. Explain the mechanism connecting a reporting change to a financing or operating decision.

Method

Separate a group's three exposures

  1. Contracts

    List outstanding customer and supplier amounts in their settlement currencies. Include signed orders only when their enforceability and amount fit the analysis.

  2. Reporting

    Identify the foreign operation's net assets and reporting-rate change. State any simplifying accounting treatment used for the question.

  3. Future business

    Map which sales volumes, input prices and competitor decisions could change after the exchange move. Test these assumptions independently of booked invoices.

Worked case

A group with three currency effects

A euro group has a $300,000 export receivable and a $100,000 payable due together. A separate dollar subsidiary has $2m of net assets. In this simplified exercise apply a closing rate to those net assets. The dollar moves from €0.95 to €0.90.

Working

  1. The group's net contracted dollar inflow is $200,000. Its euro value falls from €190,000 to €180,000, a €10,000 transaction effect.
  2. The subsidiary's translated net assets fall from €1.90m to €1.80m, a €100,000 reporting movement under the assumptions.
  3. If competitors use cheaper dollar-priced inputs to cut euro prices, the group's future European sales may fall. Measuring that effect would require cost and volume assumptions beyond the two booked balances.

Interpretation

Adding €10,000 and €100,000 and calling the sum a cash outflow would confuse remeasurement of a future net receipt with translation of an entire foreign operation.

A common mistake

Assuming invoices denominated in home currency eliminate all currency risk.

How to correct it

Follow competitors, suppliers and customer demand. Future prices and volumes can change even if the amount on today's signed invoice stays fixed.

Quick check
A French manufacturer has no foreign invoices but loses orders when a foreign competitor’s currency weakens. Which exposure is this?

Future demand and competitive prices change. There is no identified foreign-currency invoice or foreign operation being translated in the scenario.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

A home-currency manufacturer has a 70,000 foreign-unit payable and a 20,000 foreign-unit receivable due together. Its foreign subsidiary has net assets of 400,000 foreign units. The exchange rate rises from 1.20 to 1.30 home units per foreign unit.

  1. Part A · 2 marks

    Calculate the net transaction effect.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    What is the translation effect under the simple closing-rate assumption?

  3. Part C · 3 marks

    Give one piece of information needed to quantify economic exposure beyond these amounts.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 2 marks

Net payment is 50,000 foreign units. Cost rises from 60,000 to 65,000 home units, a 5,000 adverse effect on the cash required.

Part B 2 marks

Translated net assets rise from 480,000 to 520,000, a 40,000 positive reporting movement. The question specifies no dividend or sale.

Part C 3 marks

For example, estimate how much demand changes when imported rival products become more expensive, together with the contribution per added unit. Contract values alone do not provide that operating sensitivity.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A subsidiary keeps local books but invoices most sales in dollars. Why is identifying its functional currency the first step in explaining its FX results?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would establish functional currency first because it determines whether the invoice is a foreign-currency monetary item for that business. For a euro-functional seller the $100,000 invoice falls from €92,000 to €88,000 at year-end, then recovers to €90,000 at payment. Translating a dollar-functional subsidiary into euros for group reporting involves its financial statements as a whole. Commercial competitiveness can add a third exposure beyond either accounting movement.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if exactly the same invoice belongs to a dollar-functional subsidiary of the euro parent?

Compare with the follow-up answer

At the subsidiary level a fixed dollar receivable is already denominated in its functional currency, so that invoice does not create the illustrated foreign-currency remeasurement there. The euro group still has to translate the subsidiary's statements and assess its wider net exposure.
