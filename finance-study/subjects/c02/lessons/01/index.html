Quotations and cross rates

Decide which currency is in the numerator. Reversing the quote requires taking its reciprocal, including appropriate treatment of the bid and offer sides.

The starting point

Write a currency quote as an amount with units, such as 1.10 dollars for one euro. Multiplying €50 by $1.10 per euro gives $55. A cross rate joins two quotes by cancelling the common currency just as you would convert metres through centimetres.

A dealer’s bid is the price it pays for the quoted unit and its offer is the price it charges. When reversing a two-sided quote, invert both numbers and switch their order. Otherwise the dealer would appear to buy at a higher price than it sells.

Theory and method

Triangular arbitrage and executable cross rates

Bid–ask spread · Synthetic exchange rate

Three screens disagree about the price of sterling in euros. How do you tell whether someone can lock a profit?

A dealer quotes a different price for buying a currency and selling it. A second route through dollars can create another executable price. Imagine beginning with euros, buying sterling through the cheapest route and selling the resulting pounds back for euros. If the completed round trip leaves more euros after all fees without exposure to later exchange movements, the price difference is actionable. Comparing three midpoints can hide a loss on the actual trades.

How the result is built

Profit = Q × (bᴰ − aˢ) when the direct bid exceeds the synthetic offer

Q is pounds traded, bᴰ is the direct dealer's euro bid per pound and aˢ the executable euro cost per pound of the synthetic purchase. Profit is euros before any separate costs. The reverse direction also needs testing.

  1. With dollars per pound at 1.20–1.22 and euros per dollar at 0.90–0.92, a synthetic euro bid per pound is 1.20 × 0.90 = 1.08.
  2. The synthetic offer is 1.22 × 0.92 = 1.1224. It costs €112,240 to obtain £100,000 by this route.
  3. If a direct dealer buys £100,000 at €1.13 each, the sale realizes €113,000. The locked gross residual is €760 if all legs execute at the stated quantities.

When the reasoning holds

  • Quotes must be contemporaneous, firm and available for the desired size. A displayed indication or partial fill can leave an open position.
  • Settlement dates, funding, fees and counterparty limits must match. Gross profit smaller than total costs is not an executable net opportunity.

Terms in this lesson
Currency quote
The number of units of one named currency exchanged for one unit of another. The order determines whether to multiply or divide.
Settlement
The final transfer of cash or securities needed to complete a transaction.
First example

A dealer buys euros at $1.10 and sells euros at $1.12. Someone selling $1 to this dealer receives 1/1.12 = €0.8929. Buying $1 costs 1/1.10 = €0.9091. The reciprocal quote is therefore €0.8929 bid and €0.9091 offered per dollar.

Read a quote as a conversion instruction

A dealer quotes 1.24–1.26 dollars per euro. The unit being priced is one euro. Selling euros to that dealer earns its bid of 1.24. Buying euros costs its offer of 1.26. Write the currency names beside the numbers before doing arithmetic.

The bid–offer spread compensates the dealer for inventory risk, execution costs and other factors. Headlines often show the midpoint. A customer dealing at the posted prices loses part of a round trip to the spread even if the quotes never change.

Invert an executable market

Inverting a quote turns dollars per euro into euros per dollar. A customer selling dollars is buying euros at the old offer. The new bid is therefore one divided by the old offer. The new offer is one divided by the old bid.

The same principle works without memorizing the switch. Imagine holding one dollar and buying as many euros as the dealer will deliver. Then ask how many euros you must surrender to acquire one dollar. The buying price for the newly quoted unit should exceed its selling price.

Build a cross rate through an intermediary

Some currency pairs can be traded by going through a third currency. To sell sterling for yen through dollars, sell the sterling at the dollar bid and then sell the acquired dollars at the yen bid. To buy sterling through the same intermediaries, use the offers on the reverse path.

Multiplying like quote directions cancels the intermediary unit. Mixed directions require a division or inversion first. A discrepancy relative to a direct dealer quote only creates an executable arbitrage if all legs can actually be placed at favourable bid or offer prices after costs.

Method

Price an executable cross

  1. State the direction

    Write what the customer has, what the customer wants and which currency one unit of each dealer quote prices.

  2. Build both sides

    Trace the sale of one unit through the intermediary at executable rates. Trace the cost of buying a unit through the reverse route.

  3. Check the result

    Carry currency units and verify that the implied bid does not exceed the implied offer. Compare direct and indirect routes on the same side.

Worked case

A buyer needs Swiss francs

A dealer quotes $1.08–1.10 per euro and CHF 0.88–0.90 per dollar. Assume immediate settlement, sufficient volume, and no extra fees.

Working

  1. Selling €1 gives $1.08 and selling those dollars gives 1.08 × 0.88 = CHF 0.9504.
  2. Buying €1 costs $1.10. Obtaining those dollars by paying francs costs 1.10 × 0.90 = CHF 0.9900.
  3. The synthetic quote is CHF 0.9504–0.9900 per euro. A customer selling €20,000 receives CHF 19,008. Inverting the midpoint would not give an executable price.

Interpretation

The 0.0396-franc gap reflects two spreads. A direct dealer offering a narrower spread might improve one or both sides.

A common mistake

Taking the reciprocal of each number without swapping bid and offer.

How to correct it

Imagine selling one unit in the new direction. The amount you receive identifies the bid and exposes any reversed spread.

Quick check
Ignoring spreads, if a euro buys $1.20 and a dollar buys 150 yen, how many yen does €1 buy?

Multiply $1.20 per euro by 150 yen per dollar. The dollars cancel, leaving 180 yen per euro.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

A bank quotes $1.30–1.32 per pound and 140–142 yen per dollar. Another dealer quotes 185–188 yen per pound directly. Ignore additional fees.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find the synthetic yen bid and offer per pound.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Choose the better route to buy £10,000 and quantify the saving.

  3. Part C · 2 marks

    Do the quotes imply a riskless round trip?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

The synthetic bid is 1.30 × 140 = 182. The offer is 1.32 × 142 = 187.44 yen per pound.

Part B 2 marks

Buying through dollars costs 1,874,400 yen. Buying directly costs 1,880,000 yen. The synthetic route saves 5,600 yen.

Part C 2 marks

No. The better buying price is 187.44 and the best selling price is 185. Selling after buying loses 2.44 yen per pound before any other costs.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Three screens disagree about the price of sterling in euros. How do you tell whether someone can lock a profit?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would write a loop in one starting currency and select the dealer side for each leg. Here the executable synthetic offer of €1.1224 is below the direct bid of €1.13. At £100,000 that is €760 gross. I would check firm size, simultaneous execution, settlement and all charges before calling it an arbitrage or instructing a trader to execute.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if only £40,000 can be sold at the direct bid and total fixed charges for the full loop are €400?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The matched gross gain on £40,000 is 40,000 × 0.0076 = €304. After the €400 fixed charges the trade loses €96. Buying additional pounds without an executable sale would add open risk.
