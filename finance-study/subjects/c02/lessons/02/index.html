Purchasing power parity

Compare the price of the same basket in two currencies. Transport costs, non-traded goods and changing baskets weaken short-run predictions.

The starting point

Purchasing power parity (PPP) compares how much currency is required to buy an equivalent basket in two places. Absolute PPP asks what exchange rate would make the baskets cost the same. Relative PPP relates changes in the exchange rate to differences in inflation.

A basket includes goods or services that cannot always be transported or resold across borders. Rent, transport costs, taxes and product quality can prevent prices from equalizing. A PPP gap is therefore an economic comparison, not a guaranteed trading profit or a deadline for the exchange rate to move.

Theory and method

Purchasing power parity and the Balassa–Samuelson effect

Law of one price · Relative PPP · Nontradable prices

Why can a more productive exporting country have systematically more expensive haircuts even when traded-goods prices are aligned?

A factory that produces more tradable output per hour can pay higher wages while competing at the world goods price. If hairdressers can move into factory jobs, salons may have to match these wages to retain staff even when haircuts per hour stay unchanged. A basket containing services then costs more in the productive country. The Balassa–Samuelson mechanism explains why a persistent overall price-level gap can coexist with effective competition in tradable goods.

How the result is built

w ≈ PᵀAᵀ ; Pᴺ ≈ w/Aᴺ ; Pᴺ/Pᵀ ≈ Aᵀ/Aᴺ

w is the wage in common currency per hour, Pᵀ and Pᴺ prices of a tradable unit and a nontradable service, and Aᵀ and Aᴺ their outputs per labour hour. The simplified model uses labour as the production input.

  1. Normalize the common tradable price to one. With competitive labour-only production, a factory producing four units per hour can support a wage of four.
  2. If a salon provides two services per hour and pays that same wage, each service costs two. A country with tradable productivity of two and the same salon productivity charges one.
  3. Relative PPP can still serve as an inflation-based benchmark over time. If relative sector productivity or basket weights shift, a persistent real exchange-rate change can accompany growth.

When the reasoning holds

  • Workers must be mobile enough across domestic sectors for wage pressure to transmit. Large segmentation weakens the salon-price effect.
  • The simple price relation assumes comparable tradable prices and production conditions. Transport costs, markups and quality differences provide additional explanations.
Terms in this lesson
Purchasing power parity
A comparison of what a common basket costs in different currencies. It offers a reference point for comparing price levels.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Benchmark
A reference chosen in advance for comparison, such as a market index or an equal-weight portfolio.
First example

A basket costs €80 at home and $100 abroad. Its PPP rate is $100/€80 = $1.25 per euro. At a market rate of $1.10, the euro basket converts to $88, which is $12 less than the foreign basket.

From one tradable good to a basket

If identical tradable goods can move freely, a large common-currency price gap can attract buyers to the cheap location and sellers to the expensive one. Shipping costs and import restrictions can prevent that adjustment. A restaurant meal also includes local rent and labour that cannot simply be shipped.

Absolute purchasing power parity extends the comparison to a basket. Express both prices per copy of the same basket. The implied exchange rate is home-currency basket cost divided by foreign-currency basket cost. It is a benchmark sensitive to quality, weights and what is tradable.

Derive the relative-price prediction

Let the exchange rate be home currency per foreign unit. When home prices rise faster than foreign prices, more home units would be needed for the foreign currency if relative purchasing power stayed constant. Multiply the initial rate by the home price-growth factor and divide by the foreign one.

This relative version asks whether the price relationship stays stable from an initial level. It does not require baskets to have exactly equal prices at the starting exchange rate. Over long intervals multiplication of successive growth factors matters, especially when inflation is high.

Measure the real exchange rate

One convention defines the real exchange rate as the nominal rate multiplied by the foreign basket price and divided by the domestic basket price. A value above one under this convention means the foreign basket costs more after conversion. State your definition because inverted ratios reverse the sign of change.

Consumer-price indices measure changes over time and may use different baskets. Their arbitrarily chosen base values cannot generally be treated as comparable price levels for absolute PPP. They are useful for relative changes when their definitions are understood.

Method

Use prices for the appropriate question

  1. Levels

    Check that the priced baskets really are comparable before calculating home cost divided by foreign cost.

  2. Growth

    Convert each inflation rate into a factor. Apply home factor divided by foreign factor to the initial home-per-foreign quote.

  3. Interpretation

    Compare the implied and observed rates. Identify a tradability, data or timing reason a gap could persist.

Worked case

Two baskets under different inflation rates

A comparable basket costs 240 local units at home and 60 euros abroad. The initial market rate is 4 local units per euro. Next year the local basket rises by 12% and the euro basket by 5%. The observed rate becomes 4.10.

Working

  1. New costs are 268.8 local units and €63. The PPP rate is 268.8/63 = 4.2667 local units per euro.
  2. Using inflation factors gives the same result, 4 × 1.12/1.05. The local currency depreciated less in the market than the PPP benchmark suggests.
  3. At 4.10 the €63 basket costs 258.3 local units, against 268.8 at home. The foreign basket is about 3.91% cheaper measured against the home basket's cost.

Interpretation

Food transport, housing costs and different sales taxes can affect whether anyone can capture the measured difference.

A common mistake

Interpreting an observed deviation as a dated forecast that the currency must correct next month.

How to correct it

Identify the particular basket and the mechanism that could close the gap. Purchasing-power comparisons alone do not establish a trading horizon.

Quick check
If only the dollar basket rises from $100 to $105, what is the new absolute PPP rate?

The unchanged €80 basket would need to buy $105. Divide 105 by 80 to get $1.3125 per euro.

Written practice · 7 marks

Independent exam problem

Initially the rate is 3 home units per dollar. Home prices rise 8% and dollar prices 2% over one year. Assume relative PPP for the first calculation.

  1. Part A · 2 marks

    Find the implied end rate.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    If the rate stays at 3, compute the change in the real exchange rate defined as nominal rate times foreign prices divided by home prices.

  3. Part C · 2 marks

    Could CPI indices both rebased to 100 establish initial absolute parity?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 2 marks

3 × 1.08/1.02 = 3.1765 home units per dollar. A dollar buys more home currency under this assumption.

Part B 3 marks

The real-rate factor is (3/3) × 1.02/1.08 = 0.94444, a fall of about 5.56%. The foreign basket becomes cheaper relative to the home basket.

Part C 2 marks

No. The common index label is a normalization. Actual comparable basket prices, or reliable price-level data, are needed to assess initial absolute parity.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why can a more productive exporting country have systematically more expensive haircuts even when traded-goods prices are aligned?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would look at sector productivity. If a factory's productivity rises, tradable-goods competition can sustain a higher wage. Domestic salons need to compete for workers, and with unchanged haircuts per hour their service price rises. That can keep a national basket expensive without an arbitrage. I would compare matched tradable prices, nontradable weights, sector wages and output per worker before attributing a basket gap to exchange-rate mispricing.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What if service productivity doubles at the same time as tradable productivity?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The two productivity terms can rise proportionately. With traded price fixed, wages would rise but service output per worker would also rise, leaving the simplified relative service price unchanged. A persistent basket gap would then need another explanation.
