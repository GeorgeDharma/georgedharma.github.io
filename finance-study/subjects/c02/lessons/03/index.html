Covered interest parity

Compare investing at home with converting, investing abroad and locking in the future conversion. Equal riskless payoffs imply a forward rate under the model assumptions.

The starting point

An investor can save at home, or exchange money now, save abroad and agree today on the rate for converting the proceeds back. That last agreement is a forward contract. Once the future conversion is locked, these two routes can be compared in the same currency.

Covered interest parity equates the two final amounts when an investor can borrow and lend on comparable terms, faces the same credit risk and can transact without relevant costs. For a quote in home currency per foreign unit, forward = spot × (1 + home rate)/(1 + foreign rate) for one period.

Theory and method

Covered interest parity

CIP · Money-market replication · Forward arbitrage bounds

How do a bank's borrowing and lending spreads turn a single CIP rate into a no-arbitrage interval?

One future foreign unit can be created by buying and depositing enough foreign currency today, financed by borrowing at home. The opposite replication borrows foreign funds, sells them today and invests the home proceeds. The two directions use different dealer sides and funding rates. A forward price lying between the executable synthetic sale and purchase prices may differ from a formula using midpoint rates without leaving any locked profit a trader can collect.

How the result is built

Synthetic offer = aₛ(1+iᴴᵇ)/(1+iᶠˡ) ; synthetic bid = bₛ(1+iᴴˡ)/(1+iᶠᵇ)

aₛ and bₛ are spot offer and bid in home currency per foreign unit. i is the interest rate over the contract term. H and F mean home and foreign. Superscripts b and l mean borrowing and lending. Values are home currency per future foreign unit.

  1. To buy one foreign unit forward synthetically, deposit its discounted foreign amount at the foreign lending rate and buy today's principal at the spot offer.
  2. Borrow the home purchase cost at the home borrowing rate. The maturity repayment yields the synthetic offer. For a synthetic sale, borrow foreign, use the spot bid, and invest at the home lending rate.
  3. A market forward bid above the synthetic offer permits buying synthetically and selling forward. A forward offer below the synthetic bid permits the opposite matched trade.

When the reasoning holds

  • All rates apply to the same term, collateral, credit and settlement. Divergent maturities or balance-sheet charges alter the attainable bounds.
  • The direction uses rates available to that specific trader. A company cannot generally borrow at the interbank deposit rate.

Forward
A contract to exchange an agreed amount at a set price on a future date.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
First example

If spot is $1.10 per euro, a dollar deposit pays 5% and a euro deposit 2%, the one-year forward is 1.10 × 1.05/1.02 = $1.13235 per euro. Starting with $110 yields $115.50 directly or €102 converted at that forward.

Replicate a locked future conversion

A forward fixes today the exchange rate for a future currency delivery. A money-market strategy can reproduce that delivery by borrowing in one currency and investing in the other. If both strategies deliver exactly the same certain cash in every state, their costs must coincide under frictionless trading.

Take one foreign unit due next year. Buying only 1 divided by the foreign gross interest factor today will grow into that unit. Translate that cost at spot and borrow the home amount today. The home repayment due next year is the synthetic forward price for one foreign unit.

Derive the parity ratio from cash flows

Define S as today's home currency per foreign unit, F as its one-year forward and iH and iF as the respective one-year rates. The synthetic home repayment is S × (1 + iH)/(1 + iF). A higher home rate raises F with this quotation convention.

The argument compares two locked payoffs. It makes no prediction of the eventual spot rate. Distinguish the forward rate fixed in a contract from a forecast used to plan uncertain receipts. At maturity an existing contract can gain or lose value relative to spot.

Test a possible arbitrage

If an actual dealer will buy foreign currency forward above the synthetic acquisition cost, an investor could borrow home money, buy and invest foreign currency, then deliver it forward. If the dealer will sell foreign currency sufficiently cheaply, the reverse trade may work.

Real dealers quote spreads and borrowers may pay more than savers earn. Compare executable borrowing, lending, spot and forward terms on the correct sides. Differences in collateral, maturity or creditworthiness can explain a gap without any attainable risk-free profit.

Method

Price one future unit

  1. Size the deposit

    Discount the foreign amount using the foreign deposit rate. The deposit must mature on the day the forward settles.

  2. Fund it

    Buy that deposit at today's spot and borrow the required home funds. Compute the home repayment at maturity.

  3. Compare receipts

    Match that repayment against a firm forward sale of the foreign maturity amount. A positive residual after all costs is a candidate locked payoff.

Worked case

A supplier needs foreign funding in a year

Spot is 2 home units per foreign unit. The home rate is 6% and the foreign rate 4%. One-year borrowing and lending rates are identical in each currency. A dealer offers to buy 104 foreign units in one year at 2.08 home units each.

Working

  1. Deposit 100 foreign units to receive 104 next year. Buy the 100 foreign units today by borrowing 200 home units.
  2. At maturity owe 200 × 1.06 = 212 home units. The implied forward cost is 212/104 = 2.03846 per foreign unit.
  3. Deliver the 104 foreign units to the dealer for 216.32 home units and repay 212. The residual is 4.32 home units under these specified terms.

Interpretation

No future spot conversion is needed. If the dealer's 2.08 were its selling price only, this exact trade would lack the required forward buyer.

At the board

Build a forward rate by following two deposits

One euro buys US$1.10 today. Assume one-year simple rates of 2% in euros and 5% in dollars, the same borrowing and lending rates, and no costs. You need the forward price in dollars per euro.

Equalize the terminal dollar cash: F = 1.155 / 1.02 = 1.13235 dollars per euro. A forward quote of 1.16 produces US$1.1832 from the euro route. In the idealized market, borrow US$1.10, use it to buy and invest €1, sell €1.02 forward, and repay US$1.155. The difference is US$0.0282. Real executable bid/offer quotes, funding costs and collateral decide whether the trade survives.

A common mistake

Using the two deposit rates to claim profits that require borrowing at a cheaper, unavailable rate.

How to correct it

Match each rate to the action actually taken and each quote to the dealer side you can execute.

Quick check
If the home and foreign one-year rates are identical under these assumptions, how should forward compare with spot?

Both numerator and denominator have the same interest factor. They cancel, leaving the initial spot rate.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

Spot is 1.50 home units per foreign unit. The foreign one-year rate is 5%, home rate 2%. A firm will need 105 foreign units. Ignore spreads and credit risks.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Derive the parity forward price.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Show a home borrowing and foreign deposit hedge for the bill.

  3. Part C · 3 marks

    If a dealer sells 105 foreign forward for a total of 151 home, explain the reverse arbitrage.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

One year's forward equals 1.50 × 1.02/1.05 = 1.45714 per foreign unit. The higher foreign interest lets fewer foreign units be bought today.

Part B 2 marks

Borrow 150 home units, buy 100 foreign and invest to 105. Repay 153 home next year. This locks 153/105 per foreign unit.

Part C 3 marks

Borrow 100 foreign, sell them for 150 home and invest to 153. Buy 105 foreign forward for 151, repay the foreign loan and retain 2 home, assuming every leg is executable.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How do a bank's borrowing and lending spreads turn a single CIP rate into a no-arbitrage interval?

Compare with a spoken answer

I would calculate both executable replications. Buying future foreign currency uses home borrowing, the spot offer and foreign lending. Selling future foreign currency uses foreign borrowing, the spot bid and home lending. With the stated rates the resulting interval spans 0.99029 to 1.05030. I would compare an actual forward bid with the upper bound and an actual forward offer with the lower bound.

Interviewer’s follow-up

A dealer will buy one million foreign units forward at 1.055. What gross one-year residual does the synthetic purchase lock?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Using full precision the synthetic offer is 1.02 × 1.04/1.01 = 1.050297. Sell at 1.055 and buy synthetically. The difference times one million is about 4,703 home units before any additional costs.
