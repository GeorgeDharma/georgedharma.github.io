Repatriation and country risk

Cash at the subsidiary and cash available to the parent can differ. Model legal or tax restrictions as explicit case assumptions and test their effect.

The starting point

A subsidiary can earn cash that its parent cannot receive immediately. A distribution limit, a tax on transfers or a reserve the subsidiary must hold can change the amount or date of the parent’s receipt. Set out the case assumptions explicitly because rules differ by place and date.

Separate the business forecast from the schedule of remittances to the parent. Then convert and discount each cash flow on its own date. If a risk is already reflected in the amounts or scenarios, think carefully before charging for the same risk again in the discount rate.

Theory and method

Parent versus subsidiary project valuation

Remittable cash flow · Blocked funds · Withholding tax

A foreign subsidiary says it can distribute its accounting profit next year. What would you model before putting that amount into the parent's valuation?

A foreign business can record earnings while working capital, maintenance spending and debt terms absorb its cash. Even distributable cash may need to remain in a ring-fenced account or face a transfer charge. A parent expecting euro receipts needs a timetable of money it can lawfully receive, the currency available at that date and the value of any retained funds still owned. Following one unit through that timetable prevents both premature recognition and double counting.

How the result is built

Parent PV = Σₜ [Dₜ(1−τₜ)Sₜ]/(1+kᴴ)ᵗ in the specified deterministic case

Dₜ is gross foreign cash actually distributable at date t, τₜ the applicable transfer tax fraction, Sₜ home currency per foreign unit on that date, kᴴ the appropriate home discount rate. PV is today's value of all home receipts.

  1. Start from cash available after local obligations. A rule allowing only 60 of 100 to leave now limits the first distribution before tax.
  2. Apply each date's tax and conversion. If 60 is taxed 10% now at 2 home per foreign, it produces 108 home today.
  3. Track the retained 40 to its release. If it grows 5% to 42 and is taxed 10% at a year-one rate of 1.8, it produces 68.04 home next year before discounting.

When the reasoning holds

  • Transfer limits and rates here are fictional contract assumptions. Real cases require entity-specific evidence and precise taxes on each stage.
  • An uncertain exchange rate or political restriction can covary with operating cash. Evaluate joint scenarios and avoid imposing the full same loss again through a discount spread.
Terms in this lesson
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Discount rate
Rate used to express a future payment in today’s money, reflecting time and the relevant risk.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
First example

Suppose a subsidiary earns 100 local units now. The case permits 60 to be distributed now and 40 a year later. Assume no taxes, interest on retained cash or exchange-rate change and a 10% discount rate. Parent value today is 60 + 40/1.10 = 96.36 units.

Value the cash the parent can actually use

A subsidiary can earn a profit, generate operating cash and still be unable to distribute it immediately. Local lenders may require a minimum balance. A shareholder agreement may allocate dividends differently from accounting ownership. Transfers can face taxes or temporary restrictions under the assumptions of a case.

Build a remittance schedule beside the operating forecast. Show local cash left available after necessary reinvestment, the amount allowed for distribution, any withholding charge, and eventual release of retained funds. Taxes and limits in a practice problem are case assumptions. Actual arrangements vary.

Trace the fate of retained funds

Cash temporarily trapped in a subsidiary still belongs to someone. Its value depends on permitted local uses, earnings on reinvestment, future conversion rates and the date it becomes accessible. A permanent haircut has a different economic effect from a one-year delay.

Model each constraint on the correct base. A 10% withholding charge on the distributed amount differs from a 10% charge on gross local profit. If a distribution limit applies before tax, calculate the eligible gross amount first and then deduct the tax.

Represent uncertainty explicitly

Country exposure includes possible convertibility restrictions, disruption of operations and payment delays. These mechanisms affect different lines of the valuation. A scenario can lower local cash, cap distributions or change the translation rate. Specify which mechanism is assumed and whom it affects.

When probability-weighting cash flows, keep the treatment of the remaining risks consistent with the discount rate. An analyst who applies the full default haircut in expected cash and then a second identical charge in the discount rate may count the same expected loss twice.

Method

Build a parent cash waterfall

  1. Local capacity

    Calculate the cash available after operating and local financing needs, then apply the permitted distribution fraction.

  2. Transfer

    Deduct the stated charges on their proper base and convert the actual amount on its remittance date using the stated exchange assumption.

  3. Delay

    Carry retained balances forward with any permitted return, apply the later tax or release rule and discount each home receipt on its own date.

Worked case

A distribution cap expires in year two

A wholly owned subsidiary has 120 local units available now. It may distribute half now and the other half in one year. A 10% withholding charge applies to every distribution. Retained cash earns 5% locally. The rate is fixed at 2 local per home and the home discount rate is 8%.

Working

  1. The first gross distribution is 60 local. Tax leaves 54, which converts to 27 home immediately.
  2. The retained 60 grows to 63. After tax, 56.7 local is remitted next year. At the fixed rate that is 28.35 home.
  3. Parent value today equals 27 + 28.35/1.08 = 53.25 home. Immediate full distribution would deliver 120 × 0.90/2 = 54 home.

Interpretation

The delay costs 0.75 home in present value because the retained balance earns less than the parent discount rate under the fixed-currency assumptions. It has not been completely lost.

A common mistake

Writing off the entire blocked cash balance while also valuing a future dividend that includes it.

How to correct it

Roll opening retained cash to closing cash. Count each unit once when it becomes available or include its separately estimated residual value.

Quick check
If the 40 must instead wait two years under the same assumptions, what is its present value?

Discounting the delayed part for two periods gives 40/(1.10 × 1.10) = 33.06. The immediate part remains 60.

Written practice · 8 marks

Independent exam problem

A subsidiary has 80 local units now, may remit 30 now and the rest in one year. No withholding tax applies. Retained cash earns 4%. Exchange rates are 4 local per home now and 5 next year. Discount home receipts at 10%.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Calculate both home receipts.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Calculate parent value today and compare it with a permissible immediate full transfer.

  3. Part C · 2 marks

    Which two assumptions drive the difference?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Now receive 30/4 = 7.5 home. The retained 50 becomes 52 local before release, worth 52/5 = 10.4 home in one year.

Part B 3 marks

Present value is 7.5 + 10.4/1.10 = 16.95 home. Immediate transfer of 80/4 = 20 would be worth about 3.05 more.

Part C 2 marks

Timing means later cash is discounted, and the local currency buys fewer home units next year. The 4% local return partly offsets those effects.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A foreign subsidiary says it can distribute its accounting profit next year. What would you model before putting that amount into the parent's valuation?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would reconcile profit to local cash, then show local debt service, required reserves, legal dividend capacity, parent ownership, withholding and remittance dates. In the example only 108 home reaches the parent immediately. The retained cash contributes another 63 in present value, assuming its stated return and release. I would model an enforceable delay and a permanent loss separately because they have different values and risks.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If the 42 foreign release faces a 50% probability of a permanent block but could still be sold locally, should you simply halve its value?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Only if the blocked branch has zero recoverable parent value under a consistent risk treatment. Model the sale proceeds after costs, access and timing in that branch. Weight the resulting branch values using the case's pricing assumptions.
