Risk-neutral probability

Replication can be rewritten as a discounted expected payoff under pricing probabilities. These probabilities depend on traded prices and the financing rate.

The starting point

Suppose a market offers a share and a safe one-period investment. In a two-state model, a mathematical set of weights makes the share’s weighted future value grow at the safe rate. Those weights are called risk-neutral probabilities. They offer a compact way to express a replication price.

With up and down factors u and d and a risk-free growth factor R between them, the up weight is (R − d)/(u − d). To price a replicable payoff, calculate its weighted average using these probabilities, then divide by R. This procedure requires no estimate of an investor’s personal forecast of the two states.

Theory and method

Fundamental theorem of asset pricing and the martingale measure

Discounted-price martingale · Backward conditional expectation

How do you discount a two-step option when the second-period safe interest rate will depend on the first-period outcome?

A cash account earns the rate available at each node. After a high-rate first outcome, a euro due one step later is worth less at that intermediate date than after a low-rate outcome. Risk-neutral probabilities expressed under the money-market numeraire allow discounted traded values to satisfy a martingale condition. The pricing calculation must respect the relationship between each branch’s cash flow and its own funding path. One unconditional average rate generally discards that relationship.

How the result is built

Vₜ/Bₜ = Eᵠ[Vₜ₊₁/Bₜ₊₁ | ℱₜ]

Vₜ is the tradable claim value at date t, Bₜ the accrued money-market account at that date, Q the money-market pricing measure, ℱₜ current information, and Eᵠ conditional expectation. The formula shown assumes no intervening distributions during the step.

  1. Use half pricing weight at each branch. The up-node final payoffs €20 and €10 average €15. Its local safe growth is 1.10, so the intermediate claim is €13.6364.
  2. At the down node final payoffs €10 and zero average €5. Its safe growth is 1.00, so its intermediate price is €5.
  3. First-period safe growth is 1.02. Root price is (0.5 × 13.6364 + 0.5 × 5)/1.02 ≈ €9.1355. Pathwise discounting yields the same answer.

When the reasoning holds

  • The measure and numeraire are a consistent pair. Switching to a forward measure changes probabilities as well as the discounting representation.
  • In a finite complete arbitrage-free market the pricing measure is unique. With unspanned risks several admissible measures may price traded claims while disagreeing about a new unreplicable payoff.

Terms in this lesson
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Risk premium
Extra expected return for bearing risk, or the amount a risk-averse person would give up to replace a gamble with certainty, depending on context.
Option
A contract giving the buyer a right to buy or sell on stated terms. The buyer pays a premium for the right.
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
How to read the notation
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.

All notation examples

First example

A €100 share can become €120 or €80, while €100 safely becomes €104. The up weight is (1.04 − 0.80)/(1.20 − 0.80) = 0.60. A claim paying €10 only in the up state costs 0.60 × €10/1.04 ≈ €5.77.

Derive the pricing weight from the share

Let today’s share value be S and next-period outcomes Su and Sd. Choose q so discounting the weighted future share returns S today. The condition q × u + (1 − q) × d = R gives q = (R − d)/(u − d). When d < R < u the weight lies strictly between zero and one.

This q prices every payoff replicable using the same share and safe asset under the model. It is an alternative notation for the two state prices q/R and (1 − q)/R. Pricing is therefore consistent across different securities that respond to identical modeled future states.

Physical probability and priced probability

An economist estimating how often shares rise provides a physical probability. A trader inferring q from observed prices provides the weight that values future cash consistently. The investor may demand a risk premium for holding shares, so the two weights can differ while today’s prices remain internally consistent.

A real expected payoff computed with physical probabilities cannot generally be discounted at the safe rate for a risky claim. Doing so assumes away its priced risk. Conversely using q to forecast default frequency or to plan average sales can misstate actual expected outcomes.

Extension to exercise decisions

For a European option, backward induction at each node gives discounted risk-neutral next values. For an American-style option, compare that continuation value with current exercise value whenever exercise is permitted. Choose the larger because the holder decides. This can matter for puts or dividend-paying shares.

Check the time unit in R. A 6% annual effective rate implies square-root growth over half a year, whereas a quoted simple six-month convention uses its own expression. Incorrect period funding changes both q and discounting.

Method

Choose the correct probability

  1. No-arbitrage

    Check down, risk-free and up growth factors. If the safe asset dominates the share in every state or conversely, the assumed unrestricted model may already contain an arbitrage.

  2. Price

    Use implied weights and discount exactly once per step. Where the contract permits early exercise, compare intrinsic and continuation value.

  3. Forecast

    If the question asks for an actual frequency or physical expectation, supply physical data independently and explain any gap from pricing weights.

Worked case

A digital claim and an exercise choice

A €100 share becomes €130 or €90 in one year. Safe growth is 1.02. A digital contract pays €20 only in the down outcome. A forecaster assigns 50% physical probability to the down outcome.

Working

  1. The pricing up weight is (1.02 − 0.90)/(1.30 − 0.90) = 0.30, so down weight is 0.70. Digital value is 0.70 × €20/1.02 ≈ €13.73.
  2. The forecaster’s expected terminal cash is €10. Discounting it at the safe rate would give €9.80, which would fail to match replication under the stated market prices.
  3. Consider also a put struck at €140 that can be exercised now or in one year. Future payoffs €10 and €50 give continuation (0.30 × €10 + 0.70 × €50)/1.02 ≈ €37.25. Immediate exercise pays €40, so it is optimal here.

Interpretation

The exercise comparison is for this specified contract and tree. It depends on the ability to exercise now, which an otherwise identical European put lacks.

A common mistake

Using 50% because the tree contains two branches.

How to correct it

The number of branches says nothing about their economic or pricing probabilities. Solve q from the underlying and safe growth or use explicitly supplied physical probabilities for forecasting.

Test the assumptions in the interactive model

Quick check
Under these same prices, what is the model value of a claim paying €10 only in the down state?

The down weight is 0.40. Its weighted payoff of €4 is due next period, giving a price today of €4/1.04 ≈ €3.85.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

At one node u = 1.20, d = 0.85 and R = 1.025. A claim pays €8 up and €2 down.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Compute q and value.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    If physical up probability is 70%, what is physical expected payoff, and why is it different?

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

q = (1.025 − 0.85)/(1.20 − 0.85) = 0.5. Value is (0.5 × €8 + 0.5 × €2)/1.025 ≈ €4.878.

Part B 2 marks

Expected payoff is 0.7 × €8 + 0.3 × €2 = €6.20. Physical frequency weights forecast cash. The risk-neutral weights also reflect how different states are priced.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How do you discount a two-step option when the second-period safe interest rate will depend on the first-period outcome?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would work backward with conditional values. At the up node the €15 average receives a 1.10 discount, whereas the down-node €5 average receives a 1.00 discount. Then I apply the common first-period growth to their weighted prices. That gives about €9.1355. The same procedure follows from discounting by each path’s accrued money-market account under its matching pricing measure.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If both second-step rates instead have gross growth 1.05, can you discount the overall average in one expression?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Yes. Both cumulative discount factors are then 1/(1.02 × 1.05), and the four equally weighted payoffs average €10. The resulting price is about €9.3371. This simplification works because the growth factor is the same across paths.
