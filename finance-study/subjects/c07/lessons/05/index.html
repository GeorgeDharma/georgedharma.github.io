Options and bounds

Calls give the right to buy and puts the right to sell. Use simple bounds and parity relationships to check European option prices.

The starting point

A European call gives its buyer the right to buy an asset at the strike price on the expiry date. A European put gives the right to sell on that date. With matching strike and expiry on a non-dividend-paying share, a call plus cash set aside to pay the strike can be compared with a share plus a put.

Both packages are worth the greater of the share price and the strike at expiry. Under consistent borrowing and lending terms and frictionless trading, equal terminal payments require equal prices today. This put–call parity also helps test whether a set of quoted prices can fit together.

Theory and method

Convexity in strike and butterfly arbitrage

Static no-arbitrage bounds · Breeden–Litzenberger state pricing

Listed European calls decrease with strike. Is that observation alone sufficient to rule out a static arbitrage across strikes?

Two calls on adjacent intervals can be combined to create a butterfly that never loses at expiry before its purchase cost. At widely separated final prices all the legs offset or expire. In the middle the position pays a triangular positive amount. Consistent option prices must assign this nonnegative payoff a nonnegative cost. This convexity requirement tests a different restriction from simple monotonicity and links strike curvature to the market prices of local future states.

How the result is built

B = C(K − h) − 2C(K) + C(K + h) ≥ 0

B is the euro cost of a long symmetric butterfly, C(k) the current price of a European call with strike k, K the center strike and h the equal spacing in euros. All calls have the same expiry and underlying unit.

  1. Buy a call at €90, write two at €100, and buy one at €110. Below €90 all expire. At €100 only the low call pays €10. Above €110 the net terminal payoff is zero.
  2. Suppose the three premiums are €15, €9 and €2. All decrease with strike, but butterfly cost is 15 − 18 + 2 = −€1. Buying the structure actually brings in one euro today.
  3. The trader keeps the initial receipt and has a nonnegative terminal payoff in every state under these assumptions. For dense strikes, scaled second differences relate to discounted risk-neutral state density under smoothness.

When the reasoning holds

  • The strike distances, exercise rights, underlying, unit size and expiry are identical as specified. Unequal spacing requires reweighting the legs.
  • Negative cost must be executable after buying at asks, selling at bids, and allowing for collateral and financing. A midmarket violation may disappear.
Terms in this lesson
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
Option
A contract giving the buyer a right to buy or sell on stated terms. The buyer pays a premium for the right.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
No-arbitrage
A pricing condition that rules out a feasible trade producing a certain gain for no net investment and with no risk of loss.
First example

A share costs €50, a call costs €6 and the present value of the €50 strike is €48. Parity gives call + discounted strike = share + put. The put is €6 + €48 − €50 = €4.

Establish floors from attainable cash flows

A European call’s value cannot be negative and normally cannot exceed the current value of a non-dividend-paying share under ordinary financing assumptions. A lower bound is the greater of zero and current share price minus the present value of the strike. For the corresponding put, the lower bound is the greater of zero and discounted strike minus share price.

To understand a bound, compare terminal payoffs of the option with a traded portfolio that it dominates. If a portfolio can never pay more than the option, the portfolio cannot cost more under frictionless no-arbitrage. This logic avoids treating a pricing formula as a rule to memorize.

Prove parity by matching every state

One package buys a call and saves the discounted strike. The other buys one share and one put. If the final share price exceeds the strike, both deliver the share’s value. Below the strike, both deliver the strike. Thus call + PV(strike) = put + share for the matched European contracts without intervening dividends.

If dividends are known, a holder of the share receives cash before expiry while the option package does not. Adjust the initial share side for the prepaid cost of delivery, typically spot minus present known dividends. American early exercise can break the exact European identity.

Turn a mispricing into a portfolio

Identify which whole package is overpriced. Sell that package and buy the cheaper one. Selling a package containing a cash deposit means borrowing its present amount and repaying the strike at expiry. Track every initial receipt and terminal obligation. A floating finance convention or short-sale prohibition can prevent the textbook trade.

Check that all contracts use identical underlying, strike, expiry, exercise rights and settlement. A contract covering 100 shares and another covering one share need unit conversion. Bid–ask bounds may erase apparent midquote discrepancies.

Method

Check an options screen

  1. Bounds

    Compare option quotes with nonnegativity, the attainable underlying cost, and the exercise-price present-value floor.

  2. Parity

    Calculate each complete package at executable prices. Choose the cheap portfolio to buy and the expensive one to sell.

  3. Verify

    Write the terminal cash for spot below and above strike and show any retained initial cash after transaction and financing costs.

Worked case

A parity discrepancy with explicit financing

A share costs €49, a European call costs €7, the matching put costs €5 and the strike’s present value is €48. The strike due at expiry is €50. There are no dividends or trading restrictions, and all quotes can be executed.

Working

  1. Call plus present strike costs €55. Share plus put costs €54. Short the call for €7 and borrow €48. Buy the share for €49 and put for €5. This leaves €1 today.
  2. If spot ends above €50, deliver the share on the short call, receive €50 and repay the borrowing. The put expires. Net terminal option-and-financing cash is zero.
  3. If spot ends below €50, exercise the put to sell the share for €50 and repay the borrowing. The call expires. Terminal cash is again zero, leaving the initial euro.

Interpretation

Checking both regions exposes sign errors. If bid–ask and financing frictions absorb at least €1, the apparent opportunity may be untradeable.

A common mistake

Comparing a put price directly with a call price and demanding they be equal at the same strike.

How to correct it

Parity compares packages incorporating share ownership and the discounted strike. Calls and puts alone have different payoffs.

Quick check
With the same share and discounted strike, a put now costs €5. What call price satisfies parity?

Rearranging gives call = €50 + €5 − €48 = €7. Buying a share and a put costs the same €55 as a call plus the discounted strike.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A non-dividend-paying share costs €82, present strike is €76, and a European put costs €3. The call is quoted at €8.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find parity-implied call value and identify the cheaper package.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Identify the counterpart trades under unrestricted short selling.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Call should cost €82 + €3 − €76 = €9. Call plus present strike costs €84 whereas share plus put costs €85. The call package is cheaper by €1.

Part B 2 marks

Buy the call and invest €76. Short the share and write the put, receiving €85 for the expensive package. Terminal packages offset at matching expiry and strike. Trading and borrowing frictions must be checked in practice.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Listed European calls decrease with strike. Is that observation alone sufficient to rule out a static arbitrage across strikes?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would test both ordering and convexity. A long call butterfly at equally spaced strikes has a nonnegative terminal payoff. The quotes €15, €9 and €2 let me collect €1 initially while owning it, which is an actionable contradiction if bid and ask quotes and collateral terms permit the trade. A negative second difference also conflicts with a nonnegative implied state price for that range.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What price ceiling must the €100 call satisfy if the €90 and €110 quotes stay at €15 and €2?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Convexity requires 15 − 2C(100) + 2 ≥ 0, so the middle call can cost at most €8.50. This checks one three-strike restriction. Other strike, time and put–call relationships throughout the quoted surface still need independent tests.
