Futures and margin

Daily marking to market can require cash before a position ultimately pays off. Compare this liquidity need with the timing of a forward settlement.

The starting point

A futures exchange settles gains and losses frequently, commonly each trading day. The money held against potential losses is margin. An adverse move can require fresh cash promptly, even if the futures position is an effective hedge for a sale or purchase that occurs later.

A forward can instead make a single payment at settlement, subject to its own collateral agreement. For futures, multiply the price change by contract size and direction. A buyer gains when the quoted price rises. A seller gains when it falls. Trace both the final result and the largest interim cash need.

Theory and method

Futures–forward convexity adjustment

Daily settlement and funding covariance · Stochastic interest rates

Why can a daily-settled futures quote and a single-settlement forward quote differ even on the same deliverable and date?

An investor long a futures contract receives cash along the way when the contract appreciates and must finance losses as they occur. Whether those favorable or unfavorable cash flows can be reinvested or borrowed at high or low rates matters. A forward delivers its net difference at the final date. In a simple pricing representation, stochastic discount factors inside the common risk-neutral expectation can distinguish the two quoted prices even under the same modeled terminal asset distribution.

How the result is built

Ffut = Eᵠ[S]; Ffwd = Eᵠ[DS]/Eᵠ[D]

Ffut and Ffwd are euro futures and delivery-forward prices in the simplified continuously settled setup. S is terminal euro asset value, D the realized money-market discount factor along that path, and Eᵠ the expectation under the money-market risk-neutral measure.

  1. A new forward has zero value, so Eᵠ[D(S − Ffwd)] = 0. Move the known delivery price outside the expectation and divide by Eᵠ[D].
  2. With equally weighted states, let S be €80 or €120 and D be 0.98 or 0.90, respectively. Expected S is €100, expected D is 0.94, and expected DS is €93.20.
  3. The forward quote is €93.20/0.94 ≈ €99.15 versus €100 futures. Price and discount factor are negatively related in this illustration, placing the futures quote above the forward.

When the reasoning holds

  • The futures representation assumes idealized marking and collateral remuneration consistent with the money-market numeraire. Different collateral terms, margin costs and discrete dates can modify it.
  • Risk-neutral states are pricing weights. Physical forecasts about realized asset prices or interest rates cannot simply be inserted without a risk-pricing model.
Terms in this lesson
Liquidity
Access to cash when payments fall due. For a security, it also describes how easily it can be traded at a reasonable price.
Settlement
The final transfer of cash or securities needed to complete a transaction.
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
Collateral
Cash or assets pledged to protect the other party if an obligation is missed.
Margin
In business accounts, profit expressed relative to sales. In derivatives trading, margin is collateral required to support a position.
First example

A grain producer sells a futures contract covering 100 tonnes at €200 per tonne. Tomorrow’s price is €207. The short position loses 100 × €7 = €700 for the day. The grain the producer expects to sell later supplies no cash today.

Daily settlement moves funding through time

A futures contract usually resets accumulated gains and losses through variation margin. Multiply the settlement price change by units per contract and number of contracts, reversing the result for a short position. A long contract gains on an increase even if its price remains below the level originally agreed.

Initial margin is collateral supporting the contract, not the purchase price of the underlying. Under a simplified brokerage rule, falling below maintenance margin triggers a cash call restoring the account to a specified target. Actual clearing and broker rules can differ, so use the terms given.

Separate cumulative gain from peak cash need

Summing daily gains over a price path telescopes to the final minus initial price times quantity when contract size is fixed. This identity says little about the cash needed at the lowest point. A hedger who cannot meet an intermediate call may have the position forcibly closed before the planned hedge horizon.

Cash already posted earns or costs funding according to its terms. When financing rates vary with market moves, daily-settled futures and single-settlement forwards can have different economic values. Under a constant rate and simplifying assumptions their relationship is close or identical in standard models.

Plan for liquidity on both legs

An airline buying fuel may gain on a long futures hedge when fuel prices rise. Conversely, falling prices create futures losses while the airline enjoys lower costs only when it later purchases fuel. Directional economic protection does not align the dates of the two cash streams.

Model severe price paths, daily settlement cutoffs, collateral eligibility and access to a credit facility on the required days. A hoped-for receipt on the invoice due date may be worthless for a margin call due tomorrow.

Method

Set up a margin ledger

  1. Size

    Convert the quoted change to money using units per contract, number of contracts and long or short direction.

  2. Settle

    Start from previous collateral, add that day’s variation and test the maintenance threshold under the rule specified.

  3. Fund

    Record additional deposits or withdrawals separately from trading profit. Compute peak external funding and reconcile final equity with total contributions.

Worked case

A profitable final hedge with an interim call

A trader is long two contracts of 50 units each. The opening futures price is €100, followed by settlements at €96, €91 and €101. Total initial margin is €1,500, maintenance is €1,000, and a breach requires restoration to €1,500. No interest accrues or cash is withdrawn.

Working

  1. Day one variation is 100 × (96 − 100) = −€400, leaving €1,100, above maintenance. Day two loses €500, leaving €600. The trader must provide €900.
  2. After the top-up the account starts day three at €1,500. A €10 rise credits €1,000, leaving €2,500 at close. Total outside contributions are €1,500 + €900 = €2,400.
  3. After returning the collateral account, net trading gain is €100, equal to 100 units times the €1 increase from opening to final. At one point €2,400 had to be provided despite this positive final outcome.

Interpretation

Only the €900 top-up is new cash after initial collateral. Adding withdrawals or financing expense changes the cash-flow report but not the daily price-change identity.

A common mistake

Calling the €900 top-up a second trading loss after recording day-two variation.

How to correct it

Variation changes wealth. A margin deposit moves the trader’s cash into collateral. Reconcile ending collateral against all transfers to measure profit.

Quick check
If the futures price subsequently falls from €207 to €201, what is that day’s settlement for the producer?

The producer is short, so a €6 decline generates €6 × 100 = €600 that day. Across both days the net settlement is still a €100 payment.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A short futures position covers 400 units. Price goes from €25 to €28 then to €24. The margin account opens with €2,000, maintenance is €1,000, and a breach restores €2,000.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Compute first-day call and second-day variation.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Reconcile ending balance and cumulative profit.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

The short loses 400 × €3 = €1,200, leaving €800, so the call is €1,200. On the second day the €4 decline gains €1,600.

Part B 2 marks

After restoration and the gain the balance is €3,600. Contributions total €3,200, so net gain is €400, matching 400 × (€25 − €24).

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why can a daily-settled futures quote and a single-settlement forward quote differ even on the same deliverable and date?

Compare with a spoken answer

I would explain the cash timing first. The futures holder realizes gains and losses while funding rates are moving, while a forward settles later. In this model €120 occurs on the low-discount-factor path, so weighting terminal values by discount factors gives a €99.15 delivery price rather than the €100 futures quote. With deterministic interest and otherwise matched conventions the model’s distinction vanishes.

Interviewer’s follow-up

What happens if each state instead has the same discount factor of 0.94?

Compare with the follow-up answer

Expected discounted spot becomes 0.94 × €100 = €94. Dividing by 0.94 gives €100, equal to the futures expectation. A real price difference could still reflect delivery, collateral, funding frictions or exchange-specific features outside this calculation.
