Option strategies, premiums and expiration profit

Build expiration profits for a covered call, protective put, bull call spread, bear put spread and long straddle.

Theory and terminology

Multi-leg option payoffs and break-even prices

covered call · protective put · bull call spread · bear put spread · long straddle

What you should be able to explain

  • Build expiration profits for a covered call, protective put, bull call spread, bear put spread and long straddle.
  • Identify the market view, breakevens and downside exposure that distinguish those positions.

Give every leg a signed cost

One call pays the amount by which the final stock price exceeds its strike, with a minimum payoff of zero. One put pays the amount by which strike exceeds final price, also floored at zero. A short position has the opposite payoff and initially receives the premium. A stock position costs today’s share price and finishes worth the final price.

Build profit by subtracting the initial net investment from final position value on the same date basis. Assume European exercise, a nonnegative stock price, a common expiry, zero interest, no dividends and no fees. The examples report currency per underlying share. A contract with a 100-share multiplier scales all option premiums and payoffs by 100 and calls for matching stock quantities.

Decide whether stock ownership is part of the objective

A covered call holds the share and sells a call against it. The investor collects premium and accepts a ceiling on gains above the strike. When the stock collapses, the premium provides only a small cushion. This suits someone willing to part with the share at the strike under the stated terms.

A protective put buys a put alongside the share. The put creates a gross sale floor while its premium raises the portfolio’s starting cost. The investor keeps upside participation. Compare its maximum economic loss with the cost of the original shares plus insurance, particularly if the shares were acquired earlier at a different price.

Choose a direction or the size of a move

A bull call spread buys a lower-strike call and sells a higher-strike call. The premium received reduces the cash risk and also limits the gain. Its bearish counterpart can buy a higher-strike put and sell a lower-strike put. Both are vertical spreads when strike is the only contract term that changes.

A long straddle buys a call and a put at the same strike. Both premiums are spent. At expiration, either direction can produce profit if the move is large enough to recover their combined cost. Before expiration, remaining time and implied volatility affect option values, so an expiry breakeven alone does not give the price at which a position can be closed today.

Method

Build the result

Profit = nₛ(S − S₀) + Σ nⱼ[max(wⱼ(S − Kⱼ), 0) − pⱼ].

S is the stock price at expiration and S₀ its initial price. nₛ is the signed number of shares. For each option leg j, nⱼ is its signed quantity in one-share units, Kⱼ the strike, pⱼ its positive quoted premium and wⱼ is +1 for a call or −1 for a put. A negative quantity means the leg is sold. Profit is currency per chosen position under the zero-carry assumption.

  1. List quantities and initial cash flows first. Sort strikes, then partition the final stock price into regions separated by those strikes.
  2. Inside each region substitute the intrinsic value of each leg, keeping the signs of short positions. Subtract net premiums paid and any initial stock cost.
  3. Set each regional profit equal to zero and retain solutions inside their corresponding region. Check the strike boundaries, zero stock price and the far upside for maximum gains or losses.
Worked case

One option chain, four contrasting positions

A share costs €100. The €100 call and €100 put each cost €6. The €110 call costs €2 and the €95 put costs €3. All contracts have the same expiry and the assumptions above apply. Compare one unit of each leg.

Working

  1. Covered call means buy the share and sell the €110 call. Initial debit is €98. Terminal value is the smaller of the share price and €110. Breakeven is €98, maximum gain €12 and maximum loss €98 if the share falls to zero.
  2. Protective put means buy the share and the €95 put for €103 total. Terminal value is at least €95. Maximum loss is €8, breakeven €103 and the gain is uncapped. At a final €80 the combined loss is still €8.
  3. Bull call spread means buy the €100 call and sell the €110 call for a €4 net debit. Gross payoff runs from zero to €10. Maximum loss is €4, maximum gain €6 and breakeven €104. A bearish alternative buys the €100 put and sells the €95 put for €3. Its breakeven is €97, with €3 maximum loss and €2 maximum gain.
  4. Long €100 straddle costs €12. Profit is the absolute distance of the final price from €100 minus €12. Breakevens are €88 and €112. It loses at most €12, gains without limit on the upside, and can earn at most €88 on the downside because the stock cannot fall below zero.

Decision and interpretation

At a final €110 a bullish call spread reaches its €6 maximum, while the straddle still loses €2. A correct directional forecast does not specify which structure earns the most after paying for its options.

Quick check
Using the €100 stock and worked quotes, the stock closes at €108. Which profit comparison is correct at expiration?

The call spread pays €8 gross less its €4 debit. The straddle also pays €8 gross but its two premiums cost €12.

Written practice

Independent exam problem

A share costs $50. The $50 call and put each cost $4, the $56 call costs $1.50, and the $46 put costs $2. All terms match the worked case. Compare profits at a final price of $57 for the covered $56 call, protective $46 put, $50/$56 bull call spread and $50 long straddle. Identify each position’s breakeven.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked solution
  1. The covered call costs $48.50 and ends worth $56, giving $7.50 profit and a $48.50 breakeven. The protective portfolio costs $52 and ends worth $57, giving $5 profit, a $52 breakeven and a maximum loss of $6.
  2. The bull spread costs $2.50 and pays its $6 width, leaving $3.50 profit. Its breakeven is $52.50 and its maximum loss is $2.50.
  3. The straddle costs $8 and pays $7. It loses $1 even though the share rose 14%. Its two breakevens are $42 and $58, and its maximum loss is $8 at a final $50.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Which option position fits an investor seeking protection, a limited-risk directional view or a large move in either direction, and what price change is enough to make money?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would use a protective put for someone keeping shares with a firm loss budget, a vertical for a bounded directional view, and a long straddle for a move large enough to justify both premiums. A covered call suits a holder willing to cap gains while retaining substantial downside. In this chain the bull spread breaks even at €104. The straddle requires €88 or €112 merely to recover its €12 cost.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Before expiration the stock stays at $50 but the implied volatility used in live option prices rises sharply. Must the straddle still show an $8 accounting loss?

Compare with the follow-up answer

No. The $8 loss is the terminal outcome at the strike. Before expiration both options can retain time value, and higher implied volatility typically raises the value of both long legs, other inputs equal. Revalue actual executable quotes and account for time already elapsed.
