Contract terms and payoff

Distinguish the cash paid today, future cash flows and profit after costs. Draw outcomes for the buyer and seller of the same contract.

The starting point

A derivative links payments to something else, such as a share price, an interest rate or a commodity price. Read the contract from one party’s point of view. Identify who can choose, what each party must do, when cash changes hands and whether there was an upfront payment.

Payoff describes the contract’s cash flow at the specified date. Profit also accounts for what the position cost. To compare different dates precisely, bring each cash flow to a common date using an appropriate rate. The two parties’ contract payoffs offset each other before fees or failures.

Theory and method

Vertical call spread and digital payoff approximation

Call spread slope · Cash-or-nothing option

How could two listed calls approximate a contract paying one euro when the share finishes above a particular price?

An options desk can buy a call at a lower strike and sell one at a higher strike. Below the lower strike both expire worthless. Above the higher strike the difference in exercise payoffs is fixed. Dividing the difference by the strike width produces a payment between zero and one across the narrow middle interval. As the strikes converge, the payoff approaches a cash digital except at the boundary. Quoted finite spreads still have a sloping transition region.

How the result is built

g(S) = [(S − K)⁺ − (S − K − h)⁺]/h

S is the terminal euro share price, K the lower euro strike, h the positive euro distance to the higher strike, x⁺ means max(x,0), and g is the normalized terminal euro payment per unit digital-style contract.

  1. For S below K, both calls expire with zero payoff. For K < S < K + h, only the lower strike pays and g rises by 1/h for each euro of spot increase.
  2. For S above K + h the calls differ by exactly h, giving a normalized payoff of one. With strikes €100 and €110, spot €106 gives 0.60 and €120 gives one.
  3. If calls cost €8 and €4, the normalized spread costs (€8 − €4)/€10 = €0.40. With discount factor 0.96 its risk-neutral expected ramp payoff is 0.4167.

When the reasoning holds

  • The options have identical expiry, underlying and European exercise terms. Early exercise or unequal multipliers can change the static relationship.
  • A finite-width spread prices an averaged payoff across a range. An exact probability at one strike requires a limiting relation and adequate market regularity.
Terms in this lesson
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
Derivative
A contract whose value depends on something else, such as a currency, an interest rate or a share price.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
Interest rate
The price of borrowing money or the return for lending it, quoted as a percentage over a stated period.
Settlement
The final transfer of cash or securities needed to complete a transaction.
First example

A buyer pays €3 for the right to buy a share for €50 in three months. If the share ends at €55, exercising produces a €5 payoff. Ignoring interest, the buyer’s profit is €2. The seller receives the premium and loses €5 on exercise, for a €2 loss.

Read the contract as a set of rights

Locate the underlying, the number of units, the price agreed in advance, the exercise or settlement date, and who can decide whether the exchange occurs. A forward obliges both sides to settle. A call lets its holder buy, and a put lets its holder sell, on the terms specified.

Physical delivery transfers the underlying and requires funding or inventory. Cash settlement uses a defined price observation to calculate money owed. Even two contracts on oil can settle differently if their prices reference a different grade, location or day.

Build a payoff one region at a time

A forward buyer’s terminal payoff per unit is final spot minus delivery price. A call buyer receives the greater of zero and spot minus strike. A put buyer receives the greater of zero and strike minus spot. Each seller has the negative of the corresponding buyer payoff before considering premium or default.

A premium paid upfront is a cash flow separate from the exercise payoff. To calculate profit at expiry, subtract the premium grown at the financing rate. Buying several legs produces a combined payoff by adding their signed, contract-sized amounts state by state.

Interpret slope and limits

The slope of a terminal payoff against spot price shows the additional cash received per unit price increase in that region. A protective put floors the gross terminal value of a share. Selling a call against the same share exchanges some upside for premium. A break-even is the usual point where total profit crosses zero.

The same strategy can have several break-even prices. Check every piece of a payoff graph and whether any mathematical solution actually falls in the region used to derive it. Be clear if profit includes the initial cost of the underlying.

Method

Map the position

  1. Inventory

    Record long or short direction and contract size. Place premiums, underlying purchases and settlement cash on dates before doing any arithmetic.

  2. Regions

    Split possible final prices at each strike and write the payoff in every interval. Combine legs and separately include funded initial cost.

  3. Check

    Evaluate one price below the lowest strike, one between strikes and one above. Confirm that the resulting slope and maximum loss match the contract rights.

Worked case

A collar on an existing supply share

An investor buys a €50 share, buys a European put struck at €45 for €2, and sells a European call struck at €60 for €1. All expire together, with zero interest and no dividends. Consider this complete investment including its initial cost.

Working

  1. Initial outlay is €51. Below €45 the share plus put yields €45 while the call expires. At €35 the investor receives total €45, for a €6 loss.
  2. Between €45 and €60 both options expire. At €55 the full position yields €55 and profit is €4. Break-even in this interval is €51.
  3. Above €60 the short call offsets every extra euro on the share. At €80 terminal value remains €60 and maximum profit is €9. The maximum loss remains €6 under the stated assumptions.

Interpretation

The floor and ceiling are gross terminal values of the complete position. Their distance from the funded net outlay determines profit.

A common mistake

Calling the put strike the portfolio’s maximum profit or loss.

How to correct it

First determine terminal wealth for the complete contract package. Subtract the amount invested, brought to the same date, to determine profit.

Quick check
If the share instead ends at €49, what is the buyer’s profit under the same assumptions?

The buyer can let the option expire. The exercise payoff is zero, but the €3 premium has already been paid.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

Buy a call with strike €40 for €6 and sell a call with strike €55 for €2, each on one share. The expiry is common and interest is zero.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find the net premium, break-even and maximum profit.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    At €35 and €52 respectively, give terminal profit and explain the differing slopes.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Net premium is €4. Between strikes, gross payoff is spot minus €40, giving break-even €44. At or above €55, payoff caps at €15 and net profit at €11.

Part B 2 marks

At €35 both expire and loss is €4. At €52 the long call pays €12 while the short is unexercised, so profit is €8. The spread gains one euro per price euro only between the strikes.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

How could two listed calls approximate a contract paying one euro when the share finishes above a particular price?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would buy the €100 call and sell the €110 call, dividing by the €10 width for a unit payout. The normalized position approaches a digital when the spread becomes narrow. Here it costs €0.40 and pays 0.60 at €106, so I would describe its price as a discounted expected ramp. I would also check bid–ask costs and actually tradable contract multiples.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If strikes can be only €10 apart, can 0.4167 be quoted as the exact probability of finishing above €100?

Compare with the follow-up answer

No. It is the pricing expectation of the normalized ramp. It lies between pricing probabilities for finishing above the upper and lower thresholds under consistent endpoint conventions. Finite-width state payoffs differ materially inside the interval.
