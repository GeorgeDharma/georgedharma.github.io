Basis risk

The hedging instrument and the exposure may refer to different assets, dates or locations. Their prices need not move together exactly.

The starting point

A hedge often uses a contract whose price follows the exposure imperfectly. A farmer may sell a grain variety in one region while the futures contract tracks a different location. Define the basis, here as local cash price minus futures price, and track whether it changes.

For a short futures hedge that is closed when the physical crop is sold, effective sale price equals the final local price plus the gain on futures. Rearranging gives initial futures price plus the final basis. Unexpected final basis is the remaining price uncertainty in this simple hedge.

Theory and method

Rolling a futures hedge and calendar basis

Stack-and-roll hedge · Basis risk

A producer plans to sell its crop after the nearest liquid futures contract expires. Which additional price spread will it have to accept when it rolls the hedge?

A producer initially sells the liquid near contract, buys it back before delivery, and sells a later contract covering the actual harvest. The hedge has two price changes, one for each short position, and one physical sale. Their sum reveals a final local basis risk and a calendar spread fixed only when the roll is executed. A high correlation between today’s local price and the near future does not by itself protect the price of that later roll.

How the result is built

Effective price = F₁₀ + (Sₜ − F₂ₜ) + (F₂ᵣ − F₁ᵣ)

F₁₀ is initial price of the first short future, F₁ᵣ and F₂ᵣ are near and later futures prices at roll date r, F₂ₜ is later future’s closing price at sale date t, and Sₜ is the physical local sale price. All are euros per identical quantity unit.

  1. Add the physical sale Sₜ to first futures gain F₁₀ − F₁ᵣ and second gain F₂ᵣ − F₂ₜ. Group terms by the local basis and the roll spread.
  2. Sell the near at €200. At roll, buy it for €195 and sell the later future at €202. At harvest sell the crop for €188 and close the later short at €190.
  3. Total effective price is 188 + (200 − 195) + (202 − 190) = €205. Equivalently start at 200, add final basis −2 and roll spread +7.

When the reasoning holds

  • Quantities, unit conversion and hedge ratios are matched one for one. A short crop can leave speculative futures exposure even if both contracts move as forecast.
  • This example ignores separate funding costs and bid–ask spreads at both legs of the roll. Variation margin still has to be funded on each contract.
Terms in this lesson
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
Correlation
A measure of how closely two series move together in a linear pattern.
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
Profit
Revenue left after the expenses recognized for the period.
How to read the notation
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.
A change · Δ
ΔS commonly means the new asset price less the old price. A move from €50 to €53 gives ΔS = €3. In options, delta can also name a sensitivity. A delta of 0.6 means a €1 rise in the underlying predicts about a €0.60 rise in the option, for a small move.
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.
Approximately equal · ≈
The two numbers match to the precision shown. €100 ÷ 3 ≈ €33.33. Keep more digits while calculating, then round the final answer to the precision requested.

All notation examples

First example

A producer sells futures at €200. At harvest the local crop sells for €184 and futures close at €190. The gain on futures is €10, giving an effective €194. The final basis is €184 − €190 = −€6, also giving €200 − €6 = €194.

Define basis and hedge direction together

A producer expecting to sell is exposed to falling local prices. Selling futures can offset part of the loss. A consumer expecting to buy is exposed to rising prices and can buy futures. Specify basis, for example local spot minus futures, at a stated location and closing date.

For a unit short hedge held through physical sale, effective sale price equals local final spot plus initial futures minus final futures, or initial futures plus final basis. A hedge can fail through unexpected changes in location spreads, quality, contract maturity or delivery timing.

Match the variability, not only the units

In a cross-hedge, the spot and futures price changes can have different typical sizes as well as imperfect correlation. For comparable price units, minimizing variance of ΔS − hΔF gives h = Cov(ΔS, ΔF)/Var(ΔF) = correlation × spot-change volatility divided by futures-change volatility.

This hedge ratio is estimated for the chosen horizon and objective. It minimizes variance within the model rather than guaranteeing a particular price or maximizing profit. A firm caring about extreme cash shortfalls or asymmetric risk may choose a different size.

Translate a ratio into contracts

If Q units of exposure each change in price by ΔS and a futures contract covers q units changing by ΔF, the desired number is hQ/q. Convert different units and currencies first. Rounding contracts leaves a residual. Correlations can break when transport bottlenecks or regional supply disruptions alter the economic link.

A rolling hedge may need to close the near contract and open a later one before the exposure ends. The price difference between contract maturities changes with storage conditions and market demand. A stable historical spot relationship may not capture the resulting rolling cost.

Method

Validate a proposed hedge

  1. Exposure

    State whether the firm buys or sells its product, how many units it truly expects and what dated local price matters.

  2. Fit

    Estimate co-movement and relative change sizes from aligned observations. Calculate the minimum-variance ratio and convert to contract count.

  3. Stress

    Compare local supply shocks, date shifts and forecast-quantity errors. Report planned liquidity for futures variation as a separate constraint.

Worked case

A local fuel purchaser uses a regional contract

A company will purchase 50,000 litres. Standard deviation of local price changes is €0.08 per litre and of futures changes €0.10, with correlation 0.75. One futures contract covers 5,000 litres. Treat observed estimates as applicable to the exposure horizon.

Working

  1. Minimum-variance ratio is 0.75 × 0.08/0.10 = 0.60. The purchaser goes long six contracts, covering 30,000 litres of futures exposure.
  2. Unhedged cost-change standard deviation is 50,000 × €0.08 = €4,000. At the optimal continuous ratio it falls to €4,000 × √(1 − 0.75²) ≈ €2,646 under the model.
  3. If local fuel rises €0.12 and futures rise €0.05, physical cost increases €6,000 and futures gain €1,500. Remaining adverse change is €4,500 in this realized scenario.

Interpretation

A realized adverse figure above modeled standard deviation is possible. Standard deviation is a spread measure, and an estimated historical relationship can change materially.

A common mistake

Ignoring contract size and buying 60% of one contract because the hedge ratio is 0.60.

How to correct it

Multiply the ratio by the physical amount and divide by the contract’s own units. State whether the final position is long or short.

Quick check
If the initial futures price is €200 and final basis is +€3, what is the effective sale price before fees?

The short hedge leaves initial futures price plus final basis. Receiving a local price €3 above the closing futures price lifts the effective price to €203.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A producer sells 80,000 units. Local price-change volatility is 0.12, futures-change volatility 0.08, and correlation 0.80. Each contract covers 10,000 matched units.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Compute the unrounded minimum-variance hedge and the nearest whole contract count.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Why can hedging more futures units than physical units be reasonable?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

h = 0.80 × 0.12/0.08 = 1.20. Desired short is 1.20 × 80,000/10,000 = 9.6 contracts, rounded to 10 under nearest-integer sizing.

Part B 2 marks

Here the futures price changes less per unit than local price. Matching the component of local movements explained by futures can require a ratio above one. Imperfect correlation, rounding and quantity risk still leave exposure.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

A producer plans to sell its crop after the nearest liquid futures contract expires. Which additional price spread will it have to accept when it rolls the hedge?

Compare with a spoken answer

I would separate the physical sale from the two short futures gains. With the illustrated prices the producer receives €205 per unit, composed of the starting €200, a €7 roll benefit and a €2 adverse final basis. Before the roll the calendar spread is uncertain. I would compare a less-liquid direct maturity hedge with rolling, including market depth, margin and forecast quantity.

Interviewer’s follow-up

At roll, the later future is €190 while the near price remains €195. All other final prices are unchanged. What is the effective price?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The new roll spread is −€5, a €12 deterioration relative to the original +€7. Effective sale becomes €200 − €2 − €5 = €193. Directly, the first hedge earns €5, the second earns zero and the crop sells for €188.
