Black–Scholes and Greeks

Interpret volatility, delta, gamma and time decay. Continuous hedging and constant volatility are strong assumptions for a real desk.

The starting point

The Black–Scholes model prices a European option using a continuously rebalanced share-and-cash hedge under assumptions including continuous price paths, specified volatility and frictionless trading. The Greeks describe how an option’s value responds locally to changes in model inputs.

Delta measures value change per unit of share-price change. Gamma measures how fast delta changes as the share moves. Vega measures sensitivity to a change in volatility, with its quotation units stated. Theta describes change as time passes. A hedge set using today’s delta will generally need revision tomorrow. Jumps and trading costs complicate it further.

Theory and method

Black–Scholes–Merton European call formula

Delta and risk-neutral exercise probability · Lognormal diffusion

For the same European call, a trader quotes delta near 0.637 and a risk-neutral chance of exercise near 0.560. Which number is wrong?

A call buyer receives shares only in states where exercise is attractive and finances the strike only in those same states. The present value can be decomposed into those two pieces. In the Black–Scholes–Merton model the first term uses a share-weighted probability and the cash term uses the money-market pricing probability. These different weights explain why the delta for hedging a non-dividend-paying share can exceed the usual risk-neutral chance that the option finishes in the money.

How the result is built

C = SΦ(d₁) − K exp(−rT)Φ(d₂), d₁ = [ln(S/K) + (r + σ²/2)T]/(σ√T), d₂ = d₁ − σ√T

C is the euro call value, S current euro share price, K euro strike, r continuously compounded annual safe rate, T years to expiry, σ annual volatility, Φ the standard normal cumulative probability, exp the exponential and ln the natural logarithm.

  1. Take S = K = €100, r = 5%, T = 1 and σ = 20%. The logarithm of spot over strike is zero. Substitution gives d₁ = 0.35 and d₂ = 0.15.
  2. Normal cumulative values are approximately Φ(0.35) = 0.63683 and Φ(0.15) = 0.55962. The prepaid share component is €63.683. The discounted expected strike payment is approximately €53.232.
  3. Subtract to value the option at about €10.45. The model delta is Φ(d₁), while the pricing probability of exercise is Φ(d₂) for this non-dividend-paying share.

When the reasoning holds

  • The formula assumes continuous trading, ideal financing, constant volatility and rate, lognormal share dynamics and European exercise. Dividends or jumps change the pricing relationship.
  • The exercise probability is risk neutral. Inferring the physical chance that the position pays needs a separate return model or forecast.

Terms in this lesson
Greeks
Measures of how an option’s value changes with an input. Delta measures its sensitivity to the underlying price.
Volatility
How widely returns vary over a stated time interval.
Option
A contract giving the buyer a right to buy or sell on stated terms. The buyer pays a premium for the right.
Hedge
A position or contract intended to reduce an identified exposure, such as the cost of paying in a foreign currency.
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
How to read the notation
Powers · (1 + r)²
The small 2 means multiply the factor by itself. Two years of 5% compound growth turn €100 into €100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = €110.25. Plain text also writes the power as ^2.
Time and random increments · dt, dW
Over a short interval Δt, a standard Brownian increment has mean zero and standard deviation √Δt. If a year is one time unit, a quarter-year increment has standard deviation 0.5. A drift term scales with elapsed time itself.
Mean, variance and standard deviation · μ, σ², σ
The mean is the average. Variance measures squared distances from that average. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. If equally likely returns are 2% and 6%, the mean is 4% and the population standard deviation is 2 percentage points.

All notation examples

First example

Suppose one call on one share has delta 0.60 and gamma 0.04 per euro. For a €2 share rise with time and volatility held fixed, the approximate gain is 0.60 × €2 + ½ × 0.04 × 2² = €1.28.

Black–Scholes as a replication model

For a non-dividend-paying share with continuous price movements, geometric Brownian motion is a model in which short proportional changes have a random component scaled by volatility. A European option’s value depends on share price, time remaining, volatility, strike and the financing rate. Dynamic hedging removes the leading share shock locally under the model.

With no dividends, the backward equation is Vt + ½σ²S²Vss + rSVs − rV = 0. Vt is change with calendar time, Vs is delta and Vss is gamma. At expiry a call equals max(S − K, 0). That terminal condition and model assumptions determine the solution rather than a forecast of the stock’s physical expected growth.

Greek units determine the hedge

Delta is value change for a small share-price change. Positive gamma means delta increases with spot. Theta is the change associated with passing calendar time under a specified daily or annual convention. Vega measures the sensitivity to volatility. Check whether it is quoted per full unit or per one percentage-point change.

A trader hedging delta with shares removes the first-order stock-price term for small changes. The hedge retains curvature, volatility, time and model exposure. A call writer can rebalance after a move but cannot transact continuously through a trading halt or sudden price jump.

Volatility estimated and implied

Historical volatility summarizes past observed variation. Implied volatility is the input that makes a chosen pricing model reproduce an observed option price. Different strikes or dates can imply different volatilities, signalling limitations of a single constant-volatility assumption or different priced risks.

When approximating a change over several variables, a first-order vega term and second-order spot term can still miss interactions such as spot–volatility effects. Large moves call for full repricing with justified inputs rather than mechanically multiplying old Greeks.

Method

Attribute an option move

  1. Normalize

    Clarify per-share or per-contract Greeks, day count, volatility units and position direction before applying any shock.

  2. Approximate

    Add delta × spot move, half gamma × squared spot move, vega × volatility change and theta × time passed under the quoted conventions.

  3. Hedge

    Remove the selected exposure with appropriate instruments and identify residual components. Reprice for material shocks or changed implied volatility.

Worked case

A desk explains its overnight move

One long call on one share has delta 0.40, gamma 0.05 per euro, vega €0.12 per volatility percentage point and theta −€0.04 per day. Overnight spot increases €3 and implied volatility rises two percentage points. Ignore rate changes and cross-terms.

Working

  1. Spot contributes €1.20 from delta and €0.225 from gamma. Volatility adds €0.24 and one day’s time subtracts €0.04, giving estimated total €1.625.
  2. A long-call holder shorting 0.40 share at the start loses €1.20 on that hedge as spot rises. Estimated combined gain is €0.425. Financing and dividends are ignored.
  3. New delta from the pure spot approximation is about 0.40 + 0.05 × €3 = 0.55. An updated neutral hedge would short another 0.15 share per call if other delta sensitivities are ignored.

Interpretation

Observed market profit could differ through a volatility surface change, skew, transaction costs or changing gamma. Explain residuals before treating them as trading skill.

A common mistake

Multiplying a vega already quoted per volatility percentage point by 0.02 for a two-point change.

How to correct it

Read the unit. A €0.12-per-point vega uses 2, yielding €0.24. Vega quoted per 1.00 change would use 0.02.

Quick check
A desk has sold 100 of these one-share calls. What initial share holding offsets their delta?

The short calls have total delta −100 × 0.60 = −60 shares. A long holding of 60 shares offsets this first-order exposure initially.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A desk is short ten contracts on 100 shares each. Per-share long-call delta is 0.55 and gamma 0.03. It immediately hedges delta. Spot then falls €2 with volatility and time fixed.

  1. Part A · 2 marks

    Find the starting share hedge.

  2. Part B · 3 marks

    Estimate residual profit from gamma after the move and explain the sign.

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 2 marks

The short calls have total delta −10 × 100 × 0.55 = −550. Buy 550 shares initially to offset this first-order stock exposure.

Part B 3 marks

Total gamma is −30. Delta is initially neutral, leaving ½ × (−30) × (−2)² = −€60 expected curvature P&L locally. Negative gamma penalizes a move in either direction under this approximation.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

For the same European call, a trader quotes delta near 0.637 and a risk-neutral chance of exercise near 0.560. Which number is wrong?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

Both figures are correct under the stated model. Delta, about 0.637, measures how many shares locally offset a short one-share call. The risk-neutral chance of exercise is about 0.560. They differ because the option’s asset and strike components have different state weightings. I would hedge with delta and forecast exercise only after specifying whether the desk needs a pricing probability or an actual outcome frequency.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If you sell 500 of these one-share calls and hedge using the exercise probability instead, what is the initial residual share exposure?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The short calls carry about −318.415 shares of delta. Buying 500 × 0.55962 = 279.81 shares leaves approximately −38.61 shares unhedged. A small €1 rise would initially cost about €38.61 before gamma, time and volatility changes.
