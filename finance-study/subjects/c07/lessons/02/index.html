Forwards and carry

Replicate later delivery by buying and financing the underlying today. Storage, dividends or other benefits of holding the asset affect fair delivery prices.

The starting point

A forward fixes today the price for a purchase or sale on a later date. To reason about the fair delivery price, compare two ways to obtain the asset later. One is the contract. The other is to borrow, buy the asset now and carry it until delivery.

At a fair initial price both routes have equal cost under the assumptions. Financing and storage raise carrying cost. Dividends or other income received by owners can reduce it. Physical commodities can offer a benefit simply from having stock on hand. Trading limits affect how closely the textbook relationship can be enforced.

Theory and method

Cost-of-carry model with convenience yield

Cash-and-carry arbitrage · Backwardation

Physical copper costs €80 today and six-month delivery is only €78. Does that automatically produce a cash-and-carry arbitrage?

A manufacturer may value copper in its own warehouse because it can keep a production line running during shortages. A futures or forward contract delivers later and cannot supply that service today. Financing inventory and paying storage increase the carrying cost, while a convenience yield represents the benefit of retaining the physical commodity. A downward-sloping forward quote can be consistent with valuable inventory services or limits on borrowing metal. The observable price difference alone does not reveal a free trade.

How the result is built

F = S exp[(r + u − y)T]

F is euro forward price per unit, S spot price per unit, r continuously compounded annual financing rate, u annual proportional storage cost, y implied annual convenience yield under the chosen model, T years to delivery, and exp the exponential function.

  1. Grow today’s purchase and proportional carrying costs at r + u, then deduct the modeled benefit from holding the physical asset over the same interval.
  2. With S €80, T 0.5, r 4% and u 2%, no convenience benefit implies F about €82.44. A quote at €78 implies a positive holding benefit or another market friction.
  3. Rearrange to y = r + u − ln(F/S)/T. With the €78 quote the implied y is about 11.06% annually under these conventions.

When the reasoning holds

  • The calculation assumes one comparable grade and location, a constant proportional cost and benefit, and the given compounding basis. Physical delivery differences can create another spread.
  • A reverse trade requires access to borrow the commodity and replace the owner’s operating benefits. A non-tradable convenience benefit can prevent a strict equality-based arbitrage.
Terms in this lesson
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
Dividend
A payment a company makes to shareholders from available resources.
Asset
Something owned or controlled that can provide future value, such as cash, an invoice owed to you or equipment.
Forward
A contract to exchange an agreed amount at a set price on a future date.
Cash flow
Money actually entering or leaving an account over a period.
Discount factor
The price today of one unit of currency payable at a particular future date under specified risk assumptions.
First example

A share costs €80, pays no dividend and can be financed for one year at 5%. Paying the borrowing back costs €84. With frictionless trading and identical borrowing and lending terms, the one-year forward price is €84.

Replicate delivery with a financed purchase

A buyer wanting an asset on a future date can buy now and carry it or sign a forward for delivery later. If the two routes deliver identical cash flows and the asset can be freely bought, financed and sold, an executable price discrepancy can be traded against. Carry includes financing and cash costs, less benefits received by the holder.

For a share paying known cash dividends before maturity, subtract the present value of dividends from spot before financing the remaining exposure. With discount factor d(T), fair delivery price is [spot − PV(dividends)]/d(T). Guaranteed storage charges add to costs under equivalent timing conventions.

An existing forward can have value

At inception a newly struck fair forward has zero value in the simple model. The delivery price printed on it remains fixed. Later market conditions can make that commitment advantageous. A long contract at old delivery price K has value d(t,T) × [current fair forward price − K] under matched settlement and credit assumptions.

A favorable forward price difference is a terminal cash difference, so discount it to get value today. Distinguish the new forward quote, the old delivery price and the present market value required to transfer an existing contract.

Find the correct arbitrage direction

If market delivery is too dear relative to carrying the asset, borrow to buy the asset, retain relevant income and sell forward. At expiry deliver the asset and repay financing. If delivery is too cheap, reversing the strategy generally requires borrowing the physical asset and meeting its income obligations.

Commodity convenience yield represents an operating benefit of physical inventory, such as keeping a factory running. If that benefit cannot be sold separately or inventory cannot be borrowed, a simple textbook equality may be an upper bound or an imperfect guide.

Method

Check a forward quote

  1. Match

    Verify identical grade, currency, timing, settlement method and ownership of intervening cash or operating benefits.

  2. Carry

    Value asset income and carrying costs on their own dates and finance the net price to the forward date.

  3. Trade

    List the time-zero and maturity cash flows for the candidate direction, including short borrow and transaction costs before claiming arbitrage.

Worked case

A known dividend enters once

A share costs €100 and pays a certain €2 dividend in six months. Discount factors to six and twelve months are 0.98 and 0.95. Consider a one-year forward with full ability to borrow, lend and trade this share at those factors.

Working

  1. The present value of the dividend is €1.96. Net prepaid cost of one share for delivery at year one is €98.04. Fair delivery price is €98.04/0.95 = €103.20.
  2. A forward quote of €106 allows the asset to be bought and carried while selling future delivery dear. The locked year-end difference is €2.80 per share, before costs.
  3. The present value of a long existing agreement at €101 is 0.95 × (€103.20 − €101) = €2.09. This value is paid for an existing favorable right, not added to the new fair forward delivery price.

Interpretation

The dividend was subtracted at present value because owning the asset in the carry trade collects it, while simple receipt of the asset in one year does not.

A common mistake

Subtracting the dividend at maturity value and also reducing the current share price by its present value.

How to correct it

Track the holder of the dividend once and carry it to the chosen comparison date with the matching discount factors.

Quick check
If the share also pays a certain €2 dividend exactly at delivery to the current holder, what forward price follows?

The owner’s dividend supplies €2 of the €84 financing repayment. A delivery price of €82 makes the two ways of obtaining the share economically equivalent.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

Spot is €60, there is no income, and the nine-month discount factor is 0.96. A firm already agreed to buy at €64 in nine months.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find today’s fair delivery price and the value of the old long position.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Why might reverse cash-and-carry fail for a cheap forward on specialized inventory?

Saved on this device as you type.
Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

New fair price is €60/0.96 = €62.50. Existing long value is 0.96 × (62.50 − 64) = −€1.44 per unit.

Part B 2 marks

The asset could be unavailable for borrowing or provide production benefits lost when shorting. The necessary short position, financing or replacement inventory may be unattainable at the assumed terms.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Physical copper costs €80 today and six-month delivery is only €78. Does that automatically produce a cash-and-carry arbitrage?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would first check grade, delivery point, financing, storage and whether the metal can actually be shorted. Given the stated model, the €78 quote implies about 11.06% convenience yield, so physical availability may be highly valuable. A trader shorting warehouse stock must replace it and may sacrifice production or incur borrow fees. I would show executable cash flows before calling the relationship an arbitrage.

Interviewer’s follow-up

Unexpectedly, storage rises from 2% to 4% while spot, financing and the quote stay fixed. How does your inference change?

Compare with the follow-up answer

The implied convenience yield rises by two percentage points to about 13.06% under the same proportional model. The fixed quote by itself cannot distinguish a stronger physical service from a newly omitted storage or delivery friction, so I would verify the cost measurement.
