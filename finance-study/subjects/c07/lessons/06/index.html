Binomial replication

Match the option payoff in an up and a down state using shares and cash. The cost of the matching portfolio provides the model price.

The starting point

A binomial model gives the share two possible prices at the next date. A derivative has a specified payoff in each outcome. Choose shares and borrowing or lending so that the resulting portfolio pays exactly the same as the derivative in both outcomes. This is replication.

Find the share holding, or delta, by dividing the difference in derivative payoffs by the difference in final share prices. Use cash to match the remaining payoff. If trading is frictionless and both states can be matched, the cost of this position gives the derivative price in the model.

Theory and method

Cox–Ross–Rubinstein binomial model

Recombining tree · Delta replication

Why does a Cox–Ross–Rubinstein tree scale the log price step with the square root of elapsed time, and how is the hedge found?

A desk observing 20% annual volatility needs a model for a three-month pricing step. Independent diffusion-like increments accumulate variance in proportion to time, so the typical log move scales with the square root of time. Symmetric up and down log moves allow an up followed by a down to return to the initial price. The calibrated tree then uses traded funding and share outcomes to price a claim. Replication determines the pricing weight even when forecasters disagree about which branch is more frequent.

How the result is built

u = exp(σ√Δt), d = 1/u, R = exp(rΔt), q = (R − d)/(u − d), Δ = (Vᵤ − Vd)/[S(u − d)]

σ is annual volatility, Δt step length in years, r continuously compounded annual safe rate, S current share price, u and d price multipliers, R safe gross growth, q pricing up weight, Vᵤ and Vd next option values, and Δ shares held per option.

  1. Use S = €100, σ = 0.20, Δt = 0.25 and r = 4%. Log move size is 0.10, giving u ≈ 1.10517, d ≈ 0.90484 and R ≈ 1.01005.
  2. An expiry call struck at €100 pays €10.5171 up and zero down. Solve the matched-share payoff equation for delta ≈ 0.52498 shares.
  3. The pricing up weight is about 0.52519, so call value is q × €10.5171/R ≈ €5.4685. A short position in the call can buy approximately 0.525 shares as its local hedge.

When the reasoning holds

  • The chosen time step must satisfy d < R < u for positive pricing weights. A coarse grid or inconsistent parameters may violate the inequality.
  • The terminal payoff and tree assume a non-dividend-paying share and the modeled two price changes. Discrete dividends, jumps or costly rebalancing need adjustments.

Terms in this lesson
Option
A contract giving the buyer a right to buy or sell on stated terms. The buyer pays a premium for the right.
Derivative
A contract whose value depends on something else, such as a currency, an interest rate or a share price.
Interest rate
The price of borrowing money or the return for lending it, quoted as a percentage over a stated period.
Present value (PV)
An amount at a future date expressed in today’s money using a rate suited to its timing and risk.
Replicating portfolio
A combination of traded claims that delivers the same future cash flows as a contract in every state covered by the model.
Share
A unit of ownership in a company. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or lose money if its price changes.
How to read the notation
A change · Δ
ΔS commonly means the new asset price less the old price. A move from €50 to €53 gives ΔS = €3. In options, delta can also name a sensitivity. A delta of 0.6 means a €1 rise in the underlying predicts about a €0.60 rise in the option, for a small move.

All notation examples

First example

A €100 share will be €120 or €80. A call struck at €100 pays €20 or zero. Its delta is €20/€40 = 0.5. At a zero interest rate, half a share and €40 of borrowing return €20 if up and zero if down. The call costs €50 − €40 = €10.

Solve two payoff equations

At one node, let the underlying become Su or Sd next period and the derivative become Vu or Vd. Hold Δ shares and an amount B in cash today. At the next date cash grows by R. Match both states with ΔSu + BR = Vu and ΔSd + BR = Vd. Subtraction gives Δ = (Vu − Vd)/(Su − Sd).

Then B = (Vd − ΔSd)/R. A negative B represents borrowing. The option’s present value is ΔS + B because the replicating portfolio behaves identically in both states. This argument needs the specified borrowing and trading opportunities and an exhaustive two-state model.

Work backward through a tree

At final nodes, calculate the contractual payoff directly. At the preceding nodes use local up and down option values to choose the local shares and cash. Once you have both intermediate values, repeat from the original node. Delta is a local solution that generally changes when the share price changes.

A self-financing hedge pays for rebalancing using its existing holdings. Selling some shares can finance an increase in cash. Borrowing can finance additional shares. When the model is internally consistent, no arbitrary top-up should be needed to match a European terminal payoff.

Understand incompleteness

Two independently paying assets can span two future states. If there are three distinct states, a share and a bond provide only two independent quantities to choose. Some derivative payoffs can still happen to be spanned, but a general claim may require another traded asset or produce a range rather than a unique replication price.

The model’s exact hedge is conditional on the tree. In a real market an unmodeled jump, a halt or costly rebalancing can produce hedging error. Proving equality at every specified leaf demonstrates model consistency, not immunity to every real event.

Method

Build a replicating tree

  1. Terminal

    List actual share values and corresponding contract payoffs at the leaves. Include expiry terms and exercise rules.

  2. Backward

    At each preceding node solve the two payoff equations for share units and current cash. Record the resulting option value.

  3. Verify

    Run one complete path forward and check that the hedge rebalances from its existing value into a portfolio delivering the required terminal cash.

Worked case

A call that changes its hedge after one step

A €100 share follows a two-period tree. Each step multiplies its price by either 1.25 or 0.8. The safe rate is zero. A European call struck at €100 pays only after the second step.

Working

  1. Final share prices are €156.25, €100 and €64. Corresponding call payoffs are €56.25, zero and zero. At the intermediate down node both future payoffs are zero, so its call value is zero.
  2. At the €125 up node, delta = 56.25/(156.25 − 100) = 1. Borrow €100 to make the down-state payoff zero. The up-node call value is €25.
  3. At inception delta = (25 − 0)/(125 − 80) = 5/9. Borrow €44.44, yielding an initial price near €11.11. If the first move is up, this original portfolio is worth €25 and can finance the new one-share hedge.

Interpretation

A static initial delta held for both periods would generally miss the terminal payoff. The exercise demonstrates precisely why a trader rebalances after learning which node occurred.

At the board

Replicate one option with a share and a loan

A share at 100 finishes at 120 or 80 after one year. A call with exercise price 100 pays 20 or zero. The annual risk-free rate is 5%. Assume frictionless trading, borrowing and lending at the stated rate, and no dividends.

The hedge ratio is (20 − 0) / (120 − 80) = 0.5 share per option. Borrow just enough for the down-state payoff to be zero. The net cost 50 − 38.0952 is the no-arbitrage call price in this two-state model. If it were possible to buy the same two payoffs more cheaply elsewhere, the expensive position could be sold against the cheaper one.

A common mistake

Calculating initial delta from the final extreme prices while ignoring the intermediate branch.

How to correct it

Work backward one period at a time. At the first node use option values at the next date, already incorporating future opportunities to rebalance.

Quick check
In this same tree a put struck at €100 pays zero if up and €20 if down. What share holding replicates its sensitivity?

Use the same state order in both differences. (0 − €20)/(€120 − €80) = −0.5. A short share holding rises in value as the stock falls.

Written practice · 5 marks

Independent exam problem

A share costs €50 and becomes €60 or €45 in a year. A derivative pays €9 or zero respectively. Safe growth is 1.05.

  1. Part A · 3 marks

    Find replicating shares, cash and current value.

  2. Part B · 2 marks

    Verify the up payoff and identify one assumption whose failure undermines replication.

Reveal the worked marking guide

Part A 3 marks

Delta = 9/(60 − 45) = 0.6 share. Down-state share value €27 is offset by borrowing €27/1.05 ≈ €25.714 today. Price is €30 − €25.714 ≈ €4.286.

Part B 2 marks

0.6 × €60 − €27 = €9. A third unmodeled price state, inability to borrow, or different funding terms could prevent exact matching of the given contingent claim.

Interview practice

Explain it aloud

Why does a Cox–Ross–Rubinstein tree scale the log price step with the square root of elapsed time, and how is the hedge found?

Saved on this device as you type.
Compare with a spoken answer

I would begin with variance per unit time. A three-month diffusion step at 20% annual volatility has about 10% log standard deviation. CRR uses equal and opposite log moves, then derives pricing weights from the safe growth factor. The call’s hedge is a ratio of payoff differences, around 0.525 shares here. The 0.525 pricing weight is numerically close in this case but comes from a different equation.

Interviewer’s follow-up

If you halve the step length while keeping annual volatility fixed, should the log move also halve?

Compare with the follow-up answer

It should shrink by the square root of one half, around 0.7071. Starting from 0.10 the new step is roughly 0.07071. Using 0.05 would make aggregate variance collapse as the number of time steps grows, misrepresenting the original annual volatility.
